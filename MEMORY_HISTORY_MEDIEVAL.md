# 🟣 THEME 6: BHAKTI-SUFI TRADITIONS (c. 8th–18th century) (10M)

**The chapter in six sentences:** The "integration of cults" (**DAR: Disseminate via simple-Sanskrit Puranas; Accept and Rework local beliefs**) absorbed local gods — **Puri's Jagannatha ("lord of the world," a form of Vishnu, with Subhadra and Balabhadra, identified by the 12th century)** and village goddesses re-imagined as **wives of male deities (Lakshmi, Parvati)** — while **Tantric practice, open to women and men and ignoring caste, influenced Shaivism and Buddhism**. In Tamil Nadu the **Alvars (devotees of Vishnu) and Nayanars (devotees of Shiva), c. sixth century onwards**, sang Tamil hymns from artisan, cultivator and outcaste backgrounds, produced the **Nalayira Divyaprabandham ("Tamil Veda")** and **Tevaram (Appar, Sambandar, Sundarar — classified in the 10th century by MUSIC)**, and opposed Buddhism and Jainism; the **Cholas (9th–13th c.)** then built the great Shiva temples at **Chidambaram, Thanjavur and Gangaikondacholapuram**. In 12th-century Karnataka **Basavanna's Virashaivas (Lingayats)** wrote **vachanas in Kannada**, rejected **rebirth, caste, the Vedas and pollution**, **buried their dead**, and encouraged **post-puberty marriage and widow remarriage**. Sufism organised itself into **khanqahs (11th c.) and silsilas ("chains" to the Prophet, 12th c.)** — and the **Chishtis (Muinuddin Sijzi d. 1235 Ajmer; Bakhtiyar Kaki d. 1235 Delhi; Fariduddin Ganj-i Shakar d. 1265 Ajodhan; Nizamuddin Auliya d. 1325 Delhi; Nasiruddin Chiragh-i Dehli d. 1356 Delhi)** won the subcontinent through the **langar run on futuh (unasked charity), ziyarat, sama' and qawwali, Hindavi conversation, and moral distance from power** — which Sultans rewarded with **auqaf and inam** because they needed **legitimation** beyond the ulama. The saint-poets — **Kabir (weaver; Alakh and Niranjana; ultabansi; verses in the Bijak of the Kabirpanth, the Kabir Granthavali of the Dadupanth, and the Adi Granth)**, **Guru Nanak (1469–1539, Nankana Sahib; nirguna "Rab"; shabad hymns in Punjabi; sangat; successor Angad; Adi Granth compiled by Guru Arjan; Guru Granth Sahib and the Khalsa's five symbols under Guru Gobind Singh)** and **Mirabai (Rajput princess of Merta who took Raidas, a leather-worker, as guru)** — made devotion personal and vernacular.

# 🟡 THEME 7: AN IMPERIAL CAPITAL — VIJAYANAGARA (c. 14th–16th century) (6M)

**The chapter in five sentences:** **Colin Mackenzie (born 1754, later first Surveyor General of India) made the first map of the Hampi ruins in 1800** from local informants and oral traditions; photography followed (**Alexander Greenlaw, 1856**), then epigraphists and archaeologists. The empire — founded at **Hampi in 1336 by Harihara and Bukka (Sangama dynasty)**, then **Saluva, Tuluva (Krishnadeva Raya, 1509–1529, who wrote the Amuktamalyada), and Aravidu (post-1565, at Penukonda)** — fell after the **1565 battle of Rakshasa-Tangadi (Talikota)**, and the city was **sacked and abandoned**. Its economy ran on **cavalry**: **horses imported from Arabia and Central Asia** (traders called **kudirai chettis**, "horse merchants"), then contested by the **Portuguese (from 1498)**; the three contemporary kings took the titles **narapati, ashvapati and gajapati** (lords of men, horses and elephants). The **amara-nayaka system** gave military chiefs territories (**amaras**): they **collected taxes, kept part for themselves, maintained horses and troops, paid fixed tribute, supplied soldiers on demand — and were transferred periodically** (a near-cousin of the Delhi Sultanate's iqta; after 1565 the nayakas became kings of **Madurai, Tanjavur and Ikkeri**). The capital itself wore its power in architecture — **seven lines of forts enclosing farms and canals, the water system (Hiriya canal from a dam across the Tungabhadra, Kamalapuram tank, aqueducts, the stepped stone tank), the royal centre (Mahanavami dibba, Lotus Mahal, Hazara Rama) and the sacred centre (Virupaksha and Vitthala temples)**.
