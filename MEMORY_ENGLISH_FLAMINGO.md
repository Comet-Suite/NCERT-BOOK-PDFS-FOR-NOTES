# PART 1 — THE FIVE POEMS

## 1. My Mother at Sixty-six — KAMALA DAS
**One-line identity:** a daughter driving from her **parent's home to Cochin airport** on a Friday morning realises her **ageing mother may be dying**.
**Scene-recall:** the mother dozes **"open mouthed"** beside her, face **"ashen like that of a corpse"**; the poet looks away to **"sprinting"** trees and **"merry children spilling out of their homes"** (life moving on); at security check she looks back — mother compared to **"a late winter's moon"** (pale, dim, cold); the old **"familiar ache... childhood's fear"** returns; she can only say **"See you soon, Amma,"** and smiles **"and smile and smile and smile"** — a forced smile hiding fear.
**Quotes to write:** "ashen like that of a corpse" • "merry children spilling out of their homes" • "late winter's moon" • "smile and smile and smile."
**Themes:** fear of losing a parent, ageing vs youth, the gap between felt pain and spoken words.
**🧠 Hook:** "**corpse-face → trees-kids → moon-face → smile-smile-smile**" — the ugly truth, the escape, the tender truth, the mask.
**🎯 Traps:** it is **one single sentence** poem (one breathless thought); the two similes are **corpse (fear)** and **winter's moon (love/tenderness)**; "late winter's moon" = **pale + dim + cold + late in life**, all at once.

## 2. Keeping Quiet — PABLO NERUDA
**One-line identity:** the poet asks all humanity to **stop for a count of twelve** — a moment of **stillness and introspection**, not death.
**Scene-recall:** **count to twelve, keep still** — "it would be an exotic moment without rush, without engines"; he does **not** want confusion with **total inactivity** — "Life is what it is about"; images: **fishermen not harming whales**, the **man gathering salt would look at his hurt hands**; wars end — "**Green wars, wars with gas, wars with fire, victory with no survivors**"; men walk about **"in the shade, doing nothing"** and **put on clean clothes**; **"Earth can teach us as when everything seems dead and later proves to be alive"**; the poet **counts to twelve and walks away** — you must keep the quiet.
**Themes:** introspection, peace, anti-war, harmony with nature, the difference between stillness and inactivity.
**🧠 Hook:** "**count-12 → no engines → whale + hurt hands → no wars → earth's lesson → he walks away.**"
**🎯 Traps:** twelve is just a **count** (symbol of clock/completeness) — not a religious number; the poem is **NOT about death** — quote "no truck with death"; the poet **leaves at the end** because the silence must be kept by us.

## 3. A Thing of Beauty — JOHN KEATS (excerpt from Endymion)
**One-line identity:** beauty is an **endless, immortal source of joy** that lifts the **pall** (shroud) of gloom from human spirits.
**Scene-recall:** "**A thing of beauty is a joy for ever: its loveliness increases; it will never pass into nothingness**"; it gives a **"bower quiet for us, and a sleep full of sweet dreams, and health, and quiet breathing"**; every day we **"wreath a flowery band to bind us to the earth"** despite **despondence, dearth of noble natures, gloomy days, unhealthy and o'er-darkened ways**; **"some shape of beauty moves away the pall from our dark spirits"**; the beauty list: **sun, moon, trees old and young giving a shady boon to simple sheep, daffodils, clear rills, the mid-forest brake with musk-roses**; then human beauty: **"the grandeur of the dooms"** (glorious endings) of the **mighty dead**, and lovely tales — **"an endless fountain of immortal drink, pouring unto us from the heaven's brink."**
**Themes:** permanence of beauty, nature as healer, art/legacy of the "mighty dead," romantic optimism.
**🧠 Hook:** "**joy-forever → bower → band-to-earth → pall-off → sun-moon-trees-daffodils-rills-roses → mighty dead → endless fountain.**"
**🎯 Traps:** book glosses: **rills = small streams, brake = mass of ferns**; "dooms" = **fates/ends of the mighty dead** (glorious, not gloomy); Endymion = **Greek legend**, Cynthia the **Moon Goddess**.

## 4. A Roadside Stand — ROBERT FROST
**One-line identity:** poor rural folk put up a **roadside shed to sell berries and squash for city money** — and are ignored, cheated by "charity," and pitied wrongly.
**Scene-recall:** the **little old house with a little new shed** "out at the road's edge" — old poverty wearing new hope; **"the polished traffic passed with a mind ahead"** — and if it stops, only to **complain the sign spoils the landscape** (N and S turned wrong); they are **not begging for a dole of bread** but for **"some of the money, the cash, whose flow supports the flower of cities"** — they feel **"out of sorts"** at being **"seen but refused"**; the city's fake sympathy: the **"party in power"** plans to move the "pitiful kin" into villages "next to the theatre and the store" — **"greedy good-doers" and "beneficent beasts of prey"** who **"soothe them out of their wits"** with "benefits"; the sadness **lurks in the shuttered stand, waiting for the sound of stopping cars**; the poet's "mad" thought — to end their pain at one stroke — and his return to sanity: **"I wonder how I should like you to come to me and offer to put me gently out of my pain."**
**Themes:** rural-urban divide, hollow political promises, false charity vs real help, the poet's own helplessness.
**🧠 Hook:** "**old-house-new-shed → berries-squash → N-S wrong → GG & BB (greedy good-doers, beneficent beasts of prey) → lurk-at-shutter → 'how would YOU like it?'**"
**🎯 Traps:** the two oxymorons: **greedy good-doers, beneficent beasts of prey**; the folk want **city money, not charity**; the "relief" at the end is the **poet's own ironic suggestion** — he tests the reader.

## 5. Aunt Jennifer's Tigers — ADRIENNE RICH
**One-line identity:** a terrified wife **embroiders fearless tigers** on a screen — her art outlives her oppression.
**Scene-recall:** Stanza 1 — the tigers **"prance" across a screen**, "**bright topaz denizens of a world of green**," they **do not fear the men beneath the tree** and pace in **"sleek chivalric certainty."** Stanza 2 — Aunt Jennifer's **fingers flutter through her wool**, even the **ivory needle** is hard to pull; **"the massive weight of Uncle's wedding band sits heavily upon Aunt Jennifer's hand."** Stanza 3 — when she is dead, her **terrified hand** will lie **"ringed with ordeals she was mastered by"** — but the tigers in her panel **"will go on prancing, proud and unafraid."**
**Themes:** male oppression in marriage (the wedding band as a weight/ring of ordeals), courage vs fear, **art as immortality and escape**.
**🧠 Hook:** "**tigers-bold → hand-crushed → death-and-triumph**"; the two "rings" = **encircled by ordeals + the wedding ring**; the poem's **last word = "unafraid."**
**🎯 Traps:** **denizen (book gloss) = an inhabitant**; the tigers symbolise the **freedom Aunt never had**; the needle is hard because her hands **tremble with fear**, not age alone; rhyme scheme **aabb couplets** = the tigers' prancing rhythm.

# PART 2 — THE SIX PROSE CHAPTERS

## 1. The Last Lesson — ALPHONSE DAUDET
**Context:** set in the **Franco-Prussian War (1870–71)**; France defeated by **Prussia under Bismarck**; the districts of **Alsace and Lorraine pass into Prussian hands**; order from **Berlin: only German to be taught** — "the new master comes tomorrow."
**Scene-recall:** narrator **Franz** is late, dreads the **participle test**, tempted by birds and Prussian soldiers drilling; blacksmith **Wachter** mocks him ("Don't go so fast, bub"); the school is **"as quiet as Sunday morning"**; **M. Hamel** wears his **green coat, frilled shirt and embroidered black silk cap** (inspection-and-prize-days dress); the back benches hold the village elders — **old Hauser with his three-cornered hat and primer, the former mayor, the former postmaster** — thanking Hamel's **forty years of faithful service** and mourning "the country that was theirs no more"; Hamel blames everyone — **parents (sent children to farms and mills), himself (flower-watering, fishing holidays), and Alsace's habit of "putting off learning till tomorrow"**; the famous line: **"when a people are enslaved, as long as they hold fast to their language it is as if they had the key to their prison"** — French is "the most beautiful language in the world... the clearest, the most logical"; writing lesson: new copies reading **"France, Alsace"** like little flags; Franz's thought as pigeons coo: **"Will they make them sing in German, even the pigeons?"**; the babies chant **"ba, be bi, bo, bu"**; at the church clock striking twelve and the **Angelus**, the **Prussian trumpets** sound; Hamel, choked, writes in the biggest letters: **"Vive La France!"** and dismisses class with his gesture.
**Themes:** linguistic chauvinism's cruelty, love of mother tongue = love of freedom, procrastination's regret.
**🧠 Hook:** "**late-Franz → bulletin-Berlin → green-coat → Hauser-primer → key-to-prison → pigeons → Vive La France.**"
**🎯 Traps:** narrator = **Franz**, teacher = **M. Hamel (40 years)**; war = **Franco-Prussian 1870–71**; the "key to prison" = **mother tongue**.

## 2. Lost Spring — ANEES JUNG
**Two parts:** **"Sometimes I find a rupee in the garbage"** (Saheb) and **"I want to drive a car"** (Mukesh).
**Saheb:** a ragpicker of **Seemapuri** (periphery of Delhi), full name **Saheb-e-Alam ("lord of the universe")** — ironic against his barefoot poverty; his people are **squatters from Bangladesh (1971)**: **10,000 ragpickers** in mud-and-tin structures, **no identity, no permits, but with ration cards** — "food is more important for survival than an identity"; "**garbage to them is gold**" — wrapped in **wonder** for children, **survival** for elders; the **Udipi priest's son** story (the old prayer for shoes, the new boy with socks and shoes = generational change); the **tennis shoes** (a rich boy's discards — "even shoes with a hole is a dream come true"); finally Saheb takes a job at a **milk booth — 800 rupees and all his meals** — but "**the steel canister seems heavier than the plastic bag... Saheb is no longer his own master!**"
**Mukesh:** of **Firozabad**, centre of India's **glass-blowing/bangle industry**; every other family makes bangles; **20,000 children** work illegally in **hot furnaces and dingy cells**, "often losing the brightness of their eyes"; his grandmother: "**it is his karam, his destiny... Can a god-given lineage ever be broken?**"; **Savita** solders glass, not knowing the bangle's **sanctity — suhaag**; the old woman: "**Ek waqt ser bhar khana bhi nahin khaya**"; the **vicious circle** = **sahibs, middlemen, police, bureaucrats, politicians** — the poor cannot organise, and the young men echo the elders' lament; Mukesh **dares to dream**: "**I will be a motor mechanic. I will learn to drive a car**" — a dream "looms like a mirage amidst the dust."
**Themes:** child labour, the loss of childhood (the "spring" lost), cycles of poverty and caste, dreams as resistance.
**🧠 Hook:** "**Saheb = garbage-gold → canister-burden; Mukesh = karam-destiny → furnace-blindness → garage-dream.**"
**🎯 Traps:** author = **Anees Jung**; Saheb = **ragpicker (Seemapuri, Bangladesh 1971)**; Mukesh = **bangle maker (Firozabad)**; Saheb's job = **milk booth, 800 rupees**; Mukesh's dream = **motor mechanic (not driver of cars he can't fix)**.

## 3. Deep Water — WILLIAM DOUGLAS
**Context:** an excerpt from **Of Men and Mountains**; author = **William O. Douglas, US Supreme Court Justice (longest-serving, 36 years)**.
**Scene-recall:** fear began at **three or four**, waves knocking him down at a **California beach**; at **ten or eleven** he decides to learn swimming at the **YMCA pool, Yakima** (2–3 feet shallow, **nine feet deep**, drop gradual; the **Yakima River was "treacherous,"** mother kept "fresh in my mind the details of each drowning"); a **"big bruiser of a boy, about eighteen"** yells **"Hi, Skinny!"** and **tosses him into the deep end**; his plan (feet hit bottom → big jump → lie flat → paddle) fails — **"those nine feet were more like ninety"**; three sinkings, **"stark terror... terror that knows no understanding"**; the drowsy peace and — **"I crossed to oblivion, and the curtain of life fell"**; the bully: **"But I was only fooling."** Aftermath: **haunting fear**, never returned to the pool; fear **ruined fishing and canoeing** (Maine lakes, New Hampshire, Oregon, Columbia); one **October** he hires an instructor: **belt + rope + pulley on an overhead cable**, five days a week, an hour a day; **three months** before tension slack; **exhale under water, inhale at the top — "hundreds of times"; kick "for weeks"; "piece by piece he built a swimmer";** April: **"Now you can swim"**; but Douglas tests himself — **alone in the pool**, then **Lake Wentworth (New Hampshire), diving at Triggs Island, swimming two miles to Stamp Act Island**, and finally **Warm Lake** — he swims across and back and **shouts with joy, Gilbert Peak returning the echo**: "I had conquered my fear of water." Lesson: **"In death there is peace. There is terror only in the fear of death, as Roosevelt knew when he said, 'All we have to fear is fear itself.'"** The will to live grew in intensity.
**Themes:** fear and its conquest, the "misadventure's" psychological scar, determination, professional help + self-testing.
**🧠 Hook:** "**beach-3-4 → YMCA-bully → 9-feet-90 → curtain-of-life → rope-pulley-piece-by-piece → Wentworth-Warm-Lake → fear-fear-itself.**"
**🎯 Traps:** author = **Justice William Douglas** (not a swimmer-author); pool = **YMCA, Yakima**; instructor's gear = **rope, pulley, belt**; final test = **Warm Lake**; quote = **Roosevelt's "All we have to fear is fear itself."**

## 4. The Rattrap — SELMA LAGERLÖF
**Context:** Swedish writer (1858–1940); her universal theme = **essential goodness can be awakened through understanding and love**; the tale is told **"in the manner of a fairy tale,"** set among the **iron mines of Sweden**.
**Scene-recall:** a peddler sells **wire rattraps**, lives by **begging and petty thievery**; his philosophy — **"the whole world... was nothing but a big rattrap,"** offering riches and joys as baits; at a **little grey cottage** the old **crofter** (ex-worker of **Ramsjö Ironworks**, supported now by his **cow**) welcomes him with **porridge, tobacco, cards ("mjolis")** and proudly shows **"thirty kronor"** — **three wrinkled ten-kronor bills in a leather pouch on the window frame**; next morning the peddler **smashes a pane and steals the thirty kronor** — then gets lost in the forest: **"the whole forest closed in upon him like an impenetrable prison"** — he himself is in the rattrap now; the thumping he follows leads to **Ramsjö Ironworks**, where the **ironmaster** mistakes him in the forge-light for **Nils Olof, an old regimental comrade ("Captain von Stahle")**; invited to the manor, the peddler **refuses** (he fears being caught with the stolen money — "voluntarily into the lion's den"); the ironmaster sends his daughter **Edla Willmansson**, who comes with a **valet and fur coat**, pities him, and **promises he may leave as freely as he came — only stay for Christmas Eve**; next morning, cleaned and shaved in the ironmaster's suit, he is exposed; the ironmaster threatens the **sheriff**, and the peddler flings his philosophy at them — **"if the sheriff comes... you, Mr Ironmaster, must remember that a day may come when you yourself may want to get a big piece of pork, and then you will get caught in the trap"**; as he leaves, **Edla intercedes** — he has "not a single place in the whole country where he is welcome... I should like to have him enjoy **a day of peace** — just one in the whole year"; Christmas Eve he sleeps **"as quietly and safely"** as he never had; next day (Christmas) the church bell wakes him — and he has **vanished, leaving behind the stolen thirty kronor and a small rattrap as a Christmas gift, with a letter signed "Captain von Stahle"** — the rat would have been caught in the world's rattrap had the kindness of the master's daughter not **"raised him to a captain... because that, you see, made him a man."**
**Themes:** the world-as-rattrap philosophy, compassion and dignity reform the criminal, essential goodness, Edla as the moral centre.
**🧠 Hook:** "**rattrap-philosophy → crofter-30-kronor → forest-trap → ironmaster-mistake → Edla-invites → Christmas → rattrap-gift-Captain-von-Stahle.**"
**🎯 Traps:** author = **Swedish (Selma Lagerlöf)**; crofter's sum = **30 kronor**; mistaken identity = **Nils Olof / Captain von Stahle**; the gift contains the **stolen notes returned**; the letter is signed **"Captain von Stahle"** — the sign of the man he became.

## 5. The Interview — CHRISTOPHER SILVESTER (+ UMBERTO ECO)
**Part I — the genre:** the interview was invented a little over **130 years ago**; opinions split — **"a source of truth... an art"** vs **"an unwarranted intrusion"** that "diminishes" its victims, like primitive cultures believing a photograph **steals the soul**; **V. S. Naipaul**: people are "**wounded by interviews and lose a part of themselves**"; **Lewis Carroll** had "**a just horror of the interviewer**" and **never consented** (horror of being **lionised**); **Rudyard Kipling** — his wife **Caroline's diary, 14 October 1892**: the day "**wrecked by two reporters from Boston**"; Kipling: interviewing is **"immoral! It is a crime... cowardly and vile. No respectable man would ask it, much less give it"** — though he had himself "assaulted" **Mark Twain**; **H. G. Wells** called it "the interviewing ordeal" (1894) yet was frequently interviewed — and forty years later **interviewed Joseph Stalin**; **Saul Bellow**: interviews are like **"thumbprints on his windpipe"**; the defence — **Denis Brian**: "**our most vivid impressions of our contemporaries are through interviews... the interviewer holds a position of unprecedented power and influence.**"
**Part II — Eco:** interviewer = **Mukund Padmanabhan of The Hindu**; Eco = professor at the **University of Bologna**, scholar of **semiotics (the study of signs), literary interpretation, medieval aesthetics**; **The Name of the Rose (1980) sold 10–15 million copies**; Eco's counter-intuitive lines: **"I am always doing the same thing"** — the **same philosophical interests** pursued through academic work AND novels; his "secret": eliminate the **"empty spaces"** — the **"interstices"** — of life ("while waiting for your elevator... I have already written an article!"); his dissertation at **22** "told the story of my research" — so his essays have a **narrative aspect**; he began fiction **"by accident," at about 50**; **"I consider myself a university professor who writes novels on Sundays"**; over **40 scholarly works** against **five novels**; the novel's success **"is a mystery"** — his American publisher expected **3,000 copies**, it sold **two or three million**; journalists think people like trash, but readers "**don't want easy experiences**"; and Eco himself watches **"Miami Vice" or "Emergency Room"** at 9 pm — **but not all day**.
**Themes:** the interview as a double-edged medium; the scholar-novelist's unity of purpose; don't underestimate readers.
**🧠 Hook:** "**130-years → Carroll-Kipling-Wells-Bellow vs Denis-Brian → Eco: interstices, narrative-at-22, Sundays-professor, mystery-success.**"
**🎯 Traps:** Kipling's diary entry by **wife Caroline, 1892, Boston reporters**; Bellow = **thumbprints on windpipe**; Eco's university = **Bologna**; semiotics = **study of signs**; novels begun **at 50**, "by accident."

## 6. Going Places — A. R. BARTON
**Context:** Barton (lives in **Zurich**) explores **adolescent fantasising and hero worship**.
**Scene-recall:** **Sophie** — a schoolgirl of a poor family — tells sensible **Jansie** ("both earmarked for the **biscuit factory**") she will open a **boutique**, be a manager, "**be like Mary Quant**," an actress or fashion designer; at home — father scooping **shepherd's pie**, little **Derek**, mother with the **delicate bow on her apron against a crooked back** (incongruity = the family's poverty); brother **Geoff, three years out of school, an apprentice mechanic**, silent — **"words had to be prized out of him like stones out of the ground"**; Sophie is **jealous of his silence** — the wide world he moves in; she fantasy-rides behind Geoff in **black leathers and a yellow dress with a cape**, greeted by applause; her big claim: **"I met Danny Casey"** — the **Irish prodigy** of United — in the **arcade outside Royce's window**; she invents details: **green, gentle eyes, "not so tall as you'd think," the accent from television**, an autograph for little Derek but **no paper or pen**; **Geoff doesn't believe her ("the unlikeliest thing I ever heard")**, the father dismisses it — **"one of these days you're going to talk yourself into a load of trouble"** — yet the family itself worships Casey (father's Tom Finney memory); Saturday: United win **two-nil**, Casey scores the second, **"a blend of innocence and Irish genius"**; "I wish he was an Englishman," someone says; **Jansie leaks the story** — Sophie is furious it wasn't a Geoff-only secret; **after dark Sophie waits alone by the canal** under a **solitary elm** — "the perfect place... for a meeting of this kind"; he never comes; she admits **"the pangs of doubt stirring inside me"** and then: **"Now I have become sad... Resignation was no sudden thing"**; passing the pub she is **glad her father's bicycle is outside — he won't be home when she returns**; the story closes inside her daydream replay: his green eyes "**gentle, almost afraid. Like a gazelle's**," the imagined autograph chat — and she waits **"there alone in the arcade... remembering the soft melodious voice."**
**Themes:** fantasy vs reality, hero worship, class limits on dreams, the loneliness that feeds dreams, quiet teenage sadness.
**🧠 Hook:** "**boutique-Mary Quant → Jansie-biscuit → Geoff-silent-stones → Casey-in-arcade → canal-elm-waiting → 'resignation was no sudden thing' → gazelle-eyes replay.**"
**🎯 Traps:** Casey plays for **Ireland** (the "Irish prodigy"); Sophie's meeting **never happened** — the final scene is her imagination replayed; Geoff = **apprentice mechanic**; Jansie = the **realist** ("they don't pay well for shop work"); the title's irony — her mind "goes places" her life cannot.
