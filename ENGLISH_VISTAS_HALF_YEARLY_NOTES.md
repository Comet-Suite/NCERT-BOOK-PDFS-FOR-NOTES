# ✍️ CHAPTER 1: ON THE FACE OF IT — Susan Hill
## (A play in three scenes — the only drama in your Vistas syllabus)

## 🎬 ABOUT THE AUTHOR AND THE TEXT
**Susan Hill** is a well-known English novelist (famous for *I'm the King of the Castle* and the ghost story *The Woman in Black*). "On the Face of It" is a **short play in three scenes**. Two scenes are set in **Mr Lamb's garden** (Scenes One and Three) and one in **Derry's house** (Scene Two). Because this is a play, always remember in answers: the story is told through **dialogue (what characters say), stage directions (what they do), and pauses/silences** — there is no narrator. The title "**On the Face of It**" is an English idiom meaning "as it appears at first glance" — and the whole play attacks judging people by their appearance: Derry's ruined face and Mr Lamb's tin leg both make people assume things that are not true.

## 📖 SCENE ONE — THE GARDEN (full detailed explanation)

**The opening.** The stage directions set a peaceful picture: **birdsong, rustling leaves, long grass**. **Derry, a fourteen-year-old boy**, walks "slowly and tentatively" (hesitantly) through the grass. He has **climbed over the garden wall** — he believed the house was empty and no one would see him. This detail matters: a boy who is terrified of being *looked at* chooses a garden precisely because he thinks it has no people in it. Then an old man's voice calls out, "**Mind the apples!**" and Derry is startled — the first human contact he has not planned.

**The first exchange reveals both characters.** Mr Lamb immediately removes the threat: "**The gate's always open... I don't mind who comes into the garden. All welcome. You're welcome.**" Derry is suspicious and defensive — he insists he hasn't come to steal, and he is angry at being watched ("You were watching me"). Mr Lamb's answers are deliberately calm and ordinary. When Derry says "You don't know who I am," Mr Lamb replies simply: "A boy. Thirteen or so." Derry corrects him — "**Fourteen**" — and the tiny exchange is important: Mr Lamb sees only *a boy*, not a monster; the label Derry fears (his face) does not even register.

**Derry's confession.** "But I'm not afraid. People are afraid of me." He explains the mechanics of his life: everyone who looks at him thinks — he recites their thoughts bitterly — "**That's bad. That's a terrible thing. That's the ugliest thing I ever saw... Poor boy.**" And then comes the play's most painful line: "**When I look in the mirror, and see it, I'm afraid of me.**" *Explanation:* this is internalised rejection — society's disgust has become Derry's own self-image. The wound outside has produced a wound inside. Mr Lamb gently contradicts him: "No. Not the whole of you. Not of you" — meaning: the burned face is *part* of you, not the whole of you.

**Mr Lamb's unusual treatment — he talks about something else.** Instead of pitying Derry or pretending not to notice, Mr Lamb **changes the subject to his crab apples** — orange and golden, "magic fruit," ripe for making **jelly**, it being **September** — and asks Derry to **give him a hand**. Derry is almost insulted: "**What have you changed the subject for? People always do that... pretend it isn't true and isn't there... you don't ask me because you're afraid to.**" *Explanation:* this is the play's central technique. Everyone else either stares or avoids; Mr Lamb treats the face as a fact that can simply be *talked about* — no horror, no silence. He tells Derry plainly: if you want to be asked, say so. For the first time, Derry gets to speak about his face openly, as a normal thing.

**The acid story.** Derry explains: he was not burned in a fire — "**I got acid all down that side of my face and it burned it all away. It ate my face up. It ate me up. And now it's like this and it won't ever be any different.**" *Explanation:* notice the double object — it ate his face *and* "ate him up": the accident consumed both his appearance and his personality. His conclusion "it won't ever be any different" is total hopelessness.

**Mr Lamb's counter-view: gratitude and relativity.** Mr Lamb points out what Derry still has: **two eyes, two ears, two hands, two legs, a tongue and a brain** — "**the world's there to be looked at**." He insists **beauty is relative**; and when Derry mocks, "Do you think this is the world? This old garden?" Mr Lamb answers that when he is here, this garden **is** the world — "Not the only one. But the world, as much as anywhere." *Explanation:* meaning is not in the place but in the eye that sees it. Asked whether his leg hurts, he answers: "**Tin doesn't hurt, boy!**" — it hurt when it came off, aches in wet weather at the top of the stump, "it doesn't signify" (doesn't matter). Derry angrily replies that this is what everyone says: "Look at all those people in pain and brave and never cry... Think of all those people worse off than you — blinded, deaf, wheelchair, daft and dribbling." Mr Lamb surprisingly agrees — "that's all true" — but he never asked Derry to compare. *Explanation:* Mr Lamb's philosophy is not "someone has it worse"; it is "what you still have is worth living for."

**The cruelty Derry has collected.** Derry tells of a woman at a **bus stop** who whispered to her friend: "**Look at that, that's a terrible thing. That's a face only a mother could love.**" Mr Lamb's response is subtle: "Maybe not meant as such. Just something said between them." Derry: "Only I heard it." Mr Lamb then lands the blow that cracks Derry's worldview: "**So now you keep your ears shut.**" *Explanation:* Derry has selected one cruel whisper and made it the voice of the entire world; Mr Lamb shows he has chosen to be deaf to everything else. This prepares the **bees speech**: "Some hear bees and they say, bees buzz. But when you listen to bees for a long while, they hum... and **hum means 'sing'. I hear them singing, my bees.**" *Explanation:* the same sound is noise or music depending on the listener; the same face is tragedy or just a face depending on the seer. This is the philosophical heart of the play.

**The locked-room parable.** Mr Lamb tells of a man afraid of *everything* — a bus might run him over, a man might breathe deadly germs on him, a donkey might kick him, lightning might strike him, he might love a girl and she'd leave him, he might slip on a banana skin and be laughed at. So he **locked his door, got into bed, and stayed there**. "For ever?" — "For a while." "Then what?" — "**A picture fell off the wall on to his head and killed him.**" Derry laughs a lot. *Explanation:* the story's dark joke carries its lesson — you cannot escape death or misfortune by hiding from life; the danger you lock out matters less than the life you lock away. Derry is that man in embryo.

**Friendship — offered on Mr Lamb's terms.** Derry asks if Mr Lamb has friends; the answer: "**Hundreds.**" People come in — the gate's always open — they sit in the garden and in front of the fire in winter; **kids come for the apples and pears, and for toffee — "I make toffee with honey."** Derry objects that he doesn't know Derry's name or where he comes from. Mr Lamb's reply: "Do I have to write all your particulars down and put them in a filing box before you can be a friend?" Derry gives his name: "**Derry. Only it's Derek... but I hate that. Derry.**" — and adds, movingly, "If I'm your friend, you don't have to be mine. I choose that." When Derry says people you pass in the street and never see again aren't friends — "they're just nothing. People. That's all" — Mr Lamb corrects him: "**People are never just nothing. Never.**" *Explanation:* Mr Lamb grants every human being worth; Derry, starved of affection, cannot yet accept that a stranger can simply *like* him.

**Hate is the real acid.** Derry admits, "There are some people I hate." Mr Lamb's answer is one of the most quotable lines of the play: "**That'd do you more harm than any bottle of acid. Acid only burns your face... Like a bomb only blew up my leg.**" *Explanation:* the outward wound is temporary damage; hatred corrodes the whole person from inside. Note also — Mr Lamb's own leg was **blown off by a bomb in the war**; his suffering was man-made too, yet he has not become bitter. That is the living proof of his argument.

**Derry's mother's world.** Derry reports what people (his mother heard at the fair) say: children said if he went near the bonfire he'd be a "**fire-eater, a freak with his face burnt off**"; women said he should **go home and stay in hospital — he'd "be better off with others like himself."** His mother thinks **blind people should be with blind people, and "idiot boys" with idiot boys**. Mr Lamb quietly destroys the logic: "And people with no legs altogether? **What kind of a world would that be?**" *Explanation:* segregation — even kind-sounding segregation — creates a world of ghettos. Then his creed: "**Everything's different. Everything's the same, but everything is different. Itself.**" How does he know? "**Watching. Listening. Thinking.**" *Explanation:* each person is unique ("Itself"); grouping people by their disability erases what they actually are.

**The open house and the risk.** Derry wishes for a place like this — "a house with no curtains." Mr Lamb: "**The gate's always open. Everything's yours if you want it. What's mine is anybody's.**" Derry worries other visitors would mind his face. Mr Lamb: "**They might. They might not. You'd have to take the risk. So would they.**" Derry warns him he might lose all his other friends by keeping Derry. Mr Lamb: "**I've not moved.**" He shares his own nickname — kids shout "**Lamey-Lamb**" down the street — "but they still come into the garden... it's a game. **They're not afraid of me. Why should they be? Because I'm not afraid of them, that's why not.**" *Explanation:* fear is reciprocal — Derry's fear of people's eyes *creates* the barrier; Mr Lamb's fearlessness dissolves it.

**Sending Derry home — the test of choice.** It is getting late; Derry's home is **three miles across the fields**; his mother will fuss. Mr Lamb: "It's a fine evening. **You've got legs.**" Derry voices the trap: "Once I got home, they'd never let me come back." Mr Lamb reverses it: "**Once you got home, you'd never let yourself come back.**" *Explanation:* the real prison is internal — if Derry accepts other people's limits, they become his own. And then Derry says the truest sentence about himself: "**I don't know what I want. I want... something no one else has got or ever will have. Something just mine. Like this garden. I don't know what it is.**" Mr Lamb: "**You could find out... Waiting. Watching. Listening. Sitting here or going there.**" *Explanation:* Mr Lamb never lectures or commands; he plants possibilities and leaves the choice — and therefore the dignity — with the boy.

**Mr Lamb alone — the crack in the armour.** After Derry leaves, Mr Lamb speaks **to himself** (and to his bees): "**There my dears. That's you seen to. Ah... you know. We all know. I'll come back. They never do, though. Not them. Never do come back.**" *Explanation:* for the first time we see his loneliness — the open gate has let in hundreds of visitors, but **no one ever returns**. His cheerfulness has been a discipline, not a luck. This makes Scene Three devastating.

## 📖 SCENE TWO — DERRY'S HOUSE (full detailed explanation)
The scene is brief and explosive. Derry's **mother forbids** him: "You think I don't know about him... **Been told. Warned. We've not lived here three months, but I know what there is to know and you're not to go back there.**" Her information is pure **neighbourhood gossip** — she has never met Mr Lamb. Derry, for the first time in the play, argues with an adult with real force: "**What are you afraid of? What do you think he is? An old man with a tin leg and he lives in a huge house without curtains and has a garden. And I want to be there.**" He **runs off** — three miles across the fields — with the repeated cry: "**And I want the world... I want it... I want it.**" *Explanation:* the boy who climbed a wall to avoid people is now running *towards* a human being. The mother scene exists to show that Derry's transformation is real: he can now defy over-protection, choose for himself, and name what he wants.

## 📖 SCENE THREE — THE RETURN (full detailed explanation)
In the garden, **Mr Lamb is on a ladder**, steadying a branch, pulling down crab apples ("Steady... that's... got it"). Then: "**A creak. A crash. The ladder falls back, Mr Lamb with it. A thump. The branch swishes back. Creaks. Then silence.**" Derry bursts in, still panting, triumphantly — "**You see, you see! I came back. You said I wouldn't and they said... but I came back, I wanted...**" — and then he **stops dead**. Silence. He runs through the grass, kneels: "**Mr Lamb, It's all right... You fell... I'm here, Mr Lamb, It's all right. I came back. Lamey-Lamb. I did... come back.**" **[He begins to weep.]** The end.

**How to interpret the ending (examiners love this):** Mr Lamb's death is **implied, never stated** — the crash, the thump and the silence say it. There is deep irony: he dies doing an ordinary chore, alone, exactly the fate he and Derry had joked about ("If you fell and broke your neck, you could lie on the grass and die. If you were on your own."). But the play does **not** end in despair. Derry **came back** — the child who feared the world's gaze now weeps for another person, calls the mocking nickname "**Lamey-Lamb**" with love, and has already defied his mother and run three miles to claim a friendship. The teacher dies; **the teaching survives in the student**. Write in long answers: the ending is poignant, ironic, and yet affirmative — Derry will not go back to seclusion, because Mr Lamb's outlook has become his own.

## 👥 CHARACTERS — full profiles
**Derry (Derek):** fourteen; one side of his face destroyed by **acid**; hearing society's whispers since childhood, he has internalised them ("I'm afraid of me"); he is defensive, rude and suspicious on the surface — but this is armour over a boy who desperately wants affection and a life "just mine"; he rejects comparisons ("it won't make my face change") because he sees through hollow consolation; his arc: **wall → mirror-fear → garden → defiance of mother → "I want the world" → return**. In Scene One he cannot look at Mr Lamb; by Scene Three he kneels beside him weeping — empathy has replaced self-absorption.
**Mr Lamb:** an old man, **leg blown off by a bomb in the war**, living alone in a big house **without curtains**, keeping bees, making jelly and toffee with honey, reading books; outwardly a harmless eccentric, inwardly a practical philosopher: everything shared, fear refused, uniqueness of every person ("Itself") respected; his kindness is never sentimental — he asks the direct question, makes the dark joke, gives the hard truth ("hatred will harm you more than acid"); underneath he is **lonely** ("They never do... come back"), which makes his giving heroic. He is the **catalyst**: one afternoon changes Derry's life.
**Derry's mother:** loving but over-protective; her picture of Mr Lamb comes from gossip and warnings, not knowledge; she represents society's instinct to segregate the different ("better off with others like himself"); her love, ironically, would wall Derry in.
**The children and the fair-women:** collective minor characters who voice casual cruelty ("fire-eater," "freak," "only a mother could love") — society's unthinking hurt.

## 🧩 THEMES — with full elaboration
1. **Disability vs inner strength:** the play insists the *real* impairment is not physical — Derry can see, hear, walk and think, yet lives disabled by fear; Mr Lamb has half a leg and lives more fully than anyone. The NCERT question about "actual pain being less than the sense of alienation" is answered here: what disables Derry is not acid but other people's eyes — and his own belief in them.
2. **Appearance vs reality (the title):** on the face of it, Derry is a tragedy and Mr Lamb a jolly eccentric; in reality Derry is a boy hungry for love, and Mr Lamb a lonely man performing courage. The play repeatedly shows surfaces lying.
3. **Loneliness and companionship:** two isolated people heal each other in an afternoon; Derry gives Mr Lamb the one thing the open gate never brought — someone who **came back**.
4. **Attitude as choice:** bees buzz or sing; the same world rewards Mr Lamb and defeats Derry — the difference is the listening ear.
5. **Over-protection as a cage:** mother, fair-women and doctors all "mean well" with hospital-and-your-own-kind advice; the play shows such love walls the person out of life.
6. **Death and continuity:** Mr Lamb dies alone, yet the play ends with life winning — his vision is inherited; a good influence outlives the man.

## ❓ QUESTIONS WITH FULL MODEL ANSWERS (adapt, don't memorise word-for-word)
**Q1. What is it that draws Derry towards Mr Lamb in spite of himself? (NCERT)**
A: Derry has spent his life being stared at, whispered about and pitied, so he trusts no one. Mr Lamb is the first person who treats him with neither fear nor fake sympathy: he talks to him naturally ("Mind the apples"), asks about his burned face openly instead of avoiding it, jokes with him, argues with his despair, and shares his own disability — a bomb-lost leg — without shame. He offers welcome without conditions ("the gate's always open") and even friendship without formalities. For a boy who hears only "poor boy" and "freak," this fearless, equal treatment draws him against his own suspicion.

**Q2. In which section does Mr Lamb display loneliness? How does he overcome it? (NCERT)**
A: His loneliness shows at the end of Scene One, when, thinking himself alone, he tells his bees, "I'll come back. They never do, though. Not them. Never do come back" — revealing that despite hundreds of visitors, no one returns, and no one would know if he were alive or dead. He overcomes this loneliness by keeping fully occupied — picking apples, making jelly and toffee with honey, tending bees, reading books — by keeping the gate literally and figuratively open, welcoming strangers like Derry, and loving company of every kind. His busyness and openness are his medicine for solitude.

**Q3. The actual pain of a physical impairment is often much less than the sense of alienation felt by the person. What behaviour does such a person expect from others? (NCERT)**
A: The play shows that Derry's acid-scarred face causes him no daily physical agony; what destroys him is people's reaction — stares, whispers ("a face only a mother could love"), the advice to live "with others like himself," even his mother's over-protectiveness. What a differently-abled person expects is to be treated as a normal human being: to be spoken to directly and not about, to be asked and not pitied, to be included rather than segregated. Mr Lamb models exactly this — he asks about the face, jokes about it, and hands Derry a task as an equal.

**Q4. Will Derry get back to his old seclusion or will Mr Lamb's brief association change him? (NCERT)**
A: Derry will not return to seclusion. The change is already complete by Scene Two: he defies his mother's gossip-based ban, runs three miles back to the garden, crying "I want the world... I want it." In Scene Three he returns to find Mr Lamb dead, yet he kneels and weeps "I came back. Lamey-Lamb." His grief proves love, and love is the opposite of seclusion. Mr Lamb has taught him that the walls were internal ("Once you got home, you'd never let yourself come back"); that lesson cannot be unlearned. The future will hold sadness, not retreat.

**Q5. Justify the title "On the Face of It."**
A: The idiom "on the face of it" means judging by outward appearance, and the play is a sustained attack on such judgment. Society sees Derry's burned face and concludes tragedy, monster, "poor boy" — the reality is a frightened, affection-starved boy with two eyes, ears, hands, legs, tongue and brain. Mr Lamb, on the face of it a harmless old eccentric with a tin leg, is in reality a philosopher whose cheerfulness conceals deep loneliness. Even the insult "Lamey-Lamb" becomes, on Derry's lips at the end, a name of love. The title warns that faces — of events and of people — mislead; truth lies beneath.

**Q6. Comment on the ending of the play.**
A: The ending is ironic and poignant, yet affirmative. Mr Lamb, who preached engagement with life, falls from his ladder doing an ordinary chore and dies alone — the very fate he joked about. But the play's real ending is Derry: he has defied his mother, run three miles, and returns calling "Lamey-Lamb" with love, weeping for another person for the first time. The teacher dies; the teaching survives. Hill suggests that a liberating influence, once planted, outlives the liberator — Derry will not crawl back into seclusion.

**Q7. Compare the world of Mr Lamb and the world Derry comes from.**
A: Derry's world — home, street, fair — runs on fear and labels: people stare, whisper, advise segregation, and his mother locks him in "for his own good." Mr Lamb's world is the reverse: no curtains (nothing shut out or in), an always-open gate, bees heard as singers, weeds as plants, children welcomed to apples and toffee, and every person treated as "Itself" — unique. In the first world difference is a defect to hide; in the second it is simply variety. The play's drama is Derry crossing from one world into the other in a single afternoon.

# ✍️ CHAPTER 2: THE THIRD LEVEL — Jack Finney
## (First-person fantasy — time, escape and a psychiatrist who vanishes into his own diagnosis)

## 🎬 ABOUT THE AUTHOR AND THE TEXT
**Jack Finney** (1911–1995) was an American writer famous for fiction about **time** — his novels *Time and Again* and *From Time to Time* are classics of time travel, and his thriller *The Body Snatchers* became famous cinema. "The Third Level" (1950) is a short story written in the shadow of the **Second World War and the early Cold War** — an age when ordinary Americans genuinely felt the modern world was "full of insecurity, fear, war and worry." The story is told in the **first person** by an ordinary man, **Charley**, and it is built so carefully that fantasy and reality keep swapping places: every time the reader calls the third level imaginary, the story produces a new "documentary proof" that it is real — and every time it seems real, a rational explanation reappears. **The ambiguity is deliberate; hold both readings in every answer.**

## 📖 THE FULL STORY — DETAILED WALKTHROUGH

**The outrageous claim that opens the story.** Charley announces that the presidents of the **New York, New Haven and Hartford railroads** would "swear on a stack of timetables" that Grand Central Station has **only two levels** — but Charley says there are **three**, "because I've been there." *Explanation:* the story begins like a confession of madness — and Charley knows it, which is exactly why he immediately tells us he consulted a psychiatrist. He is an honest, self-doubting narrator, and that honesty makes the fantasy believable.

**The psychiatric explanation — given first, on purpose.** Charley's **psychiatrist friend Sam Weiner** calls the third level "**a waking-dream wish fulfillment**": Charley is unhappy — not personally, but because "the modern world is full of **insecurity, fear, war, worry**" — "and that I just want to escape." Charley notes drily that this diagnosis "made my wife kind of mad," but concedes the logic: "**Well, who doesn't? Everybody I know wants to escape.**" His friends agree and add their own evidence: his **stamp collecting** is "a '**temporary refuge from reality**.'" Charley's defence is charming but self-defeating: his **grandfather** started the collection in peaceful days when "he didn't need any refuge from reality," and besides, "**President Roosevelt collected stamps too.**" *Explanation:* the narrator keeps offering alibis that actually strengthen the diagnosis — a comic technique that keeps the reader smiling and unsure. The era's details (worry, war) root the fantasy in real 1950s anxiety.

**Grand Central as a living, growing maze.** Charley explains how such a thing could happen to him: he is "just an ordinary guy named Charley, **thirty-one years old**," in a **tan gabardine suit and a straw hat with a fancy band** — "I passed a dozen men who looked just like me." He wasn't trying to escape; he "just wanted to get home to **Louisa**, my wife." He had entered Grand Central from **Vanderbilt Avenue**, gone down to the **first level** (trains like the **Twentieth Century**), then to the **second level** (suburban trains), and then "ducked into an arched doorway heading for the subway — and **got lost**. That's easy to do." *Explanation:* the story's realism is built from exact, ordinary details — ages, clothes, street names. Then comes the beautiful conceit: Charley has wandered into a **tunnel about a mile long** and emerged in the **lobby of the Roosevelt Hotel**; another time into an office building on **Forty-sixth Street, three blocks away**. "**Sometimes I think Grand Central is growing like a tree, pushing out new corridors and staircases like roots.**" He imagines unknown tunnels "feeling their way under the city" toward **Times Square** and **Central Park** — and then the thought he never told the psychiatrist: "for so many people through the years Grand Central has been an **exit, a way of escape** — maybe that's how the tunnel I got into..." *Explanation:* the station itself becomes a metaphor for escape; the city's subconscious is digging refuges.

**Arriving on the third level — the 1894 catalogue (memorise this cluster).** The corridor "**began angling left and slanting downward**"; he heard only "the empty sound of my own footsteps," then "that hollow roar ahead that means open space and people talking." He came out on the **third level**: a **smaller room**, **fewer ticket windows and train gates**, a **wooden, old-looking information booth**, its man in a **green eyeshade and long black sleeve protectors**. The **lights were dim and flickering — open-flame gaslights**. There were **brass spittoons** on the floor. A man pulled a **gold watch** from his **vest pocket** — **derby hat, black four-button suit with tiny lapels, big black handlebar mustache**. Everyone was dressed like "**eighteen-ninety-something**" — beards, sideburns, fancy mustaches. A woman wore a dress with **leg-of-mutton sleeves** and skirts down to the top of her **high-buttoned shoes**. On the tracks stood a very small **Currier & Ives locomotive with a funnel-shaped stack**. *Explanation:* Finney does not say "I travelled back in time"; he lets **objects** announce the era, one authentic detail at a time, which is far more convincing.

**The proof of the date.** To be sure, Charley checked the newsboy's stack: the newspaper was **The World** — which "hasn't been published for years" — with a lead story about **President Cleveland**. Later Charley found that exact front page in the **Public Library files: printed June 11, 1894**. *Explanation:* this is the first "documentary proof" — the story turns fantasy into archive.

**Galesburg, Illinois — the destination of longing.** At the ticket window Charley realises he can buy tickets that would take **Louisa and him anywhere in the United States... in the year 1894** — and he wants **two tickets to Galesburg, Illinois**, where he went to school: "a wonderful town still, with **big old frame houses, huge lawns, and tremendous trees whose branches meet overhead and roof the streets**." In 1894, "**summer evenings were twice as long**," people sat out on lawns — "**men smoking cigars and talking quietly, the women waving palm-leaf fans, with the fire-flies all around, in a peaceful world. To be back there with the First World War still twenty years off, and World War II over forty years in the future... I wanted two tickets for that.**" *Explanation:* this sentence is the thesis of the story. Galesburg 1894 is not a place so much as a **time before history's disasters** — the exact opposite of the "insecurity, fear, war, worry" of Charley's century. The third level leads precisely to where the wish points: that is Sam's "wish fulfillment" made visible.

**The money failure.** The clerk glanced at the fancy hatband "but he figured the fare," and Charley had enough for **two coach tickets, one way** — but when he counted out his bills the clerk snapped: "**That ain't money, mister, and if you're trying to skin me, you won't get very far.**" Charley's money was modern; the 1894 notes were "**old-style bills, half again as big**." He fled — "There's nothing nice about jail, even in 1894." *Explanation:* the fantasy obeys its own rules — you cannot spend today in yesterday; the tiniest realistic detail (wrong currency) wrecks the dream, exactly as reality interrupts daydreams.

**The obsessive aftermath.** Next day at lunch Charley **drew three hundred dollars out of the bank — nearly all they had — and bought old-style currency** from a coin dealer, paying a premium (his $300 bought **less than $200 in old bills**); he didn't care — "**eggs were thirteen cents a dozen in 1894.**" But he **never found the corridor again**, "although I've tried often enough." **Louisa was pretty worried** and made him stop; he "went back to my stamps." *Explanation:* the psychiatrist's alarm at the currency purchase is the comic peak — the escapism has become expensive and literal. Note the chain: work → subway → lost → 1894; marriage strain → stamps as consolation. Every element doubles as "evidence" for the wish-fulfillment reading.

**The proof that ends the story — Sam's disappearance and the first-day cover.** Charley and Louisa now search every weekend, "because now we have **proof** that the third level is still there. **My friend Sam Weiner disappeared!**" Sam was a **city boy** but had always loved the sound of Galesburg. Then, one night, fussing with his stamp collection, Charley found among his **oldest first-day covers** one "that shouldn't have been there." *Explanation of the term (definitions get asked):* a **first-day cover** is an envelope mailed to yourself (or a fellow collector) on the **very first day a new stamp is issued**; the **postmark proves the date**; they are **never opened** — you just put blank paper inside. This cover was addressed to Charley's **grandfather at his home in Galesburg**, postmarked **July 18, 1894**, bearing a **six-cent dull brown stamp with a picture of President Garfield**. Naturally the grandfather had filed it in his collection, unopened — until Charley took it out and opened it. Inside, instead of blank paper, was a **letter from Sam**.

**The letter and the final irony.** Sam writes from **Galesburg, 1894**: he found the third level, bought his ticket with old currency, and is now settled there running a **hay, feed and grain business** — "he always said that's what he really wished he could do, and he certainly can't go back to his old business. Not in Galesburg, Illinois, in 1894. His old business? **Why, Sam was my psychiatrist.**" The story ends with Charley and Louisa still looking, every weekend. *Explanation:* the man who "proved" the third level was wish-fulfillment has himself fulfilled the wish — and note what Sam's new business means: no hospitals, no mad twentieth century, just hay, feed and grain; peace itself. Whether Charley invented the letter, or Finney's world genuinely has a crack in it, the text refuses to say — the **open ending is the design**.

## 👥 CHARACTERS
**Charley** — 31, ordinary, hard-working, happily married; a stamp collector; honest enough to report the evidence against himself; represents **every modern person's suppressed wish to escape**. His reliability is the story's engine: he never insists he is right.
**Louisa** — Charley's wife; first angry at the psychiatrist's "unhappy" verdict, then frightened for Charley, forbidding the search; finally she joins the weekend hunts — showing the wish for peace is shared at home.
**Sam Weiner** — Charley's **psychiatrist friend**; the voice of reason ("waking-dream wish fulfillment"); then he **vanishes into 1894**, becoming the story's central irony and its strongest "proof." The realist converted is the story's best argument.
**The grandfather** — began the stamp collection in peaceful times; his unopened first-day cover is the time-tunnel's mailbox; he represents a generation that had no need of refuge.

## 🧩 THEMES
1. **Escapism** — the master theme: the third level is the physical form of the universal wish to flee a world of insecurity, fear, war and worry; stamp collecting, Galesburg nostalgia and Sam's hay-business are all refuges of increasing literalness.
2. **Intersection of time and space** — one arched doorway in an ordinary station opens into 1894; Finney makes time travel suburban and homely: no machines, just a wrong turn.
3. **Fantasy vs reality** — the story is a tug-of-war between the psychiatrist's explanations and the archive-room proofs (the June 11, 1894 front page; the July 18, 1894 postmark; Sam's disappearance); the tension is never resolved, and that is the artistry.
4. **Nostalgia for a pre-war world** — Galesburg 1894 = peace, community, slowness, fireflies, thirteen-cent eggs; the past is idealised precisely because the present is frightening.
5. **Philately as time travel** — stamps and first-day covers are literally the plot's vehicles across time; collecting is memory-keeping.

## ❓ QUESTIONS WITH FULL MODEL ANSWERS
**Q1. What does the third level refer to?**
A: Literally, it is a mysterious third railway level at Grand Central Station, New York, that officially does not exist — a corridor Charley took by mistake and found itself in the year 1894, with gaslights, brass spittoons, old newspapers and a small Currier & Ives locomotive. Symbolically, it refers to any imaginative escape, any "waking-dream wish fulfillment," by which people flee the insecurity, fear, war and worry of the modern world into a safer, simpler past.

**Q2. Do you think the third level was a medium of escape for Charley? Why? (NCERT)**
A: Yes — on the psychiatrist's reading, everything points that way. Charley himself says the modern world is full of insecurity, fear, war and worry, and "who doesn't" want to escape? His stamp collecting is called a "temporary refuge from reality"; the destination he chooses, Galesburg 1894, is described as a peaceful world with the First World War twenty years off and the Second over forty years away — escape written all over it. Even Grand Central is imagined as "an exit, a way of escape." Yet Finney plants documentary proofs (the dated front page, the postmarked cover, Sam's disappearance) that keep the "real" reading alive — so a complete answer accepts the escape reading as the story's psychological truth while admitting the deliberate ambiguity.

**Q3. What do you infer from Sam's letter to Charley? (NCERT)**
A: The letter turns the story upside down: Sam, the psychiatrist who diagnosed the third level as "waking-dream wish fulfillment," has himself reached Galesburg in 1894 through the third level and is happily running the hay, feed and grain business "he always said that's what he really wished he could do." The inference is deliciously double-edged: either the third level is real enough to convert the sceptic, or Charley's fantasy has become so complete that it fabricated the proof. Either way, the deeper inference is human: the need for peace is stronger than psychiatric labels, and even the man of science longs for the refuge.

**Q4. Do you see an intersection of time and space in the story? (NCERT)**
A: Yes — that is the story's structural idea. Space stays constant (Grand Central Station, New York) while time shifts (from the 1950s to June 11, 1894): the same doorway opens into another era, confirmed by gaslights, spittoons, the newspaper The World with President Cleveland's story, and old-style currency "half again as big." Time also flows in reverse through objects: the first-day cover postmarked July 18, 1894 travels forward to arrive in Charley's collection. Place becomes the meeting point of centuries.

**Q5. "The modern world is full of insecurity, fear, war, worry." In what ways do we try to overcome them? (NCERT)**
A: People build refuges of many kinds — hobbies and collections (like Charley's stamps), nostalgia for the "good old days," holidays and retreats, religion and meditation, films, fiction and fantasy worlds, and simple domestic happiness (Charley's wish is only to get home to Louisa). The story's own answer is extreme: Charley tries to *buy* his way into 1894 with $300 of old currency. The point the story makes gently is that these refuges are natural and human — "everybody I know wants to escape" — the question is whether we let them remain holidays, like Sam's real hay business, or prisons, like the locked-room of anxiety.

**Q6. How does Finney interweave fantasy and reality? (Compare with 'Adventure' by Jayant Narlikar.)**
A: Finney sets the impossible inside a lattice of the ordinary: a 31-year-old commuter, a wife, a psychiatrist, subway levels, coin dealers. Fantasy is then certified by bureaucratic artefacts — library files, postmarks, timetables — so the impossible arrives with documentation. Jayant Narlikar's 'Adventure' (Hornbill XI) does the same with science: Professor Gangadharpant slips into an alternate India where the Marathas won Panipat, explained by the cataclysm theory; both writers ground the impossible in scholarship — Finney in psychiatry and philately, Narlikar in physics and history. The difference: Narlikar's fantasy obeys scientific law and reverses cleanly, while Finney leaves his doorway open — Sam is still in 1894, and Charley is still looking.

# ✍️ CHAPTER 3: MEMORIES OF CHILDHOOD — Zitkala-Sa and Bama
## (Two autobiographical episodes — two cultures, one wound: the denial of dignity)

## 🎬 ABOUT THE TEXT AND THE TWO WRITERS
This unit places side by side **two first-person autobiographical episodes** from the childhoods of two women from **marginalised communities**, written in two distant cultures: the first by an **American Indian woman born in the late nineteenth century**, the second by a **contemporary Tamil Dalit writer**.
**Zitkala-Sa** is the pen name of **Gertrude Simmons Bonnin** (born **1876**), an "extraordinarily talented and educated Native American woman who struggled and triumphed in a time when severe prejudice prevailed towards Native American culture and women." From **1900** she published articles **criticising the Carlisle Indian School** — the infamous boarding-school system that stripped Native children of their culture; her life was "dedicated against the evils of oppression."
**Bama** is the pen name of a **Tamil Dalit woman from a Roman Catholic family**. Her three main works: the autobiography **'Karukku' (1992)** — this extract's source — the novel **'Sangati' (1994)**, and the short-story collection **'Kisumbukkaaran' (1996)**. '**Karukku**' means **palmyra leaves**, whose **serrated edges on both sides are like double-edged swords**; by a felicitous pun, the word contains '**karu**' (embryo, seed) and so also suggests **freshness and newness**. *Explanation of the pairing:* one episode shows **cultural imperialism** (a white institution erasing Native identity), the other shows **caste oppression** (fellow Indians practising untouchability) — but both children suffer the same thing: **humiliation before they had the words to name it**, and both grow into writers who fight back with the pen.

## 📖 PART I — "THE CUTTING OF MY LONG HAIR" (Zitkala-Sa): DETAILED WALKTHROUGH

**Arrival in the "land of apples."** The first day is "**bitter-cold**"; snow still covers the ground and the trees are bare. A **large bell** rings for breakfast — "its loud metallic voice crashing through the belfry overhead and into our sensitive ears." The "annoying clatter of shoes," the "constant clash of harsh noises," and "an undercurrent of many voices murmuring an **unknown tongue**" made "a **bedlam** within which I was **securely tied**. And though my spirit tore itself in struggling for its **lost freedom**, all was useless." *Explanation:* every image is of imprisonment — bedlam (chaos), being "tied," lost freedom. The child's senses (sensitive ears) and spirit are native to a quiet natural world; the institution's noise is the first assault. "Land of apples" is itself ironic — in her tradition apples belong to the white man's world; the promised garden is a place of discipline.

**The dining room — humiliation by rules.** A **paleface woman with white hair** leads them; the Indian girls wear **stiff shoes and closely clinging dresses**; the small ones, **sleeved aprons and shingled hair**. Zitkala-Sa walks "**noiselessly in my soft moccasins**" and feels "like sinking to the floor, for my **blanket had been stripped from my shoulders**." She watches the three young **braves** of her party "in the rear ranks, looking as uncomfortable as I felt." Then the **three-bell torture**: a **small bell** taps and each pupil draws a chair from under the table — supposing this meant "sit," she slips into hers, and finds she is **the only one seated**. A **second bell** — all are seated "at last," and she "had to crawl back" into her chair. A man's voice murmurs (grace) while "all the others hung their heads over their plates"; the **paleface woman's eyes catch hers** — "Immediately I dropped my eyes." A **third bell** — everyone eats. "**I began crying instead**, for by this time I was afraid to venture anything more." *Explanation:* the scene is a perfect miniature of colonisation — her body reads the wrong cues because the language of manners is alien; every mistake is public and watched; the spied-upon glance of the white woman adds surveillance to confusion. Sitting when one should stand is trivial — which is exactly the point: in an institution built to erase you, even breakfast is an exam you cannot pass.

**The warning.** "But this eating by formula was not the hardest trial." Her friend **Judewin**, who "knew a few words of English," overheard the paleface woman talking about **cutting their long, heavy hair**. The cultural code is spelled out: "**only unskilled warriors who were captured had their hair shingled by the enemy**"; among their people, **short hair was worn by mourners, and shingled hair by cowards**. Judewin counsels submission — "**We have to submit, because they are strong**" — but the child answers with the episode's defining line: "**No, I will not submit! I will struggle first!**" *Explanation:* the hair is not fashion — it is honour, identity, the body of the tribe's law. To cut it is to brand her a coward and a prisoner. Judewin's realism versus the child's rebellion is the episode's moral argument in two sentences.

**The struggle, and the defeat.** She watches her chance and **disappears** — creeps upstairs "in my squeaking shoes" (her moccasins have been exchanged for hard shoes — note: even her footwear has been confiscated), finds a dim room with **three white beds** and **dark green curtains**, and **crawls under the bed** into the farthest corner. Voices call her name; she does not answer. The searchers enter, throw up the curtains — "the room was filled with sudden light" — peer behind trunks, and somehow look under the bed. She is **dragged out, "kicking and scratching wildly," carried downstairs and tied fast in a chair**. "I cried aloud, shaking my head all the while until I felt the **cold blades of the scissors against my neck**, and heard them **gnaw off one of my thick braids**. Then **I lost my spirit**." *Explanation:* the hiding under the bed is the last resource of the powerless — a child's strategy against an institution. The verbs are brutal (dragged, tied, gnawed); the institution does not negotiate with a child's will, it simply overpowers it. "I lost my spirit" — the phrase lands like a death: what is killed is not the body but the self.

**The summing up of all indignities.** "Since the day I was taken from my mother I had suffered **extreme indignities**. People had stared at me. I had been tossed about in the air **like a wooden puppet**. And now my long hair was **shingled like a coward's**! In my anguish I **moaned for my mother**, but no one came to comfort me. Not a soul reasoned quietly with me, as my own mother used to do; for now I was **only one of many little animals driven by a herder**." *Explanation:* the final simile completes the dehumanisation — from child to puppet to animal. The absence of the mother is the measure of the institution: where a mother would reason and comfort, the school only seizes and cuts. Remember this simile — it is the most-quoted line of the extract.

## 📖 PART II — "WE TOO ARE HUMAN BEINGS" (Bama): DETAILED WALKTHROUGH

**The knowing-that-has-no-name.** Bama is in the **third class** at school. She "hadn't yet heard people speak openly of **untouchability**. But I had already **seen, felt, experienced and been humiliated** by what it is." *Explanation:* the sentence is the episode's thesis — oppression teaches itself before textbooks name it. Everything that follows is a child's happy street coming apart at one seam.

**The ten-minute road that took an hour.** Walking home should take **ten minutes**; it takes her **thirty minutes to an hour**, because every street attraction "would tether my legs." The catalogue (in an answer, name four or five): the **performing monkey**; the **Maariyaata temple** with its huge bell and **pongal offerings** cooked in front of it; the **dried fish stall by the statue of Gandhi**; the sweet stall and the snack stalls side by side; the **street light "always demonstrating how it could change from blue to violet"**; the **narikkuravan huntergypsy** with his **wild lemur in cages**, selling needles, clay beads and ear-cleaning instruments; political parties putting up **stages and haranguing through mikes**; **street plays, puppet shows**, "no magic, no miracle" stunts; the **coffee clubs**, where each waiter **cools the coffee by lifting a tumbler high and pouring it into a tumbler held in the other hand**; people **chopping onions with their eyes turned elsewhere so they wouldn't smart**; the **almond tree**; seasonal treats — **mango, cucumber, sugarcane, sweet-potato, palm-shoots, gram, palm-syrup and palm-fruit, guavas and jackfruit** — and vendors of **payasam, halva, boiled tamarind seeds and iced lollies**. *Explanation:* this joyful inventory is deliberate — the author builds the street as a paradise of the senses so that the wound, when it comes, tears the whole picture. Note that the street is also her community's world: the temple, the festival food, the performers are all her people's culture.

**The threshing floor.** One day at her street's corner a **threshing floor** has been set up: the **landlord** sits "on a piece of sacking spread over a stone ledge," while "our people" drive **muzzled cattle in pairs, round and round**, to tread out the grain (the animals are muzzled "so that they wouldn't help themselves to the straw"). An **elder of her street** comes from the bazaar carrying a small packet — from the oil-stained paper, something like **vadai or green banana bhajji** — holding it **out by its string, without touching it**. Bama finds it hilarious — "such a big man... making such a game out of carrying the parcel" — she doubles up, imagining the vadais falling out. The elder reaches the landlord, **bows low and extends the packet, cupping the hand that holds the string with his other hand**; the landlord opens the parcel and "began to eat the vadais." *Explanation:* look at the geometry of power: the landlord sits, the elder stands and bows; the landlord eats, the elder holds air. The word "muzzled" applied to the cattle quietly echoes on the workers — both are harnessed to another man's harvest.

**Annan's revelation.** At home she tells the story "in all its comic detail" to her **elder brother Annan**, a **university student home for the holidays**. **"Annan was not amused."** He explains: the elder "wasn't being funny" — "**everybody believed that they were upper caste and therefore must not touch us. If they did, they would be polluted. That's why he had to carry the package by its string.**" "When I heard this, I didn't want to laugh any more, and **I felt terribly sad**." Then the anger arrives, fully formed: she feels "so provoked" she wants to **touch those wretched vadais herself straightaway**; she asks why her people should "fetch and carry for these fellows" — "**Because they had scraped four coins together, did that mean they must lose all human feelings? But we too are human beings.**" She resolves: her people should work their fields, take their wages, "and leave it at that" — no more reverent errands. *Explanation:* the title of the episode lives in this line — the claim to common humanity. Note the order of emotions: laughter → sadness → fury → resolve. That sequence IS the awakening of resistance, and examiners reward anyone who can name it.

**Annan's own humiliation — and his remedy.** Annan tells his own story: walking home along the **irrigation tank** one day, one of the **landlord's men** found him unfamiliar and asked his name; hearing it, the man asked at once — "**Thambi, on which street do you live?**" *Explanation:* the question sounds innocent; it is a caste-detector. In that world the street name announces the caste — identity reduced to address. Annan's advice to his little sister is the ideological heart of the piece: "**We are stripped of all dignity or respect... But if we study and make progress, we can throw away these indignities. So study with care, learn all you can. If you are always ahead in your lessons, people will come to you of their own accord and attach themselves to you. Work hard and learn.**" Bama obeys with everything in her: "I studied hard, with all my breath and being, **in a frenzy almost**... I stood **first in my class**. And because of that, **many people became my friends**." *Explanation:* Annan's strategy is education as a ladder out of indignity — but notice it is also an accommodation: he does not say "end untouchability," he says "rise above their reach." Bama's later life as a writer ('Karukku' is the first Dalit woman's autobiography in Tamil) shows where that first-in-class road led.

## 🧩 THE COMPARISON — the most probable long question, fully worked
**Common ground (write these as parallel sentences):** Both are **first-person childhood memoirs** of women from **marginalised communities** — Native American and Dalit. Both children experience **institutional discrimination before they can name it** — Zitkala-Sa feels "securely tied" in the school's bedlam; Bama has "seen, felt, experienced and been humiliated" without knowing the word untouchability. In both, the **humiliation of the body is really humiliation of the soul** — the shingled braid brands a proud child "coward"; the string-held packet brands a whole people "untouchable." Both children respond first as **isolated individuals** (hiding under a bed; laughing at comedy) and are then handed a **mentor's voice** — Judewin's warning, Annan's advice. Both texts end in **assertion, not defeat** — "No, I will not submit!" and "study... and stand first." And both writers grew into **chroniclers of oppression** — the school critic of 1900 and the author of 'Karukku.'
**Differences (write as contrast sentences):** The oppression is **racial-cultural** in Part I — an alien regime demolishes hair, clothes, food rituals and language; in Part II it is **social-caste** — one's own countrymen practise pollution-by-touch. Zitkala-Sa's resistance is **immediate, physical and crushed** ("tied fast in a chair... I lost my spirit"); Bama's response is **delayed, internal and successful** (education → first in class → friends). The oppressor in Part I is an **institution** (the school as system); in Part II a **mindset** (belief in pollution) that even kind neighbours carry. The remedies differ: Zitkala-Sa's later life is **public protest and writing against the Carlisle system**; Bama's path is **education and literature as dignity**. One child loses her spirit that day and must rebuild it over a lifetime; the other gains her purpose that day and never looks back.

## ❓ QUESTIONS WITH FULL MODEL ANSWERS
**Q1. The two accounts are based in two distant cultures. What is the commonality of theme? (NCERT)**
A: Both episodes record how children from marginalised communities — a Native American girl in a white-run school and a Dalit girl in a caste-ridden Tamil village — first experience discrimination in childhood, before they even have a name for it. In both, humiliation targets the body but wounds the soul: Zitkala-Sa's shingled braids brand her a coward by her own culture's code, and Bama's elder must carry a vadai packet by its string because his touch is held "polluting." Both children resist — one in futile rebellion, one through education — and both narrators grew into writers who spent their lives fighting oppression. The common theme is the denial of human dignity and the birth of the refusal to accept it.

**Q2. Why did Zitkala-Sa resist the cutting of her hair, and what does her defeat show?**
A: In her culture, only captured unskilled warriors had their hair shingled by the enemy; short hair marked mourners and shingled hair marked cowards. To submit to the scissors was therefore to erase her identity and honour — so she hid under a bed and fought "kicking and scratching wildly." Her defeat — dragged out, "tied fast in a chair," hearing the scissors "gnaw off one of my thick braids" until "I lost my spirit" — shows the crushing power of an institution over a lone child, and the violence of forced assimilation: the school did not persuade her, it simply overpowered her, leaving her "one of many little animals driven by a herder."

**Q3. Describe the dining-hall episode and its effect on Zitkala-Sa.**
A: In the bell-ruled dining room she misread every cue: when the first bell rang and chairs were drawn out she sat, and found herself "the only one seated"; a second bell brought the others down around her; she was caught by the paleface woman's watchful eyes; and at the third bell, while everyone ate, "I began crying instead." The effect is total disorientation — the child who "was afraid to venture anything more" has learned that this world punishes innocence. The episode shows how a colonising school humiliates through trivial rules, turning even breakfast into an instrument of conquest.

**Q4. Why did Bama laugh at the elder, and why did she stop laughing?**
A: She laughed because a grown, respected man was carrying a snack packet held out at arm's length by its string, bowing low as he handed it over — pure comedy to a child. She stopped when her brother Annan explained the logic: the "upper castes" believed that touching them would pollute them, so the elder carried the packet by its string. Her laughter turned to sadness and then to anger — "we too are human beings" — and to the resolve that her people should work for wages, not reverence. The moment is her awakening to untouchability: comedy transformed, in one sentence of Annan's, into injustice.

**Q5. What is Annan's advice, and what is its significance?**
A: Annan tells her that they are "stripped of all dignity or respect," but that study and progress can "throw away these indignities": "If you are always ahead in your lessons, people will come to you of their own accord and attach themselves to you." Its significance is double: it makes **education the Dalit road to dignity**, and it explains Bama's own life — she studied "in a frenzy," stood first, gained friends, and became the pioneering author of 'Karukku.' At the same time, a thoughtful answer notes its limit: education lifts the individual; the system itself is left standing — which is why Bama's pen, not just her marks, became her final answer.

**Q6. "It may take a long time for oppression to be resisted." Discuss with reference to both texts.**
A: Both narrators show resistance beginning in childhood and maturing over a lifetime. Zitkala-Sa's "No, I will not submit!" fails that day — she loses her braid and her spirit — but the adult Gertrude Simmons Bonnin fought the Carlisle school system in print from 1900, turning private humiliation into public crusade. Bama's resistance begins the evening she stops laughing, and matures into first-in-class determination and then into 'Karukku,' the autobiography that put Dalit women's experience into Tamil literature. Both lives prove the line: oppression is not ended by one gesture; it is ended by generations of steady refusal — and writing itself became their weapon.

# ✍️ CHAPTER 4: THE ENEMY — Pearl S. Buck
## (A wartime story of a surgeon's conscience — duty versus hatred, with a shrewd mind intact)

## 🎬 ABOUT THE AUTHOR AND THE TEXT
**Pearl S. Buck** (1892–1973) was an American writer who grew up in China as the daughter of missionaries and spent roughly the first half of her life in the East; she won the **Nobel Prize for Literature (1938)** — famous for *The Good Earth*. Because she belonged emotionally to both East and West, her great subject was **the common humanity underneath racial division** — exactly the theme of "The Enemy." The setting is the **Japanese coast during the Second World War**: Japan and America are enemies, Japanese newspapers report that occupied peoples welcome the Japanese army "with cries of joy at their liberation," and whispers circulate about the torture of prisoners. Into this world, an **American prisoner of war is washed ashore, dying, at the doorstep of a Japanese surgeon** — and the whole story is built on one question: **when your country's enemy is dying on your beach, are you a Japanese first or a doctor first?**

## 📖 THE FULL STORY — DETAILED WALKTHROUGH

**Dr Sadao Hoki — the man and his making.** Sadao's house stands on a **spot of the Japanese coast where he played as a little boy** — "a low, square stone house... set upon rocks well above a narrow beach that was outlined with bent pines." As a boy he climbed those pines barefoot, as he had seen men climb for coconuts in the **South Seas**, where his father often took him — and the father never failed to say: "**Those islands yonder, they are the stepping stones to the future for Japan.**" When little Sadao asked, "Where shall we step from them?" the father answered: "**Who knows? Who can limit our future? It depends on what we make it.**" *Explanation:* the father's creed is loving nationalism — Japan's future built on education and discipline; it also plants the perfectionism that will govern Sadao's every action, from surgery to escape plan. The father "never joked or played with him but spent infinite pains upon him, who was his only son"; **Sadao's education was his father's chief concern**.

**The surgeon kept back from the war.** At **twenty-two** Sadao was sent to **America** "to learn all that could be learned of surgery and medicine"; he returned at **thirty**, famous as both **surgeon and scientist**, "perfecting a discovery which would **render wounds entirely clean**." Two reasons kept him home instead of with the troops: his discovery, and the likelihood that the **old General** would need an operation "for a condition for which he was now being treated medically." *Explanation:* remember both reasons — the General's illness becomes the engine of the plot's second half.

**Hana — love with a racial filter.** Sadao met Hana in America at the house of **Professor Harley**, whose rooms were so small, food so bad and wife so voluble that Sadao nearly didn't go that night — "by chance literally." But "he had waited to fall in love with her until he was sure she was **Japanese**," because "**his father would never have received her unless she had been pure in her race**." They finished their studies, returned home, and married "**in the old Japanese way**" once his father had seen her. They are "perfectly happy," with **two children** — a small son and a **baby of nearly three months**. *Explanation:* Buck quietly plants the theme of racial purity inside the hero's own love story — Sadao is a product of his culture, not a saint; that makes his later choice weightier.

**The man from the sea.** One foggy evening — "clouds were rising from the ocean... heavy fog from the cold waves" — Hana steps onto the veranda and puts her arm through his. Then both see "**something black come out of the mists**" — a man flung up out of the ocean, staggering, crawling, falling. Sadao guesses "a fisherman washed from his boat," but the sand beside the man bears "a stain of red soaking through." Turning his head, they see the face: "**A white man!**" — young (not yet twenty-five), **long yellow hair**, rough blond beard, unconscious. Sadao's expert fingers find a **gunshot wound on the right side of his lower back, reopened** — the flesh "blackened with powder" — a rock had struck it; he had been shot "not many days ago" and never treated. In the battered cap, faint lettering: "**U.S. Navy**." He is an **escaped prisoner of war** — shot in the back, which is itself telling (a fleeing man). Sadao packs the wound with **sea moss** to stop the bleeding.

**The dilemma — stated in examinable words.** Sadao reasons aloud: "**If we sheltered a white man in our house we should be arrested and if we turned him over as a prisoner, he would certainly die.**" The "kindest thing would be to put him back into the sea" — but neither of them moves. And then the heart of the story: "If the man were whole I could turn him over to the police without difficulty. **I care nothing for him. He is my enemy. All Americans are my enemy.** And he is only a common fellow... **But since he is wounded...**" Hana completes it: "You also cannot throw him back to the sea. Then there is only one thing to do. **We must carry him into the house.**" Publicly, they will tell everyone they intend to hand him over as a prisoner — "We must think of the children and your position." *Explanation:* watch how the doctor's identity operates — Sadao distinguishes between the man he hates and the wound he cannot abandon. The medical reflex is deeper than the political one. This passage is the answer to half the exam questions on this chapter.

**The servants revolt — society speaks.** They lay the man in the **father's old bedroom**, disused since his death — a room "entirely Japanese," for the old man "would never in his own home sit on a chair or sleep in a foreign bed." The three servants object, each in character: the **old gardener** (who made one of the finest moss gardens in Japan for Sadao's father) says bluntly: "The master ought not to heal the wound of this white man... **The white man ought to die.** First he was shot. Then the sea caught him and wounded him with her rocks. If the master heals what the gun did and what the sea did **they will take revenge on us**." The **cook** is contemptuous of saving "any life." **Yumi**, the children's nurse, refuses flatly: "**I have never washed a white man, and I will not wash so dirty a one now**... I am a poor person and it is not my business." Left alone with the man, **Hana's anger beats her fear**: "**Stupid Yumi... Is this anything but a man? And a wounded helpless man!**" — and she washes him herself, carefully, though she does not like him and dares not turn him over alone. *Explanation:* the servants are not villains — they voice ordinary society's fear, superstition and patriotism, and their defection will isolate the couple exactly when courage is needed. Hana's line is the moral pivot of her character.

**The operation — Buck's best-set scene.** Sadao sterilises his instruments on a towel laid in the **tokonoma alcove** (a footnote-worthy Japanese detail: the niche where art is displayed). Hana must give the anaesthetic: "Simply saturate the cotton and hold it near his nostrils... When he breathes badly move it away a little." Sadao's face is masked with the surgeon's light; he finds the bullet "**dangerously near the kidney**." Hana's face goes "the colour of sulphur"; she claps her hands to her mouth, runs into the garden and retches — "**Don't faint... If I stop now the man will surely die**" — but she **comes back** ("Where is the anesthetic?"). While operating, Sadao thinks "**there is no reason under heaven why he should live**," and the thought makes him "ruthless"; he mutters, "**Groan if you like... I am not doing this for my own pleasure. In fact, I do not know why I am doing it.**" Hana, holding the anaesthetic, thinks of **General Takima**, who "beat his wife cruelly" at home after his victorious battle in **Manchuria** — if such a man was cruel to a wife, what of a prisoner? She notices "**deep red scars on his neck, just under the ear**" and hopes the young man was not tortured. Sadao remembers his American professor of anatomy: "**Ignorance of the human body is the surgeon's cardinal sin, sirs!... anything less than that is murder.**" And he murmurs, in his professional habit, "**My friend**" — "forgetting that this was his enemy" — before "the cleanest and most precise of incisions" brings **the bullet out**. The prisoner mutters English ("They got... my guts..."); Sadao gives a hypodermic injection; the pulse steadies: "**This man will live in spite of all.**" *Explanation:* three details carry the chapter's meaning — (1) the body remembers torture even when the mouth can't speak (neck scars); (2) "My friend" slips out when the surgeon forgets to be a nationalist — habit reveals belief; (3) the couple's teamwork, Hana's nausea overcome by duty, mirrors their marriage and their joint moral courage.

**The household crisis — day by day.** The prisoner wakes; Hana herself feeds him from a porcelain spoon ("How come... you speak English..." — "**I was a long time in America**"). The servants decide the couple — "so long in America" — have "forgotten to think of our own country first... They think we like Americans." Yumi says it is the **children** to think of: "What will be their fate if their father is condemned as a **traitor**?" Meanwhile Sadao **types a letter to the Chief of police** — "On the twenty-first day of February an escaped prisoner was washed up on the shore in front of my house" — and then puts the **unfinished report into a secret drawer**. *Explanation:* the unsent letter is the story's most precise symbol — Sadao's two selves in one act: the patriot who types, the doctor who does not send. On the **seventh day** the three servants leave together, crying; Yumi even runs back to say, "If the baby misses me too much tonight, send for me." Hana pays them off "with dignity," then does all the housework herself — cooking, cleaning, nursing. The prisoner, unasked, offers his name: "**Tom**"; Hana only bows "her little distant bow" — she is kind, not fond.

**The General — power, sickness and cynicism.** Sadao is summoned to the **old General**, who trusts no one else's knife: "The best ones have been **trained by Germans** and would consider the operation successful **even if I died**. I do not care for their point of view." He jokes that it is "a pity we cannot better combine **German ruthlessness with American sentimentality**" — "As a Japanese, could you not combine these two foreign elements?" Then, "irritably," the plan: "**It would be best if he could be quietly killed** — not by you, but by someone who does not know him. **I have my own private assassins.**" They will come "any night" through the open partition; they are "very capable assassins — **they make no noise and they know the trick of inward bleeding**"; they can even remove the body. Sadao agrees, reflecting that "such persons were essential in an **absolute state** such as Japan was. How else could rulers deal with those who opposed them?" *Explanation:* the satire has three blades — the dictator who fears his own doctors, the state that solves problems by secret murder, and the irony that Sadao's safety under the regime depends on his usefulness to a sick tyrant.

**Three sleepless nights — and the state's silence.** Sadao tells Hana nothing ("she would be timid at the idea of assassins in the house") and waits. "Time and time again he woke, thinking he heard the rustling of footsteps... a noise such as men might make who carried a burden." The assassins **never come**. Meanwhile Tom, recovering, tells him warmly: "**If I hadn't met a Jap like you — well, I wouldn't be alive today... I guess if all the Japs were like you there wouldn't have been a war.**" Sadao "could not speak." *Explanation:* the soldier's gratitude completes Buck's argument — the "enemy" is a boy like any boy; wars are made by states, not by wounded sailors.

**Sadao's solution — doctor and patriot in one plan.** Having waited in vain for the state to act, Sadao acts: he puts his own **boat** on the shore with **food and bottled water**, and instructs Tom: row to the little island, wait for a **Korean fishing boat** — "the fishermen use it as a haven"; signal **two flashes at sunset** if food runs out ("Do not signal in darkness, for it will be seen"), **one flash** if all is well but still there; eat **raw fish** ("a fire would be seen"). He hesitated, then gave Tom **his own small flashlight**. Tom is dressed in **Japanese clothes**; at the last moment Sadao wraps a **black cloth about the blond head**; the young man shakes his hand warmly and rows into the dark. That night, for the first time in a week, "**he slept.**" *Explanation:* notice the plan's genius — it saves the prisoner AND protects Japan (no prisoner-of-war found in his house), AND lets Sadao keep his oath. It is the one solution that serves all three of his loyalties: doctor, citizen, family man. He also tells Tom, characteristically: "I realise you are saving my life again" — "Not at all. **It is only inconvenient to have you here any longer**" — the stiff courtesy that hides kindness.

**The General's confession — the final irony.** A week later (the General has survived an **emergency operation** at which Sadao worked through the night — "the gall bladder was much involved"), Sadao reports the prisoner "escaped." The General wakes with energy: "**That prisoner... did I not promise you I would kill him for you?... The truth is, I thought of nothing but myself. In short, I forgot my promise.**" He insists it "was not lack of patriotism or dereliction of duty," and asks anxiously that if the matter comes out, Sadao will understand. "Sadao suddenly comprehended that **the General was in the palm of his hand** and that as a consequence **he himself was perfectly safe**": "I can swear to your loyalty, Excellency... You will be rewarded." *Explanation:* the assassin-state is saved by a doctor's discretion; the regime's moral cowardice — not justice, not mercy — is what protects Sadao. Buck's verdict on power is in that exchange.

**The last image.** That night Sadao searches the twilight sea: "**There was no prick of light in the dusk. No one was on the island. His prisoner was gone — safe.**" And into his mind, "although without reason," come other **white faces** — Professor Harley ("a dull man"), his silly talkative wife who wished to be kind, the old teacher of anatomy who thundered about the cardinal sin — the Americans who had been good to him. He stands on the veranda, gazing out to the sea "from whence the young man had come." *Explanation:* the story closes holding both truths at once — Sadao remains a patriot (he was relieved to serve Japan and safe in its good graces), and yet his hands could not kill and his memory returns to kindness. Humanity and nationality coexist in one man; the title "The Enemy" is finally answered by a question — enemy of whom?

## 👥 CHARACTERS — full profiles
**Dr Sadao Hoki** — brilliant Japanese surgeon-scientist; dutiful son of a nationalist father; trained in America yet a patriot who "would not think of doing anything else" than hand over the prisoner — until the oath of his profession proves stronger than race hatred. He is also **shrewd and cool under pressure**: he never confesses, never defies the state openly; he waits, uses the General's selfishness, and engineers a solution that is merciful, safe and legal-in-effect. A study in **quiet moral courage plus intelligence** — which is why the story is satisfying rather than sentimental.
**Hana** — Sadao's wife; begins fearful and conventional (insisting they promise to hand the man over, telling the servants the same); discovers her own steel: washes the white man when Yumi refuses, holds the anaesthetic through nausea, runs the whole house alone after day seven, and faces the servants' parting with "pride as a mistress." Her creed: "**Is this anything but a man? And a wounded helpless man!**"
**The General** — the story's satirical target: a sick, self-absorbed autocrat who trusts no German-trained surgeon, keeps "private assassins," plans secret murder — and then simply **forgets** ("I thought of nothing but myself"). His fear of exposure puts him "in the palm of" Sadao's hand. Buck's portrait of power in wartime: ruthless in theory, self-serving in practice.
**Tom, the prisoner** — a young American sailor, "barely twenty-five," shot in the back, tortured (red scars on his neck), yet cheerful and generous — "if all the Japs were like you there wouldn't have been a war." He is the human face of "the enemy."
**The servants (gardener, cook, Yumi)** — ordinary society's chorus: superstition, contempt, and fear for the children; they leave on day seven, leaving the moral burden entirely with the couple — and return unremarked when the danger passes ("Only the gardener was cross because he had got behind with his chrysanthemums" — the comedy of ordinary life resuming).

## 🧩 THEMES
1. **Duty as a doctor vs duty as a patriot** — the oath to save life against the law of wartime Japan; the scalpel wins, but through cunning rather than rebellion.
2. **Humanity above hatred** — wounds have no nationality; "Is this anything but a man?"; the enemy has a name (Tom) and a grin.
3. **Racial prejudice and its cracks** — the father's obsession with racial purity, Hana's "pure in her race" marriage, the servants' horror of a white man — set against the memory of kind Americans and the young sailor's gratitude.
4. **The self-absorption of power** — the General's private assassins, his fear of his own surgeons, his convenient amnesia: Buck's satire of the "absolute state."
5. **Fear of society vs conscience** — the servants' flight shows how communities enforce conformity; moral choice is lonely and domestic (Hana doing the cooking).
6. **Moral intelligence** — the ending refuses to make Sadao a martyr or a traitor: he saves everyone — the prisoner, his family, his country's face — which is the story's maturest idea.

## ❓ QUESTIONS WITH FULL MODEL ANSWERS
**Q1. Who was Dr Sadao? Where was his house? (NCERT)**
A: Dr Sadao Hoki was a famous Japanese surgeon and scientist, sent to America at twenty-two and back home at thirty, kept out of the war because he was perfecting a discovery to render wounds entirely clean and because the old General might need an operation. His house was a low, square stone house on the Japanese coast, set on rocks above a narrow beach outlined with bent pines — the very spot where he had played as a boy, and precisely the beach on which the wounded American prisoner of war would be washed up one foggy evening.

**Q2. Will Dr Sadao be arrested on the charge of harbouring an enemy? (NCERT)**
A: No — and the story shows why at every step. He never completes or sends the letter to the Chief of police; the servants who knew of the prisoner leave on the seventh day; the General, who alone could act, plans secret assassination and then forgets his own promise ("I thought of nothing but myself"); and after the emergency operation the General is so deep in Sadao's debt — and so fearful of exposure — that Sadao holds him "in the palm of his hand." The state's cowardice and the couple's discretion together place Sadao beyond arrest.

**Q3. How would you explain the reluctance of the soldier to leave the shelter of the doctor's home? (NCERT)**
A: Tom's reluctance is human, not cowardly. He is a hunted escaper: the beach is watched, his wound still fresh, and the only place in Japan where he has been fed, washed, operated on and protected is the doctor's house. He owes Sadao his life twice — "I realise you are saving my life again" — and knows that moving too early could kill him and ruin his saviour. His famous line, "If all the Japs were like you there wouldn't have been a war," explains the rest: he has found the one enemy whose humanity he trusts, and leaving it means returning to a world at war with him.

**Q4. What explains the attitude of the General in the matter of the enemy soldier? Was it human consideration, lack of national loyalty, dereliction of duty or simply self-absorption? (NCERT)**
A: Purely self-absorption. The General proposes secret murder by his "private assassins" — so neither humanity nor legal scruple restrains him — and then forgets the entire affair because "I was suffering a good deal... I thought of nothing but myself." His anxiety is not for the state but for his own name: "it was not lack of patriotism or dereliction of duty," he pleads, begging the doctor's silence. A man who fears his German-trained surgeons, needs Sadao's knife, and dodges every consequence shows no loyalty and no cruelty either — only a sick tyrant's total preoccupation with himself.

**Q5. While hatred against a member of the enemy race is justifiable in wartime, what makes a human being rise above narrow prejudices? (NCERT)**
A: In the story, three forces lift Sadao and Hana above prejudice. First, professional ethics: Sadao has been trained that to operate without complete knowledge of the body "is murder" — a doctor's instinct that outranks politics ("But since he is wounded..."). Second, the sight of helplessness: Hana's "Is this anything but a man? And a wounded helpless man!" — misery dissolves abstractions. Third, personal memory: Sadao's own years of American kindness (Professor Harley, the anatomy teacher) surface at the end, and Tom's gratitude ("if all the Japs were like you there wouldn't have been a war") completes the reversal. Shared humanity, once actually seen, proves stronger than slogans.

**Q6. Do you think the doctor's final solution was the best out of the difficult alternatives? (NCERT)**
A: Yes — it is the only solution that satisfied every obligation at once. Handing the prisoner over meant certain death and a broken oath; keeping him meant treason for Sadao and danger to Hana and the children; killing him was impossible for a healer. Sadao's island plan — boat, food, flashlight, the signal code (two flashes if food runs out, one if safe), raw fish, Korean fishing boat — saved the prisoner without handing him to either the Japanese police or the sea, protected the family from exposure, and left the doctor's record clean. It is typical of Sadao that mercy arrives not as a burst of heroism but as a perfectly planned operation.

**Q7. How does the story expose the hollowness of racial distinctions?**
A: Every racial boundary in the story leaks. The father's creed of racial purity is undercut by his son's debt to American teachers; the servants' horror of a "white man" is contradicted by Hana's washing and the couple's nursing; the prisoner's "foolish face" becomes a grateful boy who says the war need never have happened; the newspapers' tales of joyful liberation are contradicted by the torture scars on Tom's neck; and the state that calls the American "enemy" saves itself through a Japanese doctor's silence about its own forgotten assassins. Blood, hatred and flags divide people; wounds, skill and kindness recognise each other — that is Buck's verdict.
