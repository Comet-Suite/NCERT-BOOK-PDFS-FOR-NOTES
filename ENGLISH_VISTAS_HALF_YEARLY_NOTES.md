# ✍️ 1. ON THE FACE OF IT — Susan Hill (a PLAY, 3 scenes)

## 🎬 What kind of text is this?
This is a **play** — the only dramatic text in your Vistas syllabus — so answers should mention **scenes, dialogue, stage directions and characters speaking**. The playwright is **Susan Hill**, the English novelist famous for *I'm the King of the Castle* and *The Woman in Black*. The play has **three scenes**: Scene One and Scene Three are set in **Mr Lamb's garden**, and Scene Two in **Derry's house**.

## 📖 THE FULL STORY, scene by scene

### Scene One — The garden meeting
It is a **beautiful September day**. **Derry**, a **fourteen-year-old boy**, walks slowly and tentatively through the long grass of a garden he believes is empty — he has **climbed over the garden wall** because he thought no one was there. Suddenly an old man's voice says, "**Mind the apples!**" and Derry is startled and panics. The voice belongs to **Mr Lamb**, who immediately reassures him: the gate is always open, "I don't mind who comes into the garden... **All welcome. You're welcome.**" Derry is defensive — he says he hasn't come to steal anything — and then blurts out the truth of his life: "**People are afraid of me.**"

Derry explains what he means: everyone who sees him thinks, "**That's bad. That's a terrible thing. That's the ugliest thing I ever saw... Poor boy.**" He says people are afraid underneath, and — the most painful line — "**When I look in the mirror, and see it, I'm afraid of me.**" Mr Lamb calmly disagrees: "Not the whole of you. Not of you."

Mr Lamb then deliberately does what nobody else does: he **changes the subject to the crab apples** he is going to pick and turn into **jelly** ("orange and golden... that's magic fruit"), and asks Derry to **give him a hand**. Derry is almost offended: "**What have you changed the subject for? People always do that... pretend it isn't true and isn't there... I'll tell you — you don't ask me because you're afraid to.**" Mr Lamb replies that if Derry wants to be asked, he should just say so. This is the turning point of the play: Derry finally gets to speak about his face directly.

**Derry's story:** he was **not burned in a fire** — "**I got acid all down that side of my face and it burned it all away. It ate my face up. It ate me up. And now it's like this and it won't ever be any different.**" His own assessment of himself is total despair.

**Mr Lamb's counter-philosophy:** he tells Derry that he is **not as badly off as he thinks** — he still has **two eyes, two ears, two hands and legs and a tongue and a brain**, and "**the world's there to be looked at**." He insists **beauty is relative** — everything is "all relative," and this old garden, for him, **is the world**: "Not the only one. But the world, as much as anywhere." When Derry asks whether his tin leg hurts, Mr Lamb answers with a shrug: "**Tin doesn't hurt, boy!**" (it hurt when it came off, and aches in wet weather — but "it doesn't signify").

Derry pours out the **cruel things people say**: a woman at a **bus stop** whispered to her friend, "**Look at that, that's a terrible thing. That's a face only a mother could love.**" And children at the fair (his mother told him) said: if he went near a bonfire he would be **"a fire-eater, a freak with his face burnt off"** — they said he should **go home and stay in hospital**, he'd "**be better off with others like himself**." Mr Lamb quietly shows him the absurdity of hearing only one kind of voice: "**So now you keep your ears shut.**" His own ears are open — he tells Derry about his **hive of bees** behind the trees: "Some hear bees and they say, bees buzz. But when you listen to bees for a long while, they **humm**... and **hum means 'sing'. I hear them singing, my bees.**"

**The locked-room parable:** Mr Lamb tells of a man who was afraid of **everything** — a bus might run him over, a man might breathe deadly germs on him, a donkey might kick him, lightning might strike him, he might love a girl and be left, he might slip on a banana skin and be laughed at. So he **locked himself in his room** and stayed in bed. Derry asks, "For ever?" — "For a while." "Then what?" — "**A picture fell off the wall on to his head and killed him.**" Derry laughs a lot — and the moral lands: you cannot escape life by hiding from it; danger finds you anywhere.

Derry confesses he **likes it here** — he came in because he liked the look of it over the wall — but admits that if he had **seen Mr Lamb first, he'd not have come in**. Mr Lambert describes his own life: he sits in the sun, reads books — "**you thought it was an empty house, but inside, it's full. Books and other things. Full.**" He has **no curtains** because he is "**not fond of curtains. Shutting things out, shutting things in. I like the light and the darkness, and the windows open, to hear the wind.**"

**Their two philosophies clash and merge.** Derry wants to shut the world out; his mother thinks he should be with "people like himself." Mr Lamb destroys that logic with one question — if everyone segregated themselves, "**what kind of a world would that be?**" — and gives his creed: "**Everything's different. Everything's the same, but everything is different. Itself.**" He got that way by "**watching, listening, thinking.**" He tells Derry his house is open: "**The gate's always open. Everything's yours if you want it. What's mine is anybody's.**" When Derry worries that other visitors would mind him, Mr Lamb says, "**You'd have to take the risk. So would they.**" He shares his own stigma without self-pity: when he goes down the street the kids shout "**Lamey-Lamb**" — but they still come into his garden; "it's a game. **They're not afraid of me. Why should they be? Because I'm not afraid of them, that's why not.**"

Derry's mother will want to know where he is — it is **three miles home across the fields**. Mr Lamb sends him off gently ("It's a fine evening. **You've got legs.**") and plants the seed of self-determination: "Once you got home, they'd never let me come back" — "**Once you got home, you'd never let yourself come back.**" Only Derry knows what Derry could do. Derry admits the deepest truth of his character: "**I don't know what I want. I want... something no one else has got or ever will have. Something just mine. Like this garden.**" Mr Lamb: "**You could find out... Waiting. Watching. Listening.**"

As Derry leaves, Mr Lamb speaks **to himself** — and for the first time his own loneliness shows: "**There my dears. That's you seen to. Ah... you know. We all know. I'll come back. They never do, though. Not them. Never do come back.**"

### Scene Two — Derry's house
Derry's **mother forbids** him from returning: "**You think I don't know about him... Been told. Warned. We've not lived here three months, but I know what there is to know and you're not to go back there.**" Derry defends Mr Lamb with the exact mixture of outrage and new-born clarity that Mr Lamb has taught him: "**What are you afraid of? What do you think he is? An old man with a tin leg and he lives in a huge house without curtains and has a garden. And I want to be there**" — and he defies her. He runs the **three miles across the fields**, panting, saying to himself: "**And I want the world... I want it... I want it.**" The boy who hated being seen is now running *toward* another person. This scene exists to show that Derry has begun making **his own choices** against over-protection and gossip.

### Scene Three — The return and the ending
In the garden, **Mr Lamb is on a ladder picking crab apples** ("Steady... that's... got it"). There is a **creak, a crash — the ladder falls back, Mr Lamb with it. A thump. Then silence.** Derry bursts through the gate, still panting, triumphantly: "**You see, you see! I came back. You said I wouldn't and they said... but I came back, I wanted...**" — and then he stops dead. He runs through the grass, kneels: "**Mr Lamb, It's all right... You fell... I'm here, Mr Lamb, It's all right... I came back. Lamey-Lamb. I did... come back.**" **[He begins to weep.]** The end.

**How to read the ending in an answer:** Mr Lamb's death is **implied, not stated** — he falls from the ladder while doing what he loved, alone in the garden, exactly the fate he had joked about ("If you fell and broke your neck, you could lie on the grass and die. If you were on your own."). But the play does NOT end in despair: Derry **came back** — the fear-bound boy has been transformed. Mr Lamb's gift (courage, openness, self-belief) survives him. In a long answer, say the ending is **poignant and ironic yet affirmative**: the teacher dies, but his teaching lives in the student.

## 👥 CHARACTER SKETCHES (ready to lift into answers)

**Derry (Derek) — the burned boy:** fourteen; one side of his face destroyed by **acid**; bitterly conscious that people stare, whisper and fear him; he has internalised their cruelty so deeply that he fears his own reflection; he is **defensive, withdrawn, angry and suspicious of pity**; he keeps people at arm's length because he cannot bear their reactions; yet underneath he is **starving for affection and for a life of his own** ("I want... something just mine"). His **transformation** — from a boy who hid from the world to a boy who runs across three miles of fields because he "wants the world" — is the emotional spine of the play.

**Mr Lamb — the tin-legged optimist:** an **old man who lost a leg in the war** and lives alone in a big house **without curtains**, with a garden full of **crab apples, weeds and singing bees**. He is **physically impaired like Derry but spiritually free**; his philosophy is acceptance and engagement: the gate is always open, everything is shared, weeds are only plants in the wrong place (nature makes no mistakes), and no one need be feared **because he fears no one**. He is **kind without being sentimental** — he asks Derry about his face directly, refuses to treat him as tragic, and uses humour (the locked-room story) to cure self-pity. Beneath the cheer he is **profoundly lonely** — "They never do... come back" — which makes his generosity greater, not smaller. He is the **catalyst** of the play: in one afternoon he gives Derry a new way of seeing, and he dies as he lived — reaching for the "magic fruit."

## 💬 QUOTES THAT ANSWER QUESTIONS
- "**Mind the apples!**" — the opening line; Mr Lamb reaches out to a stranger naturally, without staring at his face.
- "**People are afraid of me... When I look in the mirror... I'm afraid of me.**" — Derry's self-hatred.
- "**That's a face only a mother could love.**" — casual cruelty of strangers.
- "**It ate my face up. It ate me up.**" — the acid destroyed both face and identity.
- "**Tin doesn't hurt, boy!**" — Mr Lamb's cheerful acceptance of his disability.
- "**The gate's always open.**" — Mr Lamb's whole philosophy in four words.
- "**Hum means 'sing'. I hear them singing, my bees.**" — attitude turns noise into music.
- "**A picture fell off the wall on to his head and killed him.**" — hiding from life doesn't save you.
- "**Everything's the same, but everything is different. Itself.**" — no two people (or weeds, or faces) are alike; comparison is meaningless.
- "**They're not afraid of me. Why should they be? Because I'm not afraid of them, that's why not.**" — fear is a choice.
- "**Once you got home, you'd never let yourself come back.**" — other people's limits only bind you if you accept them.
- "**They never do, though. Not them. Never do come back.**" — Mr Lamb's loneliness.
- "**I came back. Lamey-Lamb. I did... come back.**" — Derry's transformation completed; the nickname is now affection, not insult.

## 🧩 THEMES (each one is a probable long question)
1. **Physical disability versus inner strength** — Derry's face and Mr Lamb's leg matter less than what each man does with his remaining life; the "actual pain" of impairment is smaller than the **alienation** society adds to it.
2. **Appearance versus reality ("on the face of it")** — Derry's face makes people assume tragedy; Mr Lamb's cheerful garden hides loneliness; the title itself warns against judging by surfaces.
3. **Loneliness and companionship** — two isolated people heal each other in a single afternoon; human connection, not medicine, is the real cure.
4. **Positive attitude as a choice** — same world, two ways of hearing it: "bees buzz" versus "bees sing."
5. **Over-protection and stigma** — Derry's mother and the fair-women mean well, but their advice ("stay with your own kind") **imprisons** him; Mr Lamb's openness liberates him.
6. **Death and continuity** — Mr Lamb dies, but his outlook is inherited; a good influence outlives the person.

## 📚 WORD MEANINGS (extract-based MCQs love these)
crab apples (small, sour wild apples) • windfalls (fruit blown down by wind) • scrump (steal fruit from orchards) • tentatively (hesitantly) • daft (mad, foolish) • dribble (saliva running from mouth) • peculiar (strange) • hive (bee-box) • stubble (short beard growth) • gaunt (thin, hollow-cheeked) • panting (breathing fast) • haul (pull heavily) • bonfire (outdoor fire) • segregation by "your own kind" (separating the disabled from normal society).

## ❓ PROBABLE QUESTIONS WITH VALUE POINTS
1. **What draws Derry to Mr Lamb in spite of himself?** — Mr Lamb is the first person who neither stares nor pretends; he talks to Derry as an equal, asks about his face openly, jokes with him, treats him as able-bodied, shares his own disability without shame, and offers welcome without pity ("the gate's always open"); Derry, who has only received fear and fake sympathy, is drawn to this fearless, normal treatment.
2. **Where does Mr Lamb show loneliness and disappointment? How does he overcome it?** — Scene One end ("They never do come back") and his confession that people's visits are irregular; he overcomes it by keeping busy (apples, jelly, bees, books), keeping the gate open, welcoming strangers, and loving company of any kind.
3. **Why does Derry's mother object to his going back? Is she right?** — she has heard gossip and warnings about the old man; her motive is protective love, but her information is hearsay and her protectiveness would lock Derry out of life; the play shows over-protection breeds more disability than acid does.
4. **Will Derry return to seclusion or change?** — he will change: he defied his mother, ran back, said "I want the world," and even Mr Lamb's death cannot undo the new confidence; grief may pause him, but he now knows another way of living is possible.
5. **What is the significance of the title?** — "On the face of it" means judging by outward appearance: society judges Derry's face, but the play reveals what is beneath it (a frightened, loving boy); Mr Lamb appears a harmless eccentric but is a philosopher; both are more, and other, than their faces and legs suggest.
6. **Comment on the ending.** — ironic and sad (the mentor dies alone doing an ordinary chore), yet affirmative (Derry returns transformed; his tears are for Mr Lamb but his running is toward life); the lesson outlives the teacher.

# ✍️ 2. THE THIRD LEVEL — Jack Finney

## 🎬 What kind of text is this?
A **first-person fantasy story with a psychological explanation built in** — written by **Jack Finney**, the American writer famous for science fiction about time (*Time and Again*). The story deliberately **blurs fantasy and reality** so that the reader, like Charley, cannot be sure what is real. Every exam question will test whether you can argue BOTH sides.

## 📖 THE FULL STORY IN DETAIL
The narrator, **Charley**, a **thirty-one-year-old ordinary office worker** (tan gabardine suit, straw hat with a fancy band, married to **Louisa**), makes an outrageous claim: the **Grand Central Station in New York has a third level** that the **New York, New Haven and Hartford railroads** would deny on "a stack of timetables" — but Charley has been there.

**The psychiatrist's explanation comes first.** Charley consulted a **psychiatrist friend, Sam Weiner**, who diagnosed the third level as "**waking-dream wish fulfillment**": the modern world is "full of **insecurity, fear, war, worry**," and Charley simply **wants to escape**. Charley's friends agree, and they add that his **stamp collecting** is a "**temporary refuge from reality**." Charley half-protests: his **grandfather**, who lived in peaceful times and needed no refuge, **started the collection** — and anyway, "**President Roosevelt collected stamps too.**" (Note the narrator's technique: he keeps offering "evidence" that makes him sound MORE escapist.)

**How Grand Central breeds the fantasy:** Charley describes the station as if it were alive — "**growing like a tree, pushing out new corridors and staircases like roots**." He has wandered into a **mile-long tunnel** and emerged in the **lobby of the Roosevelt Hotel**, another time into an office building on **Forty-sixth Street, three blocks away**. He privately imagines unknown tunnels feeling their way toward **Times Square and Central Park** — and he notes that for millions of people Grand Central "**has been an exit, a way of escape**" — an idea he never dared tell the psychiatrist.

**The experience itself:** one night last summer Charley **worked late**, wanted to get home quickly, and took the **subway from Grand Central** (faster than the bus). He went down to the **first level** (where trains like the **Twentieth Century** leave), then to the **second level** (suburban trains), then **ducked into an arched doorway heading for the subway — and got lost**. The corridor **angled left and slanted downward**; he heard only his own footsteps, then a "hollow roar" of open space, went down a short flight of stairs — **and came out on the third level**.

**The 1894 details (memorise this cluster):** the room is **smaller**, with **fewer ticket windows and train gates**; the **information booth is wooden and old**; the man in the booth wears a **green eyeshade and long black sleeve protectors**; the **lights are dim, flickering, open-flame gaslights**; there are **brass spittoons** on the floor; a man pulls a **gold watch** from his vest pocket — **derby hat, black four-button suit with tiny lapels, big black handlebar mustache**; everyone is dressed like "**eighteen-ninety-something**" — beards, sideburns, fancy mustaches; a woman wears a dress with **leg-of-mutton sleeves** and skirts down to her **high-buttoned shoes**; on the tracks stands a **small Currier & Ives locomotive with a funnel-shaped stack**. The newspaper at the newsboy's feet is **The World** — which "hasn't been published for years" — with a lead story about **President Cleveland**; Charley later found that exact front page in the **Public Library files**, printed **June 11, 1894**.

**Why Galesburg matters:** at the ticket window Charley realises he can buy tickets "that would take Louisa and me **anywhere in the United States**... **In the year 1894**," and he asks for **two tickets to Galesburg, Illinois** — his school town, "a wonderful town still, with big old frame houses, huge lawns, and tremendous trees whose branches meet overhead and roof the streets." In 1894, "**summer evenings were twice as long**," people sat on lawns — **men smoking cigars, women waving palm-leaf fans, fireflies all around** — "in a peaceful world. **To be back there with the First World War still twenty years off, and World War II over forty years in the future... I wanted two tickets for that.**" (This sentence is the thesis of the story: the third level = escape from the wars of the twentieth century.)

**The money failure:** the clerk figures the fare — glancing at the fancy hatband but figuring it anyway — but when Charley counts out his money the clerk snaps, "**That ain't money, mister, and if you're trying to skin me, you won't get very far.**" Charley's bills are modern; 1894 notes were "**old-style bills, half again as big**." Fearing jail ("**There's nothing nice about jail, even in 1894**"), he flees.

**The obsessive aftermath:** next day at lunch he **withdrew three hundred dollars — nearly all they had — and bought old-style currency** from a coin dealer (you must pay a premium; his $300 bought **less than $200 in 1894 money**; he didn't care — "**eggs were thirteen cents a dozen in 1894**"). But he **never found the corridor again**, however often he tried. **Louisa was worried** and made him stop looking; he went back to his stamps.

**The proof — Sam's disappearance and the first-day cover:** Charley and Louisa are now looking every weekend, "because now we have **proof**": his friend **Sam Weiner** (the very psychiatrist who called it wish-fulfillment) has **disappeared**. Charley "sort of suspected" where — Sam was a **city boy** who loved the sound of Galesburg. Then, one night, fussing with his stamp collection, Charley found among his **oldest first-day covers** (envelopes mailed to yourself on the first day of a stamp's issue, postmarked to prove the date, containing only blank paper, never opened) one "**that shouldn't have been there**": addressed to his **grandfather at his home in Galesburg**, postmarked **July 18, 1894**, bearing a **six-cent dull brown stamp with President Garfield**. Inside, instead of blank paper, was a **letter from Sam**.

**The letter's content:** Sam writes that he is **in Galesburg in 1894** — he found the third level, bought his ticket, and is now settled there running a **hay, feed and grain business**, which "he always said that's what he really wished he could do." The delicious closing irony: "His old business? **Why, Sam was my psychiatrist.**" The man who diagnosed the third level as fantasy has himself emigrated into it. The story ends with Charley and Louisa still searching — the **open ending** that lets you argue either interpretation.

## 👥 CHARACTERS
**Charley** — an ordinary, likeable, hard-working New Yorker; imaginative but not a dreamer by profession; his "evidence" always undercuts him, which makes him believable; he represents **every modern man's quiet wish to escape** to a simpler, safer past.
**Louisa** — Charley's caring wife; first alarmed, forbids the search, then joins it — representing how the wish for peace is shared at home.
**Sam Weiner** — Charley's **psychiatrist friend**; the rational voice who explains the third level away — and then vanishes into it; the story's **central irony**.
**The grandfather** — started the stamp collection in peaceful times; his memory is Charley's shield against the "refuge from reality" charge.

## 🧩 THEMES
1. **Escapism** — the third level is the classic reading: a wish-fulfilment refuge from a world of **insecurity, fear, war and worry**; stamp collecting, first-day covers and Galesburg are all refuges.
2. **The intersection of time and space** — one physical place (Grand Central) opens into another time (1894); Finney makes time travel feel homely and suburban rather than scientific.
3. **Fantasy versus reality** — the story supplies psychological explanations AND physical "proofs" (the June 11, 1894 front page, the postmarked cover, Sam's disappearance) and refuses to choose; the ambiguity IS the art.
4. **Nostalgia for a pre-war world** — Galesburg 1894 stands for peace, community, slowness, fireflies and cheap eggs.
5. **Philately as a bridge across time** — stamps and first-day covers are literally time-travel devices in the plot.

## 📚 WORD MEANINGS
waking-dream wish fulfillment (psychoanalytic idea: a daydream that satisfies an unconscious wish) • gabardine (a worsted fabric; a suit of it) • eyeshade (accountant's green visor) • spittoon (vessel for spitting) • derby hat (round felt hat) • leg-of-mutton sleeves (puffed shoulders, tight at wrist) • Currier & Ives (famous 19th-century American print firm — the locomotive looks like one of their pictures) • premium (extra price above face value) • philately (stamp collecting) • first-day cover (envelope postmarked on a stamp's first day of sale).

## ❓ PROBABLE QUESTIONS WITH VALUE POINTS
1. **What does the third level refer to?** — literally, a mysterious third railway level below Grand Central that officially does not exist; symbolically, **any imaginative escape** from the pressures of the modern world; psychologically (Sam's reading), a waking dream of Charley's.
2. **Do you think the third level was a medium of escape for Charley? Why?** — yes by the psychiatrist's reading: the modern world is full of insecurity, fear, war and worry; everything points that way (stamps, Galesburg's pre-war peace, the wish for long summer evenings); BUT the story plants "proof" (the front page, the cover, Sam) so a full answer should hold both readings and call the story deliberately ambiguous.
3. **What do you infer from Sam's letter?** — that the fantasy has "swallowed" the realist; the psychiatrist who called it wish-fulfilment now lives in Galesburg 1894; either the third level is real or Charley invented the letter too — inference: the human need for peace is stronger than psychiatric labels.
4. **Is there an intersection of time and space?** — yes: one doorway in space (an arched corridor off the second level) crosses into another time (1894); the old currency, gaslights and newspaper confirm a different era in the same station.
5. **"The modern world is full of insecurity, fear, war, worry" — how do people try to overcome these?** — hobbies (like stamp collecting), nostalgia, religion, holidays, films and fiction, meditation — and in the story, fantasy time-travel; connect to your own examples.
6. **How does Finney interweave fantasy and reality?** — realistic frame (office, wife, psychiatrist, subway) + impossible core + "documentary" proofs (library file, postmark) + open ending; compare with **'Adventure' by Jayant Narlikar** (Hornbill XI) where the historian Gangadharpant travels to an alternate India — both make the impossible feel plausible, but Narlikar's is science-fictional (cataclysm theory) while Finney's is psychological.

**🧠 Memory hooks:** "**31-1894-Galesburg-Sam**" (age, year, destination, psychiatrist). Station levels: "**1 Twentieth Century, 2 suburban, 3 escape.**" Proof pack: "**Page-Cover-Stamp-Sam**" (June 11 front page, first-day cover, Garfield six-cent, Sam gone). Irony line: "**The doctor prescribed escape — then took it himself.**"

# ✍️ 3. MEMORIES OF CHILDHOOD — Zitkala-Sa and Bama (two autobiographical episodes)

## 🎬 What kind of text is this?
**Two autobiographical extracts** from the childhoods of two women from **marginalised communities**, written in two distant cultures but bonded by one theme: **the loss and reassertion of human dignity**.
**Zitkala-Sa** is the pen name of **Gertrude Simmons Bonnin** (born **1876**), an extraordinarily talented and educated **Native American (American Indian) woman** who struggled against severe prejudice towards Native American culture and women; from **1900** she published articles **criticising the Carlisle Indian School**; her life was "dedicated against the evils of oppression."
**Bama** is the pen name of a **Tamil Dalit woman from a Roman Catholic family**; her three main works are the autobiography **'Karukku' (1992)** — from which this extract is taken — the novel **'Sangati' (1994)** and the short-story collection **'Kisumbukkaaran' (1996)**. '**Karukku**' means **palmyra leaves**, whose **serrated edges on both sides are like double-edged swords**; by a felicitous pun, since the word contains '**karu**' (embryo, seed), it also suggests **freshness and newness**.

## 📖 PART I — THE CUTTING OF MY LONG HAIR (Zitkala-Sa)

**The first day in the "land of apples"** is **bitter-cold** — snow on the ground, bare trees. A **large bell** rings for breakfast, its "loud metallic voice crashing through the belfry" into "sensitive ears"; the clatter of shoes and the murmur of an **unknown tongue** create a "bedlam" in which the child feels "**securely tied**" — "though my spirit tore itself in struggling for its lost freedom, all was useless." A **paleface woman with white hair** supervises; the Indian girls march in **stiff shoes and closely clinging dresses**, the little ones in **sleeved aprons and shingled hair**. Zitkala-Sa walks **noiselessly in her soft moccasins**, feels like sinking into the floor, because her **blanket has been stripped from her shoulders**. She notices the three young **braves** of her party at the rear, "looking as uncomfortable as I felt."

**The three-bell humiliation:** a **small bell** is tapped and every pupil draws out a chair — she, thinking this means "sit," slips into hers, and discovers she is **the only one seated**. A **second bell** — everyone sits, and she has "to crawl back" into her chair. A man's voice murmurs (grace); everyone hangs their heads; a **paleface woman's eyes** catch hers watching; a **third bell** — all eat. "**I began crying instead**, for by this time I was afraid to venture anything more." The scene is a perfect miniature of **cultural unknownness**: her body reads the wrong cues because the rules are alien.

**The hair:** "This eating by formula was not the hardest trial." Her friend **Judewin**, who "knew a few words of English," overhears the paleface woman talking of **cutting their long, heavy hair**. The cultural stakes are explicit: "**only unskilled warriors who were captured had their hair shingled by the enemy**"; among her people, **short hair was worn by mourners, and shingled hair by cowards**. Judewin counsels submission — "**We have to submit, because they are strong**" — but the child answers with the episode's most famous line: "**No, I will not submit! I will struggle first!**"

**The struggle and defeat:** she slips away, creeps upstairs in squeaking shoes (her moccasins have been exchanged for shoes), finds a dim room with **three white beds** and **dark green curtains**, and **crawls under the bed** into the farthest corner. Voices call her name; she does not answer; the searchers enter, throw up the curtains, peer behind trunks — and somehow look under the bed. She is **dragged out, kicking and scratching wildly**, carried downstairs and **tied fast in a chair**. "I cried aloud, shaking my head all the while until I felt the **cold blades of the scissors against my neck**, and heard them **gnaw off one of my thick braids**. Then **I lost my spirit**." The summary of all her suffering since she was taken from her mother: people had stared at her, tossed her about "**like a wooden puppet**," and now her hair was "**shingled like a coward's**." In her anguish she **moaned for her mother, but no one came to comfort her**; "not a soul reasoned quietly with me, as my own mother used to do; for now I was **only one of many little animals driven by a herder**."

## 📖 PART II — WE TOO ARE HUMAN BEINGS (Bama)

**The setting:** Bama is in the **third class** at school. She has not yet heard people **speak openly of untouchability**, "but I had already **seen, felt, experienced and been humiliated** by what it is." The walk home should take **ten minutes**; it takes her **thirty minutes to an hour**, because every street attraction tethers her legs: the performing Monkey? — the **street entertainers**, the **Maariyaata temple** with its huge bell, **pongal offerings** cooked in front of it, the **dried fish stall by the statue of Gandhi**, sweet and snack stalls side by side, the **street light** "demonstrating how it could change from blue to violet," the **narikkuravan huntergypsy** with his **wild lemur in cages** selling needles, clay beads and ear-cleaning instruments; political parties putting up **stages and haranguing through mikes**; **street plays, puppet shows**, "no magic, no miracle" stunts; the **coffee clubs**, where each waiter **cools the coffee by lifting a tumbler high and pouring its contents into a tumbler in the other hand**; people chopping onions with eyes averted; the almond tree; seasonal treats — **mango, cucumber, sugarcane, sweet-potato, palm-shoots, gram, palm-syrup, palm-fruit, guavas, jackfruit** — and vendors of **payasam, halva, boiled tamarind seeds, iced lollies**. (In an answer, name three or four of these: the long list is the point — childhood joy before the blow.)

**The threshing-floor scene:** one day Bama stops to watch a **threshing floor**: the **landlord** sits on a piece of **sacking spread over a stone ledge**, watching while "our people" drive **muzzled cattle in pairs, round and round**, to tread out the grain. An **elder of her street** comes along from the bazaar carrying a small parcel — by the oil stain, something like **vadai or green banana bhajji** — holding it **out by its string, without touching it**. At the landlord's side he **bows low and extends the packet, cupping the hand that holds the string with his other hand**; the landlord opens the parcel and begins to eat the vadais. To Bama this is pure comedy — a big man carrying a snack "in that fashion" — and she doubles up laughing.

**Annan's revelation:** at home she tells the story "in all its comic detail" to her **elder brother, Annan** (a university student home for holidays). **Annan is not amused.** He explains: the man wasn't being funny — "**everybody believed that they were upper caste and therefore must not touch us. If they did, they would be polluted. That's why he had to carry the package by its string.**" Bama's laughter dies: "I didn't want to laugh any more, and **I felt terribly sad**." Then comes the anger: she feels so provoked she wants to **touch those wretched vadais herself straightaway**; she asks why "we" should fetch and carry for "these fellows" — "**Because they had scraped four coins together, did that mean they must lose all human feelings? But we too are human beings.**" She resolves that her people should work their fields, take their wages, "and leave it at that."

**Annan's own humiliation and his remedy:** Annan tells her how one of the **landlord's men** stopped him by the **irrigation tank**, found him unfamiliar, asked his name — and then immediately, "**Thambi, on which street do you live?**" — because **the street names the caste**. Annan's advice to his little sister is the ideological heart of the episode: "**We are stripped of all dignity or respect... But if we study and make progress, we can throw away these indignities. So study with care, learn all you can. If you are always ahead in your lessons, people will come to you of their own accord and attach themselves to you. Work hard and learn.**" Bama obeys with everything she has: "I studied hard, with all my breath and being, **in a frenzy almost**... I stood first in my class. And because of that, **many people became my friends**."

## 🧩 THE COMPARISON (the most probable long question)
**Common ground:** both are **childhood autobiographies of marginalised women**; both children first experience **institutional discrimination** (the white-run school; the caste village) before they can name it; both suffer **humiliation of the body** (shingled hair; the bowed elder's hand) that is really humiliation of the **soul**; both initially respond alone (hiding under the bed; laughing) and are then given a **mentor's voice** (Judewin's warning; Annan's advice); both texts end not in defeat but in **assertion** ("I will not submit" / study hard and stand first); and both authors grew into **writers who fought oppression with the pen**.
**Differences:** the oppression is **racial-cultural** in Part I (an alien regime demolishing Native identity — hair, clothes, food rituals, language) versus **social-caste** in Part II (one's own countrymen practising untouchability — pollution by touch, servility of errands); Zitkala-Sa's resistance is **immediate and physical** and is crushed, while Bama's response is **delayed, internal and educational** and succeeds; the villain is a **system** (the school) in Part I and a **mindset** (belief in pollution) in Part II; and the remedies differ — Zitkala-Sa's later life is a public crusade of writing and activism, Bama's path is **education as dignity**.

## ❓ PROBABLE QUESTIONS WITH VALUE POINTS
1. **What is the commonality of theme in the two accounts?** — discrimination and humiliation of marginalised communities in childhood, the loss of dignity, and the beginning of resistance; answer must name race/caste explicitly and quote "We too are human beings."
2. **Why did Zitkala-Sa resist the cutting of her hair, and what does her defeat show?** — shingled hair = cowards, short hair = mourners in her culture, so cutting it erased her identity; her defeat shows the brutal power of institutions over a child ("tied fast in a chair... I lost my spirit") and the violence of forced assimilation.
3. **Describe the dining-room episode and its effect.** — three bells, sitting alone, grace, crying; the child feels "securely tied" in a "bedlam"; effect: fear, disorientation, homesickness.
4. **Why did Bama laugh at the elder, and why did she stop?** — the bowed, string-held packet looked comic until Annan explained the "pollution" logic; her laughter turning to sadness and anger marks her **awakening to untouchability**.
5. **What is Annan's advice and its significance?** — study, progress, throw away indignities through excellence; it frames **education as the Dalit road to dignity** and explains Bama's later life as a writer.
6. **"It may take a long time for oppression to be resisted" (NCERT question) — discuss with both texts.** — Zitkala-Sa's people's culture survived the schools' assault; Bama's community fought on through education and literature; both narrators turned childhood wounds into lifelong work.

# ✍️ 4. THE ENEMY — Pearl S. Buck

## 🎬 What kind of text is this?
A **wartime story about a doctor's conscience** by **Pearl S. Buck** — the American Nobel Prize-winning novelist who grew up in China and understood both East and West. The setting is the **Japanese coast during World War II**, and the whole story is built on one question: **when your country's enemy is dying on your doorstep, are you a Japanese first or a doctor first?**

## 📖 THE FULL STORY IN DETAIL

**Dr Sadao Hoki and his world:** Sadao's house stands on a **spot of the Japanese coast where he played as a little boy** — a low, square stone house set on rocks above a narrow beach outlined with **bent pines**. His father, who "never joked or played with him" but "spent infinite pains upon him," took him to the southern islands and repeated his creed: "**Those islands yonder, they are the stepping stones to the future for Japan.**" Education was the father's chief concern: at **twenty-two** Sadao was sent to **America** "to learn all that could be learned of surgery and medicine," and he returned at **thirty**, famous both as a **surgeon and as a scientist**, perfecting "a discovery which would **render wounds entirely clean**." He was kept out of the war for two reasons: his discovery, and the fact that the **old General** might need an operation "for a condition for which he was now being treated medically."

**Hana:** Sadao met Hana in America — at the house of **Professor Harley**, whose rooms were small, food bad and wife voluble — but he "waited to fall in love with her until he was sure she was **Japanese**," because "**his father would never have received her unless she had been pure in her race**." They finished their studies, returned home, and married "**in the old Japanese way**" after his father saw her. They are "perfectly happy," with **two children** — a small son and a **baby boy of nearly three months**.

**The man from the sea:** one foggy evening, something black comes out of the mists — **a man flung up out of the ocean**, staggering, crawling, falling. Sadao guesses "a fisherman washed from his boat," but approaching, they see the **red stain of blood** in the sand. Turning the head, they discover: "**A white man!**" — young (not yet twenty-five), **long yellow hair**, rough blond beard, unconscious. Sadao's expert fingers find a **gunshot wound on the right side of his lower back, reopened — the flesh blackened with powder** — a rock had struck it. The cap has faint lettering: "**U.S. Navy**." He is an **escaped prisoner of war**.

**The dilemma (quote-ready):** Sadao states it exactly: "**If we sheltered a white man in our house we should be arrested and if we turned him over as a prisoner, he would certainly die.**" The "kindest thing would be to put him back into the sea" — but neither can. Sadao confesses the paradox of his profession: "If the man were whole I could turn him over to the police without difficulty. **I care nothing for him. He is my enemy. All Americans are my enemy.**... **But since he is wounded**..." Hana completes it: "You also cannot throw him back to the sea. **Then there is only one thing to do. We must carry him into the house.**" Publicly they will claim they intend to hand him over.

**The servants revolt:** in the **father's old bedroom** (purely Japanese — the old man "would never in his own home sit on a chair or sleep in a foreign bed"), they lay the man on the matted floor. The **old gardener** says bluntly: "The master ought not to heal the wound of this white man... **The white man ought to die.** First he was shot. Then the sea caught him and wounded him with her rocks. **If the master heals what the gun did and what the sea did they will take revenge on us.**" The **cook** is contemptuous; **Yumi**, the children's nurse, refuses outright: "**I have never washed a white man, and I will not wash so dirty a one now**" — "I am a poor person and it is not my business." Left alone, **Hana's anger beats her fear**: "**Stupid Yumi... Is this anything but a man? And a wounded helpless man!**" — and she washes him herself, meticulously, though she does not like him.

**The operation:** Sadao sterilises his instruments on a towel in the **tokonoma alcove** (footnote-worthy Japanese detail: a niche for flower arrangements or a kakemono). Hana must give the anaesthetic: "Simply saturate the cotton and hold it near his nostrils." The bullet is located "**dangerously near the kidney**"; Hana's face goes "the colour of sulphur," she runs out to retch — but **comes back** ("Where is the anesthetic?"). The narrator's irony: Sadao mutters "**Groan if you like. I am not doing this for my own pleasure. In fact, I do not know why I am doing it**" — while Hana thinks of **General Takima**, who "beat his wife cruelly" at home after his victorious battle in **Manchuria** — if such a man could be cruel to a wife, what of a prisoner? She notices "**deep red scars on his neck, just under the ear**" and hopes he was not tortured. Sadao remembers his American professor of anatomy: "**Ignorance of the human body is the surgeon's cardinal sin, sirs!... anything less than that is murder.**" He addresses the patient, as is his habit, "**My friend**" — "forgetting that this was his enemy" — and with "the cleanest and most precise of incisions, **the bullet was out**." The prisoner mutters English ("They got... my guts..."); Sadao gives a hypodermic injection; the pulse steadies: "**This man will live in spite of all.**"

**The household crisis:** the prisoner wakes; Hana herself feeds him from a porcelain spoon ("How come... you speak English..." — "**I was a long time in America**"). The servants decide the couple — "so long in America" — have "forgotten to think of our own country first"; Yumi says it is the **children** they must think of: "What will be their fate if their father is condemned as a **traitor**?" On the **seventh day** after Sadao types (and does not send) a letter to the **Chief of police** — "On the twenty-first day of February an escaped prisoner was washed up on the shore in front of my house" — filed unfinished in a **secret drawer** — the three servants **leave together**, crying; Yumi even runs back to say, "If the baby misses me too much tonight, send for me." Hana pays them off with dignity and does the housework herself. The prisoner, unasked, gives his name: "**Tom**."

**The General:** Sadao is summoned to the **old General** (who suffers gall-bladder trouble and trusts no other surgeon: "The best ones have been trained by Germans and would consider the operation successful **even if I died**"). The General jokes about combining "**German ruthlessness with American sentimentality**," then proposes the chilling plan: "**It would be best if he could be quietly killed** — not by you, but by someone who does not know him. **I have my own private assassins.**" They will come "any night," through the open partition; they "make no noise and they know the **trick of inward bleeding**"; they can even remove the body. Sadao agrees — and reflects that "such persons were essential in an **absolute state**." For **three nights** he sleeps badly, listening for footsteps. The assassins never come. The prisoner recovers; Tom tells him warmly: "**If I hadn't met a Jap like you — well, I wouldn't be alive today... I guess if all the Japs were like you there wouldn't have been a war.**"

**Sadao's solution:** having waited in vain for the state to act, **Sadao acts as both doctor and patriot at once**: he puts his own **boat** on the shore with **food, bottled water and his small flashlight**; instructs the prisoner to row to the little island, wait for a **Korean fishing boat**, signal **two flashes at sunset** if food runs out, **one flash** if still safe ("Do not signal in darkness"), eat **raw fish** ("a fire would be seen"). He dresses Tom in **Japanese clothes**, wraps a **black cloth round the blond head**, and the young man — shaking his hand warmly — rows away into the dark.

**The General's confession and Sadao's safety:** a week later (the General has survived an **emergency operation** at which Sadao worked through the night, "the gall bladder was much involved"), Sadao reports the prisoner "escaped." The General wakes with energy: "**Did I not promise you I would kill him for you?... The truth is, I thought of nothing but myself. In short, I forgot my promise... It was not lack of patriotism or dereliction of duty**" — and asks anxiously that if the matter comes out, Sadao will understand. Sadao "suddenly comprehended that **the General was in the palm of his hand** and that as a consequence **he himself was perfectly safe**": "I can swear to your loyalty, Excellency... You will be rewarded." His true reward is quieter: that night, "searching the spot of black in the twilighted sea," **no signal came — no one was on the island. His prisoner was gone, safe** — and into his mind come, "although without reason," other **white faces**: Professor Harley (a dull man), his silly talkative wife, the anatomy teacher, the landlady — Americans who had been good to him. The last image holds both truths together: Sadao remains a patriot, and yet his hands were never able to kill.

## 👥 CHARACTER SKETCHES
**Dr Sadao Hoki** — a brilliant surgeon-scientist torn between **duty to nation and duty to humanity**; trained in America yet a devoted Japanese patriot (his father's dreams); his profession's ethic ("a doctor can never kill") finally overrides racial hatred; he is also **shrewd** — he neither confesses nor defies the state, but uses the General's selfishness to make himself safe; a study in **quiet moral courage**.
**Hana** — Sadao's wife; initially fearful and obedient to appearances ("we must tell the servants we intend to give him to the police"), she proves **braver than she knows**: washes the white man when Yumi refuses, assists at the operation and holds the anaesthetic despite nausea, faces the servants' leaving with "pride as a mistress," and does all the housework herself; her line "**Is this anything but a man? And a wounded helpless man!**" is her philosophy.
**The General** — the story's **satirical target**: a sick, self-absorbed dictator who plans assassination by "private assassins" and then simply **forgets** ("I thought of nothing but myself"); his admission that he fears his German-trained surgeons' ruthlessness reveals the regime's rot; Sadao's safety comes not from justice but from the General's **fear of exposure**.
**Tom, the prisoner** — a young American sailor, barely twenty-five, tortured (red scars on his neck) yet cheerful, grateful and generous ("if all the Japs were like you there wouldn't have been a war"); a **face** put on "the enemy."
**The servants** — the gardener (superstitious, moss-specialist, loyal to the dead master), the cook (practical, contemptuous), Yumi (proud children's nurse who cries over the baby); they voice **ordinary society's fear and prejudice**, leaving the couple to bear the moral burden alone.

## 🧩 THEMES
1. **Duty as a doctor versus duty as a patriot** — the Hippocratic duty to save life against the national duty to hand over an enemy; the scalpel wins.
2. **Humanity above hatred** — wounds don't have nationalities; "Is this anything but a man?"
3. **Racial prejudice and its cracks** — Sadao's father's obsession with racial purity; Hana forbidden until "pure in her race"; yet the story's kindnesses cross every racial line.
4. **The self-absorption of power** — the General's "private assassins" and convenient amnesia satirise authoritarian states.
5. **Fear of society** — the servants' flight shows how ordinary people enforce conformity, and how lonely moral choices are.
6. **The ambiguity of the ending** — Sadao saves the "enemy," saves himself, and the story refuses to call him either traitor or saint — exactly like real moral life.

## ❓ PROBABLE QUESTIONS WITH VALUE POINTS
1. **Who is Dr Sadao? Where is his house?** — famous Japanese surgeon-scientist, kept home from the war for his discovery and for the General's possible operation; his stone house on the coast where he played as a boy — above the beach on which the enemy will wash ashore.
2. **Was Dr Sadao right in saving the enemy? What would you have done?** — argue the doctor's oath and common humanity against national law; note that he ALSO found a patriotic-legal solution (escape to a neutral island) — he served both gods cleverly.
3. **Will Dr Sadao be arrested for harbouring an enemy?** — no: he never files the letter, the servants leave (no witnesses), the General forgets his own order, and the General's guilt puts him "in the palm of" Sadao's hand; the state's moral cowardice protects him.
4. **What explains the General's attitude — humanity, national disloyalty, dereliction of duty or self-absorption?** — purely self-absorption: "I thought of nothing but myself"; he was in pain, feared surgery, and forgot; he then begs Sadao's silence — the satire on power.
5. **Why was the soldier reluctant to leave?** — the doctor's home was the only place of safety and kindness he had known in Japan; fear of recapture, weakness, gratitude, and the paradox that his "enemy" had given him the only human treatment available.
6. **What makes a human being rise above narrow prejudices?** — professional ethics, the sight of helplessness ("a wounded helpless man"), personal memory of kindness received (the American professor, landlady), and moral imagination — supported by Sadao's and Hana's examples.
7. **Was the doctor's final solution the best out of the difficult alternatives?** — yes by the story's logic: handing over = death (against his oath), releasing locally = arrest, keeping = danger to all; the island plan saved the prisoner, protected the family, and involved no lie of state — a "third level" of moral ingenuity.
