# 🥇 THEME 8: PEASANTS, ZAMINDARS AND THE STATE
## Agrarian Society and the Mughal Empire (c. 16th–17th century) | **15 MARKS — HIGHEST PRIORITY**

> Biggest single chapter in your paper. Expect: 3–4 MCQs, 1 source-based extract (almost always the **Ain**), 1–2 SAQs, and most likely **one 8-marker**. Master this chapter first.

---

## 1️⃣ CBSE VALUE POINTS (Exact keywords the marking scheme searches for)

### (a) Peasants & Rural Population
- **c. 16th–17th centuries: ~85% of India's population lived in villages.**
- Terms for peasant: **raiyat (pl. riaya)**, **muzarian**, **kisan**, **asami**.
- **Two kinds of peasants:** **Khud-kashta** = resident cultivator (lives in the village where he holds land); **Pahi-kashta** = **non-resident** cultivator (lives elsewhere, cultivates on **contract** — reasons: *choice* = better revenue terms, *compulsion* = economic distress/famine).
- Peasants paid revenue **in cash** → linked to markets → **monetisation**.
- **Jins-i-kamil** ("perfect crops") — crops encouraged by state for higher revenue: **cotton & sugarcane** *par excellence*, plus **oilseeds (mustard)**; Bengal famous for **sugar**, Agra **39 varieties**, Delhi **43**, Bengal **50 varieties of rice**; **do-fasla** = two crops a year.

### (b) Irrigation & Technology (MCQ favourites)
- **Wells + wooden ploughshare + hoe** = normal technology.
- **Charas** = leather bag lifted by oxen pair (well irrigation); **dhenkli** = lever device.
- **Persian wheel (rahats?)** — used in north India; **spade & iron-tipped plough**.
- Irrigation projects **by the state AND by zamindars/peasants privately** — individual effort, not just state.

### (c) Village Community (huge 3/8-marker zone)
- **Panchayat** = assembly of **elders with hereditary rights**; an **oligarchy** (not democratic); heterogeneous in mixed-caste villages; **menial workers NOT represented**; decisions **binding**.
- Functions: *revenue settlement, irrigation arrangements, enforced moral conduct (community fines, expulsion), mediated disputes, controlled markets*.
- **Headman = muqaddam / mandal** — chosen by village consensus, **ratified by zamindar**; supervised **village accounts with the patwari**; removable if he lost elders' confidence.
- **Patwari** = village accountant; maintained records at **pargana** level.
- **Panchayat funds** = 1% levy added to land revenue (a **community fund** for famine relief, expenses).
- **Village artisans (panchalas?)** — up to **25% of village households**; **balutedar system (Maharashtra — 18–20 artisans)** vs **jajmani system (north India)** — artisan families exchanged services for grain.
- **Inter-caste jajmani ties**: artisans (ironsmiths → ploughshares, carpenters → wheels) shared grain/seasonal payments.

### (d) Women in Agrarian Society (favourite 3-marker)
- Gendered work: **men tilled & ploughed; women sowed, weeded, threshed & winnowed**.
- Women's **artisanal tasks**: spinning yarn, plying charkha, in-textile-work, grinding grain, carrying loads, **paddy transplantation (esp. Bengal)**.
- **Child marriage, widow remarriage prohibition among upper castes, sati** were social realities.
- **Positive right:** women (peasant & artisan) could **inherit zamindari/milkiyat land** — zamindari inheritance *sometimes* passed to women (widows/daughters) — property right unusual for the period.

### (e) Forests & Tribes
- Beyond villages: forests = source of **fuel, timber, grazing, fodder, wild fruits**; snowbound/desert/arid zones.
- **Tribes (jati/janajati):** Khokhar, Bhil, Gond, Santhal, Kol, Ahom, **Banjaras (itinerant traders — grain/caravan transport, ox-carts)**.
- State–tribe interface: tribes raided villages; states demanded tribute; forests shrank as agriculture expanded (**inroads into forests** = permanent villages inside forest zones).

### (f) Zamindars (8-marker goldmine)
- **Definition:** landed proprietors who lived off agriculture **without producing directly**; enjoyed privileges via superior rural status.
- **Power base (3 pillars):** (1) **Caste** (high status — most were **Rajputs**; also Jats, etc. — caste–zamindari correlation), (2) **Services (khidmat) to the state** (troops), (3) **Revenue collection rights**.
- **Milkiyat** = personal/private lands of zamindar (cultivated with **hired/servile labour**); could be **sold, bequeathed, mortgaged** at will.
- Additional powers: collected **revenue on behalf of the state**, levied **extra taxes** (on markets, festivals, passing trade), owned **fortified residences (mud fortresses)** and **cavalry/military resources**; gave **credit (loans)** to peasants.
- Ways to become zamindar: **(i) by birth/lineage (hereditary clan right), (ii) purchase of land, (iii) grant/reward from state (inam? madad-i-maash to service groups), (iv) inheritance (incl. women)**.
- **Sharers in produce** — some were simply rich peasants who grew into zamindari.

### (g) Land Revenue System (most examined section — no mercy, learn exactly)
- Basic unit: **pargana** (villages grouped) → **sarkar** → **suba** (province).
- Officials at pargana: **shiqdar** (executive), **qanungo** (record-keeper), **patwari** (village accounts), **amin** (measurement/ensuring imperial regulations), **fotadar** (treasurer), **potadar**? (keeper of measures). Learn first four cold.
- **Abu'l Fazl's 4 methods of revenue assessment:** **zabti (measurement-based, most widespread)**, **batai/ghalla-bakhshi (produce division — most transparent)**, **kankut (estimation by grain heap)**, **nasaq (rough appraisal/summary of past demands)**.
- **Zabti/dahsala system (1580)** — evolved by **Raja Todar Mal**: 10-year average of **produce AND prices** = standard schedule (**dastur**), province-wise.
- **Akbar's land classification (from the Ain — verbatim extract below):**
  - **Polaj** = cultivated every year, never fallow.
  - **Parauti** = left out of cultivation temporarily to regain strength.
  - **Chachar** = fallow **3–4 years**.
  - **Banjar** = uncultivated **5 years or more**.
  - Polaj & Parauti → 3 grades: **good, middling, bad** → **average ("third") = medium produce** → **state's share = 1/3 of average produce** (in cash).
- Revenue rates in cash → peasants forced to sell in markets → **villages linked to urban markets** (monetisation).
- **Jama** = total assessed revenue (includes taxes other than land revenue — hence **inflated/overestimated**).
- **Mansabdari linkage:** mansabdars = **military-cum-bureaucratic** elite; paid by **naqdi (cash)** or **jagir (revenue assignment)** — jagirdars collected revenue but did NOT own the land; **transferred periodically** (prevented local power).

### (h) Flow of Silver (MCQ + 3-marker)
- **Expanded trade → huge silver inflow** (New World/Mexico-Peru + Japan) because India **had no natural silver resources** → **silver rupya issued by Akbar** (also copper dams, gold muhrs) → high **monetisation** despite instability of currency value.

### (i) Ain-i-Akbari (source itself)
- **Author: Abu'l Fazl** (court historian of Akbar), completed **1595** (part of **Akbar Nama**); **Book 3** = administration, revenue, army, provinces; compiled in **5 books**.
- **Purpose:** present the **vision of the Empire** — "a comprehensive vision of social harmony" where every class has a place; also a **manual for officials**.
- **Limitations (classic 3-marker):** (i) **numeric data unreliable/ambiguous**, (ii) **regional variation ignored**, (iii) **misses crops like tobacco & maize** (introduced later?/omissions), (iv) ideologically slanted towards Akbar.
- **Value:** with **Ain data**, historians (e.g., **Irfan Habib, *Agrarian System of Mughal India***) reconstruct agrarian relations, village structure, taxation.

### 📜 EXTRACT 1 — "Classification of Lands under Akbar" (Ain-i-Akbari, Source 5 in NCERT)
> *"The Emperor Akbar in his profound sagacity classified the lands and fixed a different revenue to be paid by each. **Polaj** is land which is annually cultivated for each crop in succession and is never allowed to lie fallow. **Parauti** is land left out of cultivation for a time that it may recover its strength. **Chachar** is land that has lain fallow for three or four years. **Banjar** is land uncultivated for five years and more. Of the first two kinds of land, there are three classes, good, middling, and bad. They add together the produce of each sort, and the third of this represents the medium produce, one-third part of which is exacted as the Royal dues."*

**Predicted sub-questions:**
1. What principles guided Mughal land classification? → **Fertility + continuity of cultivation**; fairness through **averaging** good/middling/bad.
2. Why was land left fallow (parauti/chachar/banjar)? → To **regain fertility**; revenue rate reduced accordingly.
3. State ONE limitation of the Ain as a source. → **Numeric/ambiguity problems; regional variation ignored** (answer both, examiner wants "problems in using the Ain" — validity of data, omissions like tobacco/maize).

### 📜 EXTRACT 2 — "Peasants and their lands" (17th-century sources)
> *"The term which Indo-Persian sources of the Mughal period most frequently used to denote a peasant was **raiyat** (plural, riaya) or **muzarian**... Sources of the seventeenth century refer to two kinds of peasants — **khud-kashta** and **pahi-kashta**. The former were residents of the village in which they held their lands. The latter were non-resident cultivators who belonged to some other village, but cultivated lands elsewhere on a contractual basis. People became pahi-kashta either out of choice — for example, when terms of revenue in a distant village were more favourable — or out of compulsion — for example, forced by economic distress after a famine."*

**Predicted sub-questions:**
1. Distinguish khud-kashta from pahi-kashta. (definition table above)
2. Why did peasants become pahi-kashta? → **Better revenue terms (choice) / famine-distress (compulsion)**.
3. Which sources reveal peasant categories? → **Indo-Persian chronicles + local revenue records (pargana documents)**.

## 5️⃣ PREDICTED LONG ANSWERS (Top 2 — write these exact skeletons)

### ⭐ Q1. "The Ain-i-Akbari is a remarkable but flawed source for reconstructing Mughal agrarian history." Examine. (8M — historical-reconstruction favourite)

**Intro:** The Ain-i-Akbari (1595), Book 3 of the Akbar Nama by **Abu'l Fazl**, is the single richest record of Mughal agrarian administration, but it must be used critically.

**Value points:**
- **Purpose:** to present the **vision of Akbar's empire** — social harmony with a place for every class — and to serve as an **administrative manual** (revenue schedules, province data).
- **Richness:** land classification (polaj–banjar), rates (1/3 of average), officials (amin, qanungo, shiqdar), crop lists (Agra 39, Delhi 43, Bengal 50 rice varieties), peasant categories (khud-kashta/pahi-kashta), zamindari, mansab-jagir.
- **Enables quantitative history** — **Irfan Habib's *Agrarian System of Mughal India*** built on Ain data.
- **Limitation 1:** statistical data **unreliable, ambiguous, overestimated (jama ≠ actual collection)**.
- **Limitation 2:** **regional/seasonal variation ignored**; (iii) **omits crops like tobacco and maize**; (iv) **court-biased** (presents an ideal, not ground reality).
- **Conclusion:** Still indispensable — **corroborated with local records, Persian chronicles and traveller accounts**, it remains the foundation of Mughal agrarian historiography.

### ⭐ Q2. Describe the role of zamindars in Mughal agrarian society. Why did they often clash with the Mughal state? (8M)

**Intro:** Zamindars were the **non-cultivating landed class** of the Mughal countryside — intermediaries between peasants and the state.

**Value points:**
- **Status:** derived from **high caste (Rajputs, Jats)**, **khidmat (services to state)**, and control of **milkiyat** (personal lands, sale/mortgage rights).
- **Functions:** collected **revenue on behalf of the state**, levied **cesses** on markets/festivals, maintained **forts and cavalry**, advanced **credit** to peasants, settled new villages.
- **Continuity:** hereditary rights made them the **most stable element** in rural society — peasants changed, zamindars remained.
- **Clash with state:** state's drive to **fix/raise revenue (zabti)** and to **bypass or dispossess** zamindars; **jagirdar transfers** disrupted local equations; high demands → default → **coercion**.
- **Result:** 17th-century **peasant-zamindar revolts** (Jats, Sikhs, Marathas, Satnamis) often led by *dispossessed zamindars*.
- **Conclusion:** Zamindars were both the **pillars of Mughal rural order** and, when squeezed, its **gravest internal challengers**.

## 1️⃣ CBSE VALUE POINTS (Examiner keywords)

### (a) Why travellers came & why their accounts matter
- People travelled for: **work, escaping natural disasters, trade/merchandise, soldiering, priesthood, pilgrimage, adventure**.
- Their records = **comparative outsider view** → used by historians **alongside** inscriptions, chronicles, archaeology; reveal aspects insiders ignored (e.g., **slavery, social customs**).
- Accounts survive because: many were **instructed/ordered to write**, copied by scribes; **"uniqueness" of each traveller's lens** (religion, homeland, profession shaped observation).

### (b) Al-Biruni (973–1048) — *Kitab-ul-Hind* (THE most examined traveller)
- Born **973 CE, Khwarizm** (present-day **Uzbekistan**); polyglot: **Syriac, Arabic, Persian, Hebrew + Sanskrit**.
- **1017:** taken to **Ghazni as a hostage** by Sultan **Mahmud**; stayed rest of life; learned **Sanskrit with Brahmana priests**; read **Samkhya of Kapila, Bhagavad Gita, Patanjali's Yoga Sutra** (works already translated into Arabic since 8th c. sparked his interest).
- **Kitab-ul-Hind** (= *Tahqiq-i-Hind*?): written in **Arabic**, "simple and lucid", **voluminous — 80 chapters**: religion, philosophy, festivals, astronomy, alchemy, manners & customs, social life, weights & measures, iconography, laws.
- **Signature structure:** each chapter = **Question → Description (from Sanskritic tradition) → Comparison with other cultures** — a "**geometric structure**" owed to his **mathematical orientation** (MCQ trap: it is NOT chronological).
- **Barriers he faced:** (i) **language**, (ii) **religion/insularity**, (iii) **"self-centredness"** — belief that one's own ways are best. **Strategies:** learned Sanskrit from texts & priests, avoided judgement, compared cultures.
- **Varna account:** described 4 varnas from **Brahmanical normative texts** (Brahmana from **head**, Kshatriya **shoulders & hands**, Vaishya **thigh**, Shudra **feet** of Brahman); he **disapproved** of caste (notion of pollution) **yet presented it as Brahmanas did** — showing reliance on Sanskrit texts over observed reality.
- **Nuance he recorded:** **antyaja** (born outside the system) categories faced social oppression **but were inside economic networks** (labour for peasants & zamindars) — real-life rigidity lower than textual ideal.

### (c) Ibn Battuta (1304–~1369) — *Rihla*
- From **Tangier, Morocco**; trained as a **qazi (judge)**; **8 years + in Delhi** as **Qazi of Delhi under Muhammad bin Tughlaq**; sent as the Sultan's **ambassador to China**; robbed, shipwrecked, yet travelled ~**30 years** (set out 1332 at age 22; returned 1354); died ~1369.
- ***Rihla*** (Arabic, "travelogue"): dictated **in Morocco to Ibn Juzayy** on the Sultan's order; **meticulous recording of people, cultures, beliefs, values**; often **compared with Marco Polo** (Italy, 1254–1323).
- **Admired India's:** **cities** ("full of exciting things"), efficient **roads** (shaded trees, wells, sarais), and the **postal system**:
  - **Uluq** = **horse post**, stations every **4 miles**.
  - **Dawa** = **foot post**, 3 stations per mile (each **⅓ mile** — couriers with **rod + copper bells**); foot post **faster** than horse post; even carried **fruits of Khurasan** to India.
  - Speed proof: Sind→Delhi news **50 days** for ordinary travellers vs **5 days** via spy reports (state surveillance efficiency).
- **Curiosities recorded:** the **bish** tree? (poisoned-leaves tree people believed charmed), wells & shady trees on highways, Multan→Delhi = **40 days** travel, India's slave markets, **Muhammad bin Tughlaq's** harsh punishments.
- **Lens:** a **qazi-legal + adventure lens** — described festivals, food, justice, markets in vivid detail.

### (d) François Bernier (1620–1688) — *Travels in the Mughal Empire*
- **French physician & political philosopher**; in India **~12 years (1656–1668)**; personal **physician to Prince Dara Shikoh**, later attached to **Danishmand Khan** (noble).
- Wrote for the **French King Louis XIV** — comparative, critical, **"reflection" genre**.
- **Method (MCQ favourite):** constant **India-vs-Europe comparison** using **binary opposition** (India = inverse of Europe) → **hierarchical ordering** → India shown **inferior**; motive partly **fear of religious persecution back home** (Europe's intolerance) — yet he still claimed European superiority.
- **Landownership thesis (his most examined claim):** **NO private property in land in Mughal India** — the **Emperor owned all land** → nobles = mere holders → (i) **no incentive to invest/improve**, (ii) landholders **couldn't pass land to children**, (iii) **agriculture decayed** → he called the empire a "**sick body politic**" / Oriental despotism.
- **Historians' correction (must quote in answers):** Bernier **exaggerated** — zamindari & milkiyat rights, madad-i-maash grants show **forms of private property existed**; yet his idea fed later European theories (**Montesquieu, Marx's "Asiatic mode"? oriental despotism debates**).
- **On cities:** Mughal cities = "**camps**" — imperial camp moved; cities existed for the court/army, not civic society (his binary again).
- **On women/slaves:** described **concubines & domestic slaves**, praised?/criticised their treatment; noted women's segregation, yet mentions influential women (rani? Gulbadan? not needed — value point: **slavery & seclusion as observed social realities**).

### 📜 EXTRACT 1 — Al-Biruni on the *System of Varnas* (NCERT Source 6)

## 5️⃣ PREDICTED LONG ANSWERS (Top 2)

### ⭐ Q1. Analyse Al-Biruni's understanding of the caste system. Why did his account differ from social reality? (8M — CBSE asks this repeatedly)

**Intro:** Al-Biruni (973–1048), the Khwarizm scholar at Ghazni, discussed caste in the **Kitab-ul-Hind**, consciously drawing on **normative Sanskrit texts**.

**Value points:**
- **Description:** 4 varnas born from Brahman's body — head (Brahmana), shoulders & hands (Kshatriya), thigh (Vaishya), feet (Shudra); Brahmanas "choice part of mankind".
- **Method:** studied Sanskrit under **Brahmanas**; read **Samkhya, Gita, Patanjali** → presented the system **as Brahmanas explained it**, i.e., **textual, not observational**.
- **Critique:** he **disapproved** of varna hierarchy & pollution concepts, comparing them unfavourably with other societies; yet accepted textual logic.
- **Reality gap:** everyday life less rigid — **antyaja** groups were socially oppressed **but economically integrated**; castes "lived together in the same towns and villages, mixed in the same houses".
- **Barriers:** language, religious insularity, self-centredness — handled via **language mastery + comparison**.
- **Conclusion:** His account shows both the **strength** (insider-textual precision) and **danger** (elite-text bias) of traveller sources — historians must cross-check with epigraphy & local records.

### ⭐ Q2. "Bernier's comparison of Mughal India with Europe was more a reflection of his own ideology than of Indian reality." Discuss. (8M)

**Intro:** Bernier, the French physician-philosopher who spent 1656–68 in India, wrote *Travels in the Mughal Empire* for **Louis XIV** as a work of **critical comparison**.

**Value points:**
- **Binary method:** India presented as the **inverse of Europe** and **hierarchically inferior**; part of a universal framework of comparing polities.
- **Crown landownership thesis:** Emperor owned all land → holders lacked incentive → **no hereditary security → under-investment → agrarian decay** → "sick body politic" / despotism.
- **Cities as "camps":** urban life seen as derivative of the imperial camp — again his binary.
- **Slavery/women:** used concubinage & slave markets as evidence of backwardness.
- **Counter-evidence:** **milkiyat lands, zamindari rights, madad-i-maash grants, thriving markets (silver inflow, textile exports)** show property & prosperity existed; his **exaggerations** served to glorify Europe's private property.
- **Legacy & use:** despite bias, his accounts are **detailed observations** of revenue practice, nobility, cities — invaluable when read against **indigenous sources** (Ain, local records).
- **Conclusion:** Bernier is best read as a **dialogue between observation and European political theory** — biased in frame, precise in detail.

## 1️⃣ CBSE VALUE POINTS (Examiner keywords)

### (a) Integration of Cults (concept — define it and you win the 3-marker)
- Historians' concept explaining how cults of local deities merged into Puranic Hinduism.
- **Two processes:** (i) **dissemination of Brahmanical ideas** — Puranic texts composed/compiled in **simple Sanskrit**, made accessible to **women & Shudras** (excluded from Vedic learning); (ii) **Brahmanas accepting & reworking** beliefs/practices of "other" social categories.
- Result: **continuous dialogue between "great" Sanskritic Puranic traditions and "little" local traditions.**
- **Classic example:** **Puri (Odisha)** — local deity identified by the **12th century** as **Jagannatha** ("lord of the world"), a **form of Vishnu** (with sister Subhadra & brother Balabhadra).

### (b) Alvars & Nayanars of Tamil Nadu (6th century onwards)
- **Alvars** = "those **immersed** in devotion to **Vishnu**"; **Nayanars** = devotees of **Shiva**.
- Travelled place to place **singing Tamil hymns**; set them up as shrines (**sacred geography** of temples).
- **Caste diversity:** came from **artisan/cultivator/outcaste (untouchable) communities** — challenged Brahmanical monopoly; some historians see a **protest against Buddhism & Jainism** (then dominant in Tamil region) — later the traditions **integrated** with Brahmanical order.
- **Women saints:** **Andal** (woman **Alvar**, considered the **bride of Vishnu**, her songs still sung at weddings) & **Karaikkal Ammaiyar** (woman **Nayanar**, renounced beauty, became a **ghoul/hag figure** devoted to Shiva).
- **Anthologies (MCQ gold):** **Nalayira Divyaprabandham** = "four thousand compositions" of Alvars, described as the **"Tamil Veda"** (compiled by the 10th century); **Tevaram** = Nayanar hymn collection.
- **Chola support (10th c. onward):** kings built ** elaborately decorated temples** with **bronze sculptures** of Shiva, Nayanar saints etc.; saints' images installed in temples.

### (c) Virashaiva / Lingayat Tradition (12th century, Karnataka)
- Leader: **Basavanna** (a Brahmana minister) + Allama Prabhu, Akkamahadevi (women vachana writers).
- Sacred texts: **vachanas** ("sayings") in **Kannada** — composed orally, later collected.
- Worshipped **Shiva as jangama** (wandering monk); wore the **linga?** — hence "Lingayat" (wearers of the lingam? - value point: devotees wore a small linga).
- **Challenged:** (i) **caste system**, (ii) **doctrine of rebirth**, (iii) **Vedic authority & ritual purity**, (iv) **temple worship** in favour of direct devotion.
- **Unique practices:** **buried their dead** (since they rejected rebirth); encouraged **post-puberty marriage & widow remarriage**; **jogatis/jangamas** devotees; women saints wrote vachanas.
- **Basavanna's vachana message (extract below):** mocks **ritualism** — pouring milk on stone serpents while killing real ones; feeding images that cannot eat while ignoring hungry devotees → **critique of empty ritual, praise of human service**.

### (d) North Indianbhakti milieu
- **Rajput court culture** (temples, Sanskrit) + **popular movements**: **Naths, Siddhas, Yogis** — questioned authority of Vedas, used **vernacular languages**; set the stage for Kabir & the Sikh tradition.

### (e) Sufism — silsilas, khanqahs, practices (half the chapter = half the marks)
- **Sufi = Muslim mystics** seeking **proximity to Allah** through asceticism, devotion, love; rejected worldliness & ostentation of clergy.
- **Silsila** (lit. "**chain**") = **continuous link between master (shaikh/pir) and disciple (murid)** stretching as an **unbroken spiritual genealogy to Prophet Muhammad**; channel of **spiritual power & blessings**.
- **Initiation rituals:** **oath of allegiance (bai'ah)**, **patched garment**, **shaved hair**; shaikh's grace = **barakat**; miracles = **karamat**; sufi with proximity to Allah = **wali (pl. auliya — friend of God)**.
- **Khanqah** = hospice/centre of social life (Persian); **dargah** (Persian "**court**") = tomb-shrine of dead shaikh → centre of **ziyarat (pilgrimage)**.
- **Major silsilas:** **Chishti** (subcontinent's most influential — simplicity, austerity, acceptance of **futuh** = unasked-for charity, distance from court), **Suhrawardi** (court-linked), later **Qadiri, Naqshbandi**.
- **Chishti silsila — the 5 teachers table (memorise verbatim):**
| Sufi Teacher | Year of Death | Dargah |
|---|---|---|

- **Life in the Chishti khanqah (source extract below):** Shaikh Nizamuddin's hospice at **Ghiyaspur, on the banks of the Yamuna** (outside Delhi) — small rooms + big hall (**jama'at khana**), shaikh's **rooftop room**, veranda, boundary wall; **open kitchen (langar)** run on **futuh**; refuge during **Mongol invasion scares**.
- **Ziyarat & qawwali:** pilgrimage to dargahs (esp. **Ajmer Urs of Muinuddin Chishti** — believed to fulfil wishes); **sama'** = spiritual music & dance assemblies; **qaul** = Arabic "**saying**" — hymn sung at the **opening or closing of qawwali**, introduced by **Amir Khusrau (1253–1325)**, disciple of Nizamuddin Auliya → qawwals still begin with the qaul.
- **Language:** Chishtis conversed in **Hindavi** (local language) — linked sufis to the common people; poetry in **Punjabi, Hindavi, Deccani**.
- **Sufi–state relations:** sultans gave **charitable grants (madad-i-maash)** and land to shaikhs; **Chishtis avoided courts** (Nizamuddin refused royal summons) → moral legitimacy for the state + popularity for sufis.

### (g) Kabir (c. 14th–15th centuries)
- **Weaver (julaha) by caste?** — low-caste mystic poet; **criticised BOTH Brahmanical Hinduism AND Islam's external rituals** (idolatry, circumcision); spoke of the formless divine — **Alakh** (the unseen), **niranjana**; used **"ultabansi"** (riddle/paradox sayings), **sakhi** & **sabad** forms; language = mixture of vernaculars (**sant bhasha**).
- **Verses survive in 3 overlapping traditions (memorise):**
1. **Kabir Bijak** — preserved by the **Kabirpanth** (Varanasi & Uttar Pradesh).
2. **Kabir Granthavali** — associated with the **Dadupanth** (Rajasthan).
3. **Adi Granth Sahib** — many compositions included by Sikh Gurus.
- Message: **bhakti + nirguna devotion, critique of orthodoxy (ulema & brahmanas both), human equality.**

### (h) Guru Granth Sahib & the Sikh tradition
- Teachings of **Guru Nanak** (1469–1539? — from Talwandi? value point: Nanak rejected external rituals, preached nirguna bhakti, equality) developed by successors.
- **Adi Granth compiled 1604 by 5th Guru, Guru Arjan** — includes **own hymns + hymns of non-Sikh saints: Sheikh Farid (sufi), Kabir, Raidas (Ravidas), Namdev** → eclectic, cross-community text; language **Punjabi (Gurmukhi)**.
- Later compiled/installed at **Harmandir Sahib (Amritsar?)** — value point: Sikh scripture exemplifies **syncretic devotional culture**.

### (i) Mirabai (c. 15th–16th century) & Shankaradeva
- **Mirabai:** **Rajput princess (Mewar)**; defied husband & royal norms; devoted to **Krishna**; recognised a **leather-worker, Raidas, as her guru** → direct challenge to **high-caste & gender norms**; songs in **Rajasthani & mixed dialects**; inspiration for centuries of common people's devotion.
- **Shankaradeva (late 15th c., Assam):** preached **Bhagavati dharma** based on **Bhagavad Gita & Bhagavata Purana**; practices: **nam kirtan** (congregational singing), **satra** (monastery) & **naam ghar** (prayer hall) — Vaishnavism in the Brahmaputra valley.

### ⭐ Q1. "The Bhakti movement challenged the existing social order while simultaneously integrating with it." Examine with reference to the Alvar-Nayanar and Virashaiva traditions. (8M)

**Intro:** From the 6th century, devotional movements in Tamil Nadu and Karnataka redefined religious participation — open protest **and** gradual absorption into Brahmanical religion.

**Value points:**
- **Challenge — caste:** Alvars/Nayanars from **artisan, cultivator, outcaste backgrounds**; Lingayats **rejected caste & rebirth**, accepted women saints (**Andal, Karaikkal Ammaiyar, vachana women**).
- **Challenge — ritual & texts:** Basavanna's vachana mocking ritual (milk on stone serpent); Naths/Siddhas rejected Vedic authority; vernacular hymns vs Sanskrit monopoly.
- **Challenge — other religions:** early saint movements opposed **Buddhism & Jainism's** dominance.
- **Integration:** Brahmanas reworked local cults — **Jagannatha at Puri (12th c.)**; saints' images installed in **Chola temples**; hymns compiled as **"Tamil Veda" (Nalayira Divyaprabandham)**; temple worship absorbed saints.
- **Dialogue framework:** "great" Sanskritic Puranic traditions ↔ "little" traditions.
- **Conclusion:** Bhakti was **both a protest and a bridge** — democratising devotion while enriching the Brahmanical mainstream — the very dialectic CBSE rewards with the term **"integration of cults."**

### ⭐ Q2. Describe the organisation and significance of the Chishti silsila. Why did it become the most influential sufi tradition in the subcontinent? (8M)

**Intro:** The Chishti silsila, established in India by **Shaikh Muinuddin Sijzi (d. 1235, Ajmer)**, grew through an unbroken master-disciple **chain** to become medieval India's most popular devotional order.

**Value points:**
- **Organisation:** **silsila** (chain to Prophet Muhammad), **khanqah** (Ghiyaspur — jama'at khana, langar on **futuh**), initiation oath (**bai'ah**), patched garment; succession via **khalifas/disciples**.
- **Values:** austerity, simplicity, **acceptance of unsolicited charity**, refusal of court offices (**Nizamuddin Auliya**), service & inclusiveness.
- **Practices:** **sama'** (music), **qaul** of **Amir Khusrau** → **qawwali**; **ziyarat** at dargahs (Ajmer, Delhi, Ajodhan/Pakpattan); hospices as **refuge** (Mongol scares).
- **Language & culture:** conversation in **Hindavi** → closeness to masses; patronage of music/poetry; **syncretic appeal** to Hindus & Muslims.
- **Texts:** **Fawa'id-al-Fu'ad** (malfuzat), **Siyar-ul-Auliya** & Akhbar-ul-Akhyar (tazkiras) spread the shaikhs' fame.
- **State relations:** sultans gave **grants/land** to khanqahs — Chishtis' moral distance made them **social legitimisers** of rulers.
- **Conclusion:** Simplicity + inclusiveness + vernacular idiom + charismatic shrines = mass following that **outlived empires** — the reason CBSE calls the Chishtis "the most influential silsila".

- Founded **1336** by brothers **Harihara I & Bukka I** (Sangama dynasty) at **Hampi, on the banks of the Tungabhadra** (doab between **Krishna & Tungabhadra**).
- **Four dynasties:** **Sangama** (till 1485) → **Saluva** (military chiefs) → **Tuluva** (**Krishnadeva Raya**) → **Aravidu** (post-1565, ruled from Penukonda — eastward shift).
- **Krishnadeva Raya (1509–1529)** — most famous Tuluva ruler: **expansion & consolidation**, defeated **Gajapati rulers of Orissa** & subordinated the Deccan sultans; **built fine temples, added impressive gopurams**; composed **Amuktamalyada** (Telugu work); capital at its most prosperous.
- **Nomenclature (MCQ gold):** in popular tradition — **rayas = narapati (lord of men)**; **Deccan Sultans = ashvapati (lord of horses)**; **Gajapati = lord of elephants (Orissa lineage)**.
- **1565 — Battle of Rakshasa-Tangadi (Talikota):** Rama Raya defeated by allied **Deccan sultans**; capital **sacked & abandoned** → ruins.

### (c) Kings, Traders & Economy
- Warfare depended on **cavalry** → **import of horses from Arabia & Central Asia** (controlled first by **Arab traders**; local **kudirai chettis** = horse merchants; from **1498 the Portuguese** arrived on the west coast with superior military technology — **muskets** — becoming power players).
- City famed for markets in **spices, textiles & precious stones**; wealthy population demanded **high-value exotic goods**; trade = status symbol; **revenue from trade strengthened the state**.

### (d) Amara-Nayaka System (best 3/4-marker)
- **Nayakas** = military chiefs who controlled **forts & armed supporters**; often spoke **Telugu or Kannada**; some gained power by conquering areas, others by royal appointment.
- **Amara** = territory/revenue assignment; **amara-nayakas**:
  1. collected **taxes & other dues** from peasants, traders, artisans,
  2. retained **part of revenue for personal use + maintaining troops & horses**,
  3. sent **annual tribute** to the king,
  4. **furnished military contingents** when required.
- Analysed as parallel to **iqta system (Delhi Sultanate)** — CBSE's favourite comparison.
- Later, powerful nayakas **established independent kingdoms** (post-1565: Mysore, Keladi/Ikkeri, Madurai, Tanjavur).

### (e) Fortification & Water (unique facts = MCQs)
- **Seven lines of forts** encircled **not only the urban core but also agricultural tracts and irrigated valleys** (defence + subsistence security).
- Walls = **unbaked brick? no — stone/rubble with mortar**; gateways arched? — value point: fortification enclosed **106 sq. km**? (skip precise number if unsure — say "vast fortified zone including farmland").
- **Water management:** canals drew water from the **Tungabhadra** — e.g., **Hiriya canal** (one of the most prominent — drew water from a dam across the Tungabhadra); **Kamalapuram tank** (15th c. — irrigation + royal centre supply); **aqueducts** carried water into the royal centre; **stepped stone tank** in the sacred centre.

### (f) Urban Zones (map + long answer)
- **Royal Centre:** ~60? (say "many") temples & shrines; **"king's palace"** = largest complex (Paes described it); **Hazara Rama temple** (royal cult? — used only by the king & family); **mahanavami dibba** + **audience hall** ("House of Victory"); **lotus mahal & stables** (so-named by later British observers).
- **Sacred Centre:** on the Tungabhadra's northern? — value point: **Virupaksha temple** (patron deity **Virupaksha = form of Shiva**) — oldest zone, **gopurams added by Krishnadeva Raya**; **Vitthala temple** (deity **Vitthala = form of Vishnu**), famous for **mandapas** & the **stone chariot? (mention)**; **temple bazaar streets** (markets flanked temple streets).
- **Distinctive architecture (2 terms CBSE always tests):**
  - **Gopuram** = **tall royal gateway** to temple.
  - **Mandapa** = **pavilion** — long pillared halls (e.g., **kalyana mandapa** for divine marriage ceremonies).
  - **Raya gopurams** — royal gateways often **associated with Krishnadeva Raya's reign**.
- **Mahanavami dibba (extract below):** one of the **highest points**; **massive platform, base ~11,000 sq. ft, height 40 ft**, **relief carvings** on base, **wooden superstructure**; rituals coincided with **Mahanavami** ("the great ninth day") of the ten-day **Dusehra/Durga Puja/Navaratri** festival (Sept–Oct): king inspected the **army & navy? (army)**, received **tribute & gifts**, watched **processions & dances**, entertained guests.

### (g) Why architecture = political statement (the conceptual 6-marker)
- Temple = **royal patronage & legitimation**: kings linked themselves to deities (Virupaksha) — rulers took **titles of gods** (e.g., "Hari hara?"), installed images of **kings & queens in temples**.
- Monumental scale = **imperial power & prosperity**; inscriptions show kings & nayakas **competing in donations**.
- Festival (Mahanavami) = **annual ritual of loyalty** — nayakas & nobles presented tribute = political subordination ritualised.

## 5️⃣ PREDICTED LONG ANSWERS (Top 2)

### ⭐ Q1. Describe the amara-nayaka system of the Vijayanagara empire. In what ways was it similar to the iqta system? (6/8M)

**Intro:** The amara-nayaka system was the **political-military backbone** of Vijayanagara, through which kings ruled through **military chiefs holding revenue assignments**.

**Value points:**
- **Who:** nayakas — military chiefs with forts & armed followers, mostly **Telugu/Kannada** speaking.
- **Amara:** revenue territory granted by the king.
- **Duties (4):** collect **taxes/dues**; retain part for **personal & military expenses**; send **annual tribute**; supply **troops** to the king.
- **Similarities with iqta:** revenue assignment in lieu of service, periodic transfers?, central claim on tribute & contingents.
- **Divergence/risk:** powerful nayakas built local bases → **independent kingdoms** (Mysore, Keladi, Madurai, Tanjavur) after 1565.
- **Conclusion:** system = **decentralised imperial management** — strength and eventual fragility of the empire.

### ⭐ Q2. "Temple architecture in Vijayanagara reflected the power and legitimacy of the rulers." Discuss with reference to gopurams, mandapas and the Mahanavami festival. (6/8M)

**Intro:** Hampi's monumental sacred & royal centres were deliberate **visual declarations of imperial majesty**.

**Value points:**
- **Royal patronage:** kings & nayakas competed in temple donations (inscriptions) — piety = political currency.
- **Gopurams & mandapas:** **tall gateways (gopuram)** & pillared pavilions (**kalyana mandapa**) — scaled up under **Krishnadeva Raya**; **raya gopurams** literally bear the king's title.
- **Virupaksha & Vitthala temples:** royal deity **Virupaksha (Shiva)** linked king & god; **temple bazaar streets** = economic hubs of the capital.
- **Mahanavami dibba:** platform (11,000 sq. ft, 40 ft) for the ten-day **Dusehra/Mahanavami** festival — king received **tribute**, reviewed **army**, held **processions** → ritualised loyalty of nayakas.
- **Royal centre's proximity:** palace + Hazara Rama temple fused sacred & secular authority.
- **Conclusion:** architecture + ritual converted **economic power into sacred legitimacy** — the core thesis CBSE rewards.
