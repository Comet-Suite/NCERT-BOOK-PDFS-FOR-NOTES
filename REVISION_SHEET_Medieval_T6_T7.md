# 🟩 THEME 6: BHAKTI-SUFI TRADITIONS — CHANGES IN RELIGIOUS BELIEFS AND DEVOTIONAL TEXTS (c. 8th–18th century)

## 🎬 The story of the chapter
Between the eighth and the eighteenth centuries, the subcontinent wove one giant religious fabric out of many coloured threads: local goddesses merged into Vishnu and Shiva, poet-saints sang in Tamil and Kannada, Islam arrived with traders and conquerors, Sufis set up hospices, and Kabir, Nanak and Mirabai dissolved boundaries. The chapter is a journey from "integration of cults" to "dialogue and dissent," and its real subject is how historians read poems, tomb-shrines and letters as history.

## 1. A Mosaic of Religious Beliefs and Practices
The most striking feature of this phase is the **increasing visibility of a wide range of gods and goddesses** in sculpture as well as texts, with the major deities — **Vishnu, Shiva and the goddess** — visualised in a variety of forms. Historians see at least **two processes** at work. The first was the **dissemination of Brahmanical ideas**, exemplified by the composition, compilation and preservation of **Puranic texts in simple Sanskrit verse**, explicitly meant to be **accessible to women and Shudras**, who were generally excluded from Vedic learning. The second was the reverse process — **Brahmanas accepting and reworking the beliefs and practices of other social categories** — so that beliefs were shaped through a continuous dialogue between what sociologists call the "**great**" Sanskritic Puranic traditions and the "**little**" traditions throughout the land.

The most striking example of integration is **Puri, Orissa**, where by the **twelfth century** the principal deity was identified as **Jagannatha** (literally, "the lord of the world"), a form of Vishnu — yet a **local deity whose image was, and continues to be, made of wood by local tribal specialists**. Among goddess cults, local deities — often worshipped simply as a **stone smeared with ochre** — were incorporated into the Puranic framework by giving them an identity as **wives of the principal male deities**: sometimes equated with **Lakshmi** (Vishnu's wife), sometimes with **Parvati** (Shiva's wife).

**Difference and conflict** coexisted with integration. Forms of worship associated with the goddess were classified as **Tantric**: Tantric practices were **widespread across the subcontinent**, were **open to women and men**, and practitioners often **ignored differences of caste and class within the ritual context**; these ideas influenced **Shaivism and Buddhism**, especially in the eastern, northern and southern parts. The divergence is starkest when we compare Vedic and Puranic traditions: the Vedic deities **Agni, Indra and Soma** became **marginal figures**, while **Vishnu, Shiva and the goddess** of the elaborate Puranic mythologies have little in common with their brief appearances in Vedic mantras — yet **the Vedas continued to be revered** throughout.

## 2. Poems of Prayer — Early Traditions of Bhakti
In many traditions of worship, **poet-saints emerged as leaders** around whom there developed a **community of devotees**. While Brahmanas remained important intermediaries between gods and devotees in several forms of bhakti, these traditions **accommodated and acknowledged women and the "lower castes"** — categories considered **ineligible for liberation within the orthodox Brahmanical framework**. Historians of religion classify bhakti into two broad categories: **saguna bhakti** (worship **with attributes**, focused on specific deities — Shiva, Vishnu and his avatars, and forms of the goddess or Devi, often in anthropomorphic forms) and **nirguna bhakti** (worship of an **abstract, formless god**).

## 3. The Alvars and Nayanars of Tamil Nadu
Some of the earliest bhakti movements (**c. sixth century**) were led by the **Alvars** (literally, those "**immersed**" in devotion to **Vishnu**) and the **Nayanars** (leaders who were devotees of **Shiva**), who **travelled from place to place singing hymns in Tamil** in praise of their gods. During their travels they identified certain **shrines as abodes of their chosen deities**, and very often **large temples were later built at these sacred places**, developing into **centres of pilgrimage**; singing the compositions of these poet-saints became part of **temple rituals**, as did **worship of the saints' images**.

**Attitudes towards caste:** some historians suggest the Alvars and Nayanars **initiated a protest against the caste system and the dominance of Brahmanas**, or at least attempted to reform it — corroborated by the fact that **bhaktas hailed from diverse social backgrounds**, ranging from Brahmanas to artisans, cultivators and even castes considered "untouchable." The importance of their traditions was indicated by the claim that their compositions were **as important as the Vedas**: the **Nalayira Divyaprabandham** ("Four Thousand Sacred Compositions"), the anthology of the **12 Alvars**, was frequently described as the "**Tamil Veda**," claiming parity with the four Sanskrit Vedas cherished by the Brahmanas. By the tenth century, the poems of **Appar, Sambandar and Sundarar** were compiled and classified as the **Tevaram** — on the basis of the **music** of the songs.

**Source box — the chaturvedin and the "outcaste":** the Alvar **Tondaradippodi**, himself a Brahmana, tells Vishnu: "You manifestly like those 'servants' who express their love for your feet, **though they may be born outcastes, more than the Chaturvedins** (Brahmanas versed in the four Vedas) who are strangers and without allegiance to your service." Compare this with **Appar**, who tells the devotee to bow to **Marperu's lord (Shiva, in Thanjavur) as your sole refuge** — proof that devotion mattered more than birth.

**Women devotees:** a most striking feature of these traditions was the presence of women. **Andal**, a woman Alvar, saw herself as the **beloved of Vishnu**, and her verses — still sung today — express her love for the deity. **Karaikkal Ammaiyar**, a devotee of Shiva, adopted the path of **extreme asceticism**; she described herself as the **female Pey (demoness) with bulging veins, protruding eyes, white teeth, shrunken stomach, red hair and jutting teeth**, wandering the forest of Alankatu, home of Shiva "who dances with his matted hair thrown in all eight directions." These women **renounced their social obligations but did not join an alternative order or become nuns**; their very existence and compositions **posed a challenge to patriarchal norms**.

**Relations with the state:** from the second half of the first millennium, the **Pallavas and Pandyas (c. sixth to ninth centuries CE)** ruled the Tamil region. **Buddhism and Jainism** had long been prevalent there, drawing support from **merchant and artisan communities**, and received occasional royal patronage — and one major theme of Tamil bhakti hymns is the **poets' opposition to Buddhism and Jainism**, especially marked among the Nayanars; historians explain this hostility as **competition between religious traditions for royal patronage**. The powerful **Cholas (ninth to thirteenth centuries)** supported Brahmanical and bhakti traditions, **making land grants and constructing temples** for Vishnu and Shiva — some of the most magnificent Shiva temples at **Chidambaram, Thanjavur and Gangaikondacholapuram** were built under their patronage, and this was the period of the most spectacular **bronze sculptures of Shiva**. Both Nayanars and Alvars were revered by the **Vellala peasants**, so rulers tried to win their support: Chola kings built splendid temples adorned with stone and metal sculpture to recreate the visions of these popular saints, **introduced the singing of Tamil Shaiva hymns in temples**, took the initiative to **collect and organise the Tevaram**, and — by inscriptional evidence from **c. 945** — **Parantaka I consecrated metal images of Appar, Sambandar and Sundarar** in a temple.

## 4. The Virashaiva Tradition in Karnataka
In the **twelfth century**, a new movement emerged in Karnataka, led by a Brahmana named **Basavanna (1106–68)**, a **minister in the court of a Kalachuri ruler**. His followers were known as **Virashaivas** ("heroes of Shiva") or **Lingayats** ("wearers of the linga"), and they remain an important community to date. They worship Shiva in his manifestation as a **linga**, and men usually wear a **small linga in a silver case on a loop strung over the left shoulder**; those revered include the **jangama**, or wandering monks. Lingayats believe that **on death the devotee will be united with Shiva and will not return to this world** — therefore they **do not practise funerary rites such as cremation** prescribed by the Dharmashastras, and instead **ceremonially bury their dead**. They **challenged the idea of caste and the "pollution" attributed to certain groups**, and **questioned the theory of rebirth** — which won them followers among those marginalised within the Brahmanical social order. They also encouraged practices disapproved by the Dharmashastras, such as **post-puberty marriage and the remarriage of widows**. Our understanding of the tradition comes from the **vachanas** (literally, "sayings") composed in **Kannada** by the women and men who joined the movement.

Two further developments matter: many ideas of the **Tamil bhaktas, especially the Vaishnavas**, were incorporated into the Sanskritic tradition, culminating in the composition of the **Bhagavata Purana**; and traditions of bhakti developed in **Maharashtra in the thirteenth century**.

## 5. Religious Ferment in North India
In north India, Vishnu and Shiva were worshipped in temples often built with the support of rulers, but historians have found **no evidence of compositions resembling those of the Alvars and Nayanars until the fourteenth century**. Why? In this period several **Rajput states** emerged, and in most of them **Brahmanas occupied positions of importance**, performing a range of secular and ritual functions — there was **little or no attempt to challenge their position directly**. At the same time, other religious leaders — the **Naths, Jogis and Siddhas** — were gaining ground. Many came from **artisanal groups, including weavers**, who were becoming increasingly important with **organised craft production**, whose demand grew with **new urban centres and long-distance trade with Central Asia and West Asia**. These leaders **questioned the authority of the Vedas** and expressed themselves in **languages spoken by ordinary people** (which developed into today's languages), but they **could not win the support of the ruling elites**. A new element was the coming of the **Turks**, which culminated in the **Delhi Sultanate (thirteenth century)** — this undermined the power of many Rajput states and of the Brahmanas associated with them.

## 6. New Strands in the Fabric — Islamic Traditions
**Faiths of rulers and subjects:** in **711**, an Arab general named **Muhammad Qasim conquered Sind**, which became part of the **Caliph's domain**. In the **thirteenth century** the **Turks and Afghans established the Delhi Sultanate**, followed by Sultanates in the Deccan and elsewhere; Islam was an acknowledged religion of rulers in several areas, and this continued with the **Mughal Empire in the sixteenth century** and many regional states of the eighteenth. Theoretically, Muslim rulers were to be guided by the **ulama**, who were expected to ensure that they ruled according to the **shari'a** — the law governing the Muslim community, which evolved from the **Qur'an, the hadis** (traditions of the Prophet), and later also **qiyas** (reasoning by analogy) and **ijma** (consensus of the community). But the subcontinent had huge non-Muslim populations, so the category of the **zimmi** ("protected," from the Arabic *zimma*) developed for peoples of **revealed scriptures — Jews and Christians** — living under Muslim rulership: they **paid a tax called jizya** and gained the right to be protected by Muslims, and in **India this status was extended to Hindus as well**. In effect, rulers adopted fairly flexible policies: several gave **land endowments and tax exemptions** to **Hindu, Jaina, Zoroastrian, Christian and Jewish** religious institutions, and expressed respect and devotion towards non-Muslim religious leaders — including Mughal rulers like **Akbar and Aurangzeb**.

**Source box — two imperial farmans/letters:** in **1598 Akbar issued a farman** permitting the **padris (fathers) of the Holy Society of Jesus to build a church** in **Kambayat (Khambat, Gujarat)**, ordering the city's dignitaries to "in no case stand in their way." And in **1661–62 Aurangzeb wrote a letter of reverence** to **Shiv Murat, Guru Anand Nath Jio, a Jogi**, offering service — evidence that even the most "orthodox" Mughal respected non-Muslim holy men.

**The popular practice of Islam:** the coming of Islam was not confined to ruling elites — it permeated **peasants, artisans, warriors and merchants**. All who adopted Islam accepted in principle the **five "pillars" of the faith**: **shahada** (there is one God, Allah, and Prophet Muhammad is his messenger), **namaz/salat** (prayers five times a day), **zakat** (giving alms), **sawm** (fasting during Ramzan), and **hajj** (pilgrimage to Mecca). But these universal features were overlaid with **diversities from sectarian affiliations (Sunni, Shi'a) and local customary practices**. The **Khojahs**, a branch of the **Ismailis (a Shi'a sect)**, developed new modes of communication, disseminating ideas derived from the Qur'an through the **ginan** (from Sanskrit *jnana*, "knowledge") — devotional poems in **Punjabi, Multani, Sindhi, Kachchi, Hindi and Gujarati**, sung in special ragas at daily prayer meetings, transmitted orally and later recorded in the **Khojaki script** derived from the local **landa** ("clipped" mercantile script). **Arab Muslim traders who settled on the Malabar coast (Kerala) adopted the local language, Malayalam**, and local customs such as **matriliny and matrilocal residence**. The blend of universal faith with local tradition is best seen in **mosque architecture**: universal features like **orientation towards Mecca, the mihrab (prayer niche) and the minbar (pulpit)** coexist with wide variations in roofs and building materials — a **thirteenth-century Kerala mosque with a shikhara-like roof**, the **Shah Hamadan mosque in Srinagar (built 1395)**, "jewel in the crown" of Kashmiri wooden architecture decorated with papier mache, and the **brick Atiya mosque in Mymensingh district, Bangladesh (1609)**.

**Names for communities:** historians studying Sanskrit texts and inscriptions of the **eighth to fourteenth centuries** point out that the term **musalman or Muslim was virtually never used**. People were identified by their region: the Turkish rulers were **Turushka**, **Tajika** were people from Tajikistan, **Parashika** were people from Persia; sometimes older terms were recycled — Turks and Afghans were called **Shakas** and **Yavanas** (a term for Greeks). A general term for migrant communities was **mlechchha**, indicating that they did not observe the norms of caste society and spoke languages not derived from Sanskrit — sometimes derogatory, but **rarely denoting a distinct religious community of Muslims in opposition to Hindus**; and the term "Hindu" itself was used in a variety of ways, not necessarily religious.

## 7. The Growth of Sufism
In the early centuries of Islam, religious-minded people called **sufis** turned to **asceticism and mysticism in protest against the growing materialism of the Caliphate** as a religious and political institution. They were critical of the **dogmatic definitions and scholastic methods** of interpreting the Qur'an and **sunna** adopted by theologians, and instead laid emphasis on seeking **salvation through intense devotion and love for God**, following His commands and the example of the **Prophet Muhammad, whom they regarded as a perfect human being** — seeking an interpretation of the Qur'an on the basis of **personal experience**.

**Khanqahs and silsilas:** by the **eleventh century** Sufism was a well-developed movement with its own literature. Institutionally, sufis organised communities around the **hospice or khanqah** (Persian), controlled by a teaching master known as **shaikh** (Arabic) or **pir / murshid** (Persian), who enrolled **disciples (murids)** and appointed a **successor (khalifa)**, establishing rules for spiritual conduct. **Sufi silsilas** began to crystallise around the **twelfth century**; **silsila** literally means a **chain**, signifying a **continuous link between master and disciple, stretching as an unbroken spiritual genealogy to the Prophet Muhammad** — through this channel spiritual power and blessings were transmitted. Special rituals of initiation included an **oath of allegiance, a patched garment, and shaving of hair**. When the shaikh died, his **tomb-shrine (dargah** — a Persian term meaning **court**) became the centre of devotion, encouraging pilgrimage or **ziyarat** to his grave, especially on his **death anniversary or urs** (literally, marriage — signifying **union with God**). A **wali** (plural **auliya**) was a "friend of God" who claimed **proximity to Allah, acquiring His grace (barakat) to perform miracles (karamat)**.

## 8. The Chishtis in the Subcontinent
The **Chishti silsila** was the most influential in the subcontinent because it adapted brilliantly to the local environment and adopted several features of Indian devotional traditions. Its great teachers and dargahs form a chain to memorise: **Shaikh Muinuddin Sijzi (d. 1235, Ajmer)**, **Khwaja Qutbuddin Bakhtiyar Kaki (d. 1235, Delhi)**, **Shaikh Fariduddin Ganj-i Shakar (d. 1265, Ajodhan, in Pakistan)**, **Shaikh Nizamuddin Auliya (d. 1325, Delhi)** and **Shaikh Nasiruddin Chiragh-i Dehli (d. 1356, Delhi)**.

**Life in the Chishti khanqah:** Shaikh Nizamuddin's hospice had several small rooms and a **big hall (jama'at khana)**, where the residents — **sufis, their followers and visitors** — lived and prayed together; the Shaikh himself lived in a **small room on the roof** of the hall and met visitors in the morning and evening. A veranda surrounded the courtyard and a boundary wall ran around the complex — once, fearing a **Mongol invasion**, people from neighbouring areas **flocked to the khanqah for refuge**. There was an **open kitchen (langar) run on futuh** — unasked-for charity. From morning till late night, people from all walks of life — **soldiers, slaves, singers, merchants, poets, travellers, rich and poor, Hindu jogis and qalandars** — came seeking discipleship, **amulets for healing**, and the Shaikh's **intercession**. Visitors included the poets **Amir Hasan Sijzi and Amir Khusrau** and the court historian **Ziyauddin Barani**, all of whom wrote about the Shaikh. Practices such as **bowing before the Shaikh, offering water to visitors, shaving the heads of initiates, and yogic exercises** represented attempts to **assimilate local traditions**. Nizamuddin appointed **several spiritual successors** and deputed them to set up hospices across the subcontinent, so the teachings, practices and fame of the Chishtis spread rapidly.

**Ziyarat and qawwali:** pilgrimage to sufi tombs, **ziyarat**, is an occasion for seeking the sufi's spiritual grace, **barakat** — for over **seven centuries** people of various creeds, classes and backgrounds have visited the dargahs of the **five great Chishti saints**. The most revered shrine is that of **Khwaja Muinuddin at Ajmer**, popularly known as **"Gharib Nawaz" — "comforter of the poor."** The earliest textual references to the dargah date to the **fourteenth century**; it was popular because of the Shaikh's austerity and piety, the greatness of his spiritual successors, and **royal patronage** — **Muhammad bin Tughlaq (ruled 1324–51) was the first Sultan to visit**, the earliest construction over the tomb was funded in the **late fifteenth century by Sultan Ghiyasuddin Khalji of Malwa**, and its location on the **trade route linking Delhi and Gujarat** brought travellers. By the **sixteenth century** the shrine was so popular that the spirited singing of pilgrims bound for Ajmer inspired **Akbar to visit the tomb fourteen times** (until **1580**), seeking blessings for conquests, fulfilment of vows and the birth of sons — in **1568 he offered a huge cauldron (degh)** for cooking for pilgrims and had a **mosque built in the compound**.

**Source box — Jahanara's pilgrimage, 1643:** the Mughal princess **Jahanara**, in her biography of Shaikh Muinuddin Chishti titled **Munis al Arwah** ("The Confidant of Spirits"), describes travelling from Agra with her father **Shah Jahan**; she resolved to perform **two cycles of optional prayer every day**, "did not sleep on a leopard skin at night," refused to point her feet towards the blessed sanctuary, went **barefoot** from the doorway, "rubbed my pale face with the dust of that threshold," **circled the tomb seven times**, applied the finest **itar** with her own hand, and placed her **rose scarf** on the tomb. This single extract is a treasure for exams: a royal woman, a Persian-style biography, and gestures of devotion.

**Music:** part of ziyarat is the use of **music and dance**, including mystical chants performed by specially trained musicians or **qawwals** to evoke **divine ecstasy**. Sufis remember God either by reciting the **zikr** (the Divine Names) or through **sama'** (literally, "**audition**") — mystical music; **sama' was integral to the Chishtis** and exemplified their interaction with indigenous devotional traditions. **Source box:** the eighteenth-century Deccan visitor **Dargah Quli Khan**, in his **Muraqqa-i Dehli** ("Album of Delhi"), wrote of the shrine of **Nasiruddin Chiragh-i Dehli** that the Shaikh in the grave is "not the lamp of Delhi but of the entire country" — crowds came on Sundays, and in the month of **Diwali** the entire population of Delhi visited, staying in tents around the spring tank, **taking baths to obtain cures from chronic diseases — "Muslims and Hindus pay visits in the same spirit."** (Also remember **Data Ganj Bakhsh**: Abu'l Hasan al Hujwiri, a native of Hujwir near Ghazni, came to Lahore as a captive in **1039**, wrote the **Kashf-ul-Mahjub** ("Unveiling of the Veiled") in Persian to explain **tasawwuf**, died in **1073**, and his Lahore tomb-shrine became the **Data Darbar** — "Court of the Giver.")

**Languages and communication:** the Chishtis adopted local languages beyond sama'. In Delhi they conversed in **Hindavi**, the language of the people. **Baba Farid composed verses in the local language which were incorporated into the Guru Granth Sahib**. Others composed **masnavis** (long poems) expressing divine love through human love as allegory — the **prem-akhyan (love story) Padmavat composed by Malik Muhammad Jayasi**, revolving around the romance of **Padmini and Ratansen, king of Chittor**, their trials symbolic of the soul's journey to the divine — often recited in hospices during sama'. Around **Bijapur (Karnataka)**, Chishti sufis of the **seventeenth and eighteenth centuries** composed short poems in **Dakhani** (a variant of Urdu), probably **sung by women while grinding grain and spinning**, including **lurinama (lullabies) and shadinama (wedding songs)** — likely inspired by the **Kannada vachanas of the Lingayats and the Marathi abhangs of the sants of Pandharpur**.

**Sufis and the state:** a major feature of the Chishti tradition was **austerity, including maintaining a distance from worldly power** — but this was by no means absolute isolation. The sufis **accepted unsolicited grants and donations** from political elites; the Sultans set up **charitable trusts (auqaf)** for hospices and granted **tax-free land (inam)**. The Chishtis accepted donations in cash and kind but **used them fully on immediate requirements** — food, clothes, living quarters and ritual necessities like sama' — which **enhanced the moral authority of the shaikhs**. Their piety, scholarship and the belief in their **miraculous powers** made sufis popular among the masses, **whose support kings wished to secure**; kings needed **legitimation from them**. When the Turks set up the Delhi Sultanate, they **resisted the ulama's insistence on imposing shari'a as state law**, anticipating opposition from their largely non-Muslim subjects — instead they sought out sufis, who **derived their authority directly from God** and did not depend on jurists to interpret the shari'a. Since the **auliya could intercede with God**, kings often wanted **their tombs in the vicinity of sufi shrines and hospices**. There were conflicts too: both Sultans and sufis expected rituals like **prostration and kissing of the feet**, and Nizamuddin Auliya's disciples addressed him as **sultan-ul-mashaikh** ("Sultan amongst shaikhs"). Other silsilas — the **Suhrawardi under the Delhi Sultans and the Naqshbandi under the Mughals** — were also associated with the state, and some sufis accepted courtly offices.

**Source box — declining a royal gift (1313):** **Amir Hasan Sijzi** describes a local ruler sending Shaikh Nizamuddin Auliya the **deed of ownership to two gardens and much land**; the Shaikh refused, lamenting, "**What have I to do with gardens and fields and lands? None of our spiritual masters had engaged in such activity**," and told the story of Sultan **Ghiyasuddin (then Ulugh Khan)** offering money and deeds for four villages to **Shaikh Fariduddin**, who took the money for the dervishes but said of the land deeds: "**Give them away to such persons as long for them.**" A perfect illustration of Chishti austerity with nuance. Also remember the **dargah of Shaikh Salim Chishti (a descendant of Baba Farid) at Fatehpur Sikri**, built by Akbar — it symbolised the **bond between the Chishtis and the Mughal state**.

## 9. New Devotional Paths — Dialogue and Dissent in Northern India
**Weaving a divine fabric: Kabir** (c. fourteenth–fifteenth centuries) is perhaps the most outstanding poet-saint of this milieu, and reconstructing his life is challenging. Verses ascribed to him survive in **three distinct but overlapping traditions**: the **Kabir Bijak**, preserved by the **Kabirpanth** in **Varanasi** and elsewhere in Uttar Pradesh; the **Kabir Granthavali**, associated with the **Dadupanth in Rajasthan**; and many compositions in the **Adi Granth Sahib**. **All these manuscripts were compiled long after his death**, and by the nineteenth century anthologies circulated in print as far apart as **Bengal, Gujarat and Maharashtra**. His poems survive in several languages and dialects: some are in the **sant bhasha**, the special language of nirguna poets; others are **ulatbansi** ("upside-down sayings") in which everyday meanings are inverted — the "**lotus which blooms without flower**" or the "**fire raging in the ocean**" — hinting at the difficulty of capturing the Ultimate Reality in words. Striking is the range of traditions he drew on to describe the Ultimate Reality: **Islamic terms (Allah, Khuda, Hazrat, Pir)**, **Vedantic terms (alakh the unseen, nirakar the formless, Brahman, Atman)**, and **yogic terms (shabda, sound; shunya, emptiness)**. Some poems use **Islamic monotheism and iconoclasm to attack Hindu polytheism and idol worship**; others use the **sufi concepts of zikr and ishq (love)** to express the Hindu practice of **nam-simaran** (remembrance of God's name). Were all these composed by Kabir? **We may never tell with certainty** — and his legacy was claimed by many groups: hagiographies composed from the **seventeenth century onwards (about 200 years after his lifetime)** debated whether he was Hindu or Muslim by birth, with **Vaishnava hagiographies attempting to claim him as a Hindu disciple of Ramananda**; such traditions **cannot be accepted at face value, but they show how important his legacy was**.

**Source box — "The One Lord":** "Tell me, brother, how can there be / No one lord of the world but two?... God is called by many names: / Names like **Allah, Ram, Karim, Keshav, Hari, and Hazrat**. / **Gold may be shaped into rings and bangles. / Isn't it gold all the same?**... One kills the goat, the other cows. / They waste their lives in disputation." This is the exam-favourite Kabir extract.

**Baba Guru Nanak (1469–1539)** was born in a **Hindu merchant family in a village called Nankana Sahib near the river Ravi** in the predominantly Muslim **Punjab**. He **trained to be an accountant and studied Persian**, was married at a young age, but spent most of his time among **sufis and bhaktas** and **travelled widely**. His hymns and teachings suggest he advocated a form of **nirguna bhakti**: he firmly **repudiated the external practices** of the religions around him — **rejecting sacrifices, ritual baths, image worship, austerities, and the scriptures of both Hindus and Muslims**. For him the Absolute or "**rab**" had **no gender or form**; the way to connect with the Divine was **remembering and repeating the Divine Name**, expressed through hymns called "**shabad**" in **Punjabi**, sung in various ragas while his attendant **Mardana played the rabab**. He organised his followers into a community with rules for **congregational worship (sangat)** involving collective recitation, and **appointed his disciple Angad to succeed him as preceptor (guru)** — a practice followed for nearly 200 years. **He did not wish to establish a new religion**, but after his death his followers consolidated their own practices, distinguishing themselves from both Hindus and Muslims. The **fifth preceptor, Guru Arjan, compiled Baba Guru Nanak's hymns along with those of his four successors and other religious poets — Baba Farid, Ravidas (Raidas) and Kabir — into the Adi Granth Sahib**; these hymns, called "**gurbani**," are in various languages. In the **late seventeenth century**, the tenth preceptor **Guru Gobind Singh** included the compositions of the ninth guru, **Guru Tegh Bahadur**, whereupon the scripture came to be called the **Guru Granth Sahib**; **Guru Gobind Singh also laid the foundation of the Khalsa Panth (army of the pure)** and defined its **five symbols: uncut hair, a dagger, a pair of shorts, a comb and a steel bangle** — under him the community consolidated as a **socio-religious and military force**.

**Mirabai** (c. fifteenth–sixteenth centuries) is perhaps the **best-known woman poet within the bhakti tradition**; her biography has been reconstructed primarily from the **bhajans attributed to her, transmitted orally for centuries**. She was a **Rajput princess from Merta in Marwar**, married against her wishes to a **prince of the Sisodia clan of Mewar, Rajasthan**. She **defied her husband**, did not submit to the traditional role of wife and mother, and instead recognised **Krishna, the avatar of Vishnu, as her lover**; her **in-laws tried to poison her**, but she escaped the palace to live as a **wandering saint** composing songs of **intense emotional expression**. **According to some traditions her preceptor was Raidas, a leather worker** — indicating her **defiance of the norms of caste society**; after rejecting the comforts of her husband's palace she is supposed to have donned **the white robes of a widow or the saffron robe of the renouncer**. **Source box:** "What can Mewar's ruler do to me? / If God is angry, all is lost, / But what can the Rana do?" — and the pyre song: "**I will build a funeral pyre of sandalwood and aloe; light it by your own hand... let flame be lost in flame.**"

## 10. Reconstructing Histories of Religious Traditions
Historians draw on **sculpture, architecture, stories about religious preceptors, and compositions** attributed to women and men in the quest for the Divine — written in several languages and styles, from the **simple, direct vachanas of Basavanna** to the **ornate Persian of Mughal farmans**; each type of text requires different skills. Remember **Shankaradeva**: in the **late fifteenth century** he emerged as a leading proponent of **Vaishnavism in Assam**; his teachings, known as the **Bhagavati dharma** (based on the **Bhagavad Gita and the Bhagavata Purana**), focused on **absolute surrender to Vishnu**, emphasised **naam kirtan** — recitation of the names of the lord in **sat sanga** (congregations of pious devotees) — and encouraged **satra (monasteries) and naam ghar (prayer halls)**; his major composition is the **Kirtana-ghosha**.

**The four varieties of sufi textual sources (learn this as a set):** First, **treatises or manuals** of sufi thought and practice — the **Kashf-ul-Mahjub of Ali bin Usman Hujwiri (d. c. 1071)**, which shows how traditions outside the subcontinent influenced Indian sufi thought. Second, **malfuzat** (literally, "uttered" — **conversations of sufi saints**), the classic example being the **Fawa'id-al-Fu'ad**, conversations of **Shaikh Nizamuddin Auliya compiled by Amir Hasan Sijzi Dehlavi**, compiled with the shaikhs' permission for didactic purposes and found across the subcontinent including the Deccan. Third, **maktubat** (literally, "written" — **collections of letters** written by sufi masters to disciples), the most discussed being the **Maktubat-i Imam Rabbani of the seventeenth-century Naqshbandi shaikh Ahmad Sirhindi (d. 1624)**, whose ideology is often **contrasted with the liberal and non-sectarian views of Akbar**. Fourth, **tazkiras** (literally, "to mention and memorialise" — **biographical accounts of saints**): the **Siyar-ul-Auliya of Mir Khwurd Kirmani** (fourteenth century) was the **first sufi tazkira written in India**, dealing principally with Chishti saints, while the **most famous is the Akhbar-ul-Akhyar of Abdul Haqq Muhaddis Dehlavi (d. 1642)**. Tazkira authors sought to establish **the precedence of their own orders and glorify their spiritual genealogies**, so many details are implausible or fantastic — yet they remain **of great value to historians**. The final caution: these traditions **continue to flourish today**, which lets historians compare living practice with texts and paintings, but there is often **no popular acceptance that these traditions may have changed over time** — the historian's challenge is to investigate with **sensitivity**, recognising that religious traditions are **dynamic and change over time**.

# 🟩 THEME 7: AN IMPERIAL CAPITAL — VIJAYANAGARA (c. 14th–16th century)

## 🎬 The story of the chapter
Vijayanagara — the "city of victory" — was both a city and an empire. After its sack in 1565 it fell to ruin, survived in oral memory as Hampi, and was slowly reconstructed by an engineer's map, photographers' lenses, epigraphists' notebooks and travellers' accounts. The chapter is detective work in stone: water tanks tell of engineering, fortifications tell of sieges, the Mahanavami platform tells of royal spectacle, and temples tell of kingship.

## 1. Vijayanagara, Hampi and the Discovery of the Ruins
**Vijayanagara**, meaning "**city of victory**," was the name of **both a city and an empire**; the empire was founded in the **fourteenth century**, and in its heyday stretched **from the river Krishna in the north to the extreme south of the peninsula**. In **1565 the city was sacked and subsequently deserted**, and although it fell into ruin in the seventeenth–eighteenth centuries, it **lived on in the memories of people living in the Krishna-Tungabhadra doab**, who remembered it as **Hampi** — a name derived from the local mother goddess, **Pampadevi**. These **oral traditions, combined with archaeological finds, monuments and inscriptions**, helped scholars rediscover the empire. The ruins were brought to light in **1800 by Colonel Colin Mackenzie**, an **engineer and antiquarian employed by the English East India Company**, who prepared the **first survey map of the site**; much of his initial information came from the **memories of priests of the Virupaksha temple and the shrine of Pampadevi**. Subsequently, **from 1856 photographers began recording the monuments**, and as early as **1836 epigraphists began collecting inscriptions**; historians collated all this with **accounts of foreign travellers** and literature in **Telugu, Kannada, Tamil and Sanskrit**.

**Source box — Colin Mackenzie:** born **1754**, he became famous as an engineer, surveyor and cartographer; in **1815 he was appointed the first Surveyor General of India**, holding the post until his death in **1821**. He collected local histories and surveyed historic sites to make governance of the colony easier — believing that studying Vijayanagara would give the East India Company "**much useful information on many of these institutions, laws and customs whose influence still prevails**."

## 2. Rayas, Nayakas and Sultans
According to **tradition and epigraphic evidence, two brothers, Harihara and Bukka, founded the Vijayanagara Empire in 1336**. The empire included peoples who spoke different languages and followed different religious traditions, and on its northern frontier its kings competed with the **Sultans of the Deccan and the Gajapati rulers of Orissa** for control of the **fertile river valleys** and the resources of **lucrative overseas trade** — yet the interaction also led to a **sharing of ideas, especially in architecture**, with Vijayanagara borrowing concepts and building techniques and then developing them further. The rulers called themselves **rayas**, building on the temple-patronage traditions of earlier powers — the **Cholas in Tamil Nadu and the Hoysalas in Karnataka**, who had patronised temples like the **Brihadishvara at Thanjavur and the Chennakeshava at Belur**. Remember the popular titles: **Gajapati** means "lord of elephants" (the Orissa lineage), the **Deccan Sultans were termed ashvapati — "lords of horses" — and the rayas were called narapati — "lord of men."**

**Kings and traders:** since warfare depended upon **effective cavalry**, the **import of horses from Arabia and Central Asia** was vital; this trade was initially controlled by **Arab traders**, with local merchant communities called **kudirai chettis ("horse merchants")** also participating. **From 1498 the Portuguese** arrived on the west coast, establishing trading and military stations — their **superior military technology, especially muskets**, made them important players in the tangled politics of the period. Vijayanagara was noted for its markets in **spices, textiles and precious stones**; trade was a **status symbol** for cities with wealthy populations demanding high-value exotic goods, and the revenue from trade contributed significantly to the prosperity of the state.

**Source box — Krishnadeva Raya on trade:** the most famous ruler of Vijayanagara, **Krishnadeva Raya (ruled 1509–29)**, composed a work on statecraft in Telugu called the **Amuktamalyada**, advising that a king should **improve the harbours of his country and encourage commerce** so that "horses, elephants, precious gems, sandalwood, pearls and other articles are freely imported"; he should look after **foreign sailors who land because of storms, illness and exhaustion**, and **attach foreign merchants to himself through daily audience, presents and decent profits — "then those articles will never go to your enemies."**

**The dynasties:** the **first dynasty, the Sangama, exercised control till 1485**; they were supplanted by the **Saluvas — military commanders — who remained in power till 1503**, when they were replaced by the **Tuluvas**; **Krishnadeva Raya belonged to the Tuluva dynasty**. His rule was characterised by **expansion and consolidation**: the land between the Tungabhadra and Krishna rivers (**the Raichur doab**) was acquired in **1512**, the rulers of **Orissa were subdued in 1514**, and **severe defeats were inflicted on the Sultan of Bijapur (1520)**; he also founded a suburb called **Nagalapuram, named after his mother**. Some of the most detailed descriptions of the city come from his time or just after. Strain showed after his death in **1529**: successors were troubled by **rebellious nayakas**, and by **1542 control at the centre had shifted to the Aravidu dynasty**, which remained in power till the end of the seventeenth century and ruled from **Penukonda and later Chandragiri (near Tirupati)**. The **alliance of the Sultanates against Vijayanagara** culminated in **1565**, when **Rama Raya, the chief minister, led the army at Rakshasi-Tangadi (also known as Talikota)**, where his forces were routed — the Sultans then **sacked and destroyed the city**. Yet relations were not inevitably hostile: **Krishnadeva Raya supported some claimants to power in the Sultanates and took pride in the title "establisher of the Yavana kingdom"**; the **Sultan of Bijapur intervened to resolve Vijayanagara's succession disputes** after KDR's death; and it was the "**adventurous policy of Rama Raya, who tried to play off one Sultan against another**, that led the Sultans to combine and decisively defeat him."

**The rayas and the nayakas:** among those who exercised power were **military chiefs who controlled forts and had armed supporters**, known as **nayakas**; they usually spoke **Telugu or Kannada**, often moved from area to area **accompanied by peasants looking for fertile land**, and although they submitted to the kings they **often rebelled and had to be subdued by military action**. The **amara-nayaka system** was a **major political innovation of the empire**, with many features likely **derived from the iqta system of the Delhi Sultanate**: **amara-nayakas were military commanders given territories to govern by the raya**; they **collected taxes and dues from peasants, craftspersons and traders**, **retained part of the revenue** for personal use and for maintaining a **stipulated contingent of horses and elephants**, and used some revenue for **temples and irrigation works**. Their contingents gave the kings an **effective fighting force** that brought the entire southern peninsula under control. The amara-nayakas **sent tribute to the king annually** and **personally appeared in the royal court with gifts** to express loyalty; kings asserted control by **transferring them from place to place**. But in the **seventeenth century many nayakas established independent kingdoms**, hastening the **collapse of the central imperial structure**. (Note: **amara** is believed to be derived from the Sanskrit **samara**, battle or war, and resembles the Persian **amir**, a high noble.)

## 3. The Capital and its Environs — Water, Forts, Roads and the Urban Core
**Water resources:** the most striking feature of the location is the **natural basin formed by the river Tungabhadra**, which flows in a **north-easterly** direction, surrounded by **stunning granite hills** that form a girdle around the city, with **streams flowing down from rocky outcrops**. Since this is **one of the most arid zones of the peninsula**, elaborate arrangements stored rainwater and conducted it to the city: embankments along streams created **reservoirs of varying sizes**; the most important tank, the **Kamalapuram tank** (built in the early years of the **fifteenth century**), both **irrigated nearby fields** and fed water **through a channel to the "royal centre"**; and the **Hiriya canal** — "apparently built by kings of the Sangama dynasty" — drew water from a **dam across the Tungabhadra** and irrigated the cultivated valley separating the **"sacred centre" from the "urban core."** **Source box:** **Domingo Paes** describes a tank built by Krishnadeva Raya at the mouth of two hills, with water carried **from more than three leagues (approximately 15 kilometres) by pipes**, three large carved pillars, and a hill "broken down" for it — with **"so many people at work that there must have been fifteen or twenty thousand men, looking like ants."**

**Fortifications and roads:** **Abdur Razzaq, an ambassador sent by the ruler of Persia to Calicut in the fifteenth century**, was greatly impressed and mentioned **seven lines of forts**, encircling **not only the city but also its agricultural hinterland and forests**. The outermost wall linked the surrounding hills; the massive masonry was **slightly tapered, with no mortar or cementing agent** — the **wedge-shaped stone blocks held themselves in place**, the inner portion was **earth packed with rubble**, and square or rectangular **bastions projected outwards**. What was most significant is that the fortification **enclosed agricultural tracts**: Razzaq noted that "**between the first, second and the third walls there are cultivated fields, gardens and houses**," and Paes described fields of rice, gardens and two lakes — findings **corroborated by present-day archaeologists**. Why? Because medieval **sieges aimed to starve defenders into submission** and could last months or years; rather than just building granaries, the rulers of Vijayanagara adopted a **more expensive and elaborate strategy of protecting the agricultural belt itself**. A **second line of fortification** went round the inner urban core, a **third** surrounded the royal centre, and each major building had its own high walls. The fort was entered through **well-guarded gates**; the **arch on the gateway and the dome over it are regarded as typical features introduced by the Turkish Sultans**, and this style is called **Indo-Islamic**, as it grew through interaction with local building practices. Roads **wound through the valleys, avoiding rocky terrain**, and the most important roads **extended from temple gateways and were lined by bazaars**.

**The urban core:** there is relatively little archaeological evidence of ordinary people's houses, but **fine Chinese porcelain** in the **north-eastern corner of the urban core** suggests occupation by **rich traders** — this was also the **Muslim residential quarter**, where **tombs and mosques have architecture resembling the mandapas of Hampi's temples**. The sixteenth-century Portuguese traveller **Barbosa** described ordinary houses as "**thatched, but nonetheless well built and arranged according to occupations, in long streets with many open places.**" Field surveys show the area was **dotted with numerous shrines and small temples** — pointing to a **variety of cults, perhaps supported by different communities** — while **wells, rainwater tanks and temple tanks** served ordinary town dwellers.

## 4. The Royal Centre
The royal centre lay in the **south-western part** of the settlement and included **over 60 temples** — clear evidence that **patronage of temples and cults was important for rulers seeking to legitimise their authority** through association with the divinities. About **thirty building complexes have been identified as palaces** — large structures not associated with ritual functions. The key architectural distinction: **temples were constructed entirely of masonry, while the superstructures of secular buildings were of perishable materials** (which is why they vanished).

**The mahanavami dibba:** the "**king's palace**" is the largest enclosure but has **not yielded definitive evidence of being a royal residence**; it contains two impressive platforms — the "**audience hall**" and the "**mahanavami dibba**." The audience hall is a **high platform with slots for wooden pillars at close and regular intervals** and a staircase to a second floor — but the closely spaced pillars left little free space, so its use is unclear. The **mahanavami dibba**, located on **one of the highest points in the city**, is a **massive platform rising from a base of about 11,000 sq. ft to a height of 40 ft**, with evidence that it **supported a wooden structure** and with **relief carvings covering its base**. Rituals associated with it probably coincided with **Mahanavami (literally, the "great ninth day")** of the **ten-day Hindu festival** in autumn (**September–October**) — known as **Dusehra in northern India, Durga Puja in Bengal, and Navaratri or Mahanavami in peninsular India** — on which the Vijayanagara kings **displayed their prestige, power and suzerainty**. The ceremonies included **worship of the image, worship of the state horse, and the sacrifice of buffaloes and other animals**; **dances, wrestling matches, and processions of caparisoned horses, elephants and chariots and soldiers**; and **ritual presentations before the king by the chief nayakas and subordinate kings**. On the **last day** the king **inspected his army and the armies of the nayakas in an open field**, and the nayakas brought **rich gifts and the stipulated tribute**. **Source box:** Paes called the audience hall plus dibba the "**House of Victory**" — "two platforms one above the other, beautifully sculpted," with a **room made of cloth** where the idol had a shrine, and a **dais bearing the throne of state, the crown and the royal anklet**.

**Other buildings:** the **Lotus Mahal** — "one of the most beautiful buildings in the royal centre" — was **named by British travellers in the nineteenth century**; its use is uncertain, but one suggestion (from a **map drawn by Mackenzie**) is that it was a **council chamber where the king met his advisers**; its **arches were probably inspired by Indo-Islamic techniques**, and it had **nine towers — a high central one and eight along the sides**. Close by stands the "**elephant stables**" (the name itself is questioned in the textbook exercises). The **Hazara Rama temple** was **probably meant to be used only by the king and his family**; the images in its central shrine are missing, but **sculpted panels survive, including scenes from the Ramayana on the inner walls of the shrine**. Nayakas later **continued and elaborated the palatial building traditions** — the audience hall at **Madurai** with its arches is an example.

## 5. The Sacred Centre
The **rocky northern end** of the city, on the banks of the Tungabhadra, is the sacred centre. **Choosing a capital:** according to **local tradition, these hills sheltered the monkey kingdom of Vali and Sugriva** mentioned in the **Ramayana**; other traditions say **Pampadevi, the local mother goddess, did penance in these hills in order to marry Virupaksha, the guardian deity of the kingdom, also recognised as a form of Shiva** — and **to this day this marriage is celebrated annually in the Virupaksha temple**. Among the hills are **Jaina temples of the pre-Vijayanagara period**, so the area was associated with **several sacred traditions**. Temple building in the region went back to the **Pallavas, Chalukyas, Hoysalas and Cholas**, and rulers encouraged it as a means of **associating themselves with the divine** — the deity was often explicitly or implicitly identified with the king, and the **king's visits to temples were treated as important state occasions**, with the king accompanied by the important **nayakas** of the empire.

**Gopurams and mandapas:** new features of **immense scale** marked **imperial authority** — best exemplified by the **raya gopurams ("royal gateways") that often dwarfed the towers on the central shrines and signalled the presence of the temple from a great distance**. Their construction required **enormous resources, techniques and skills**; other distinctive features included **mandapas (pavilions)** and **long, pillared corridors** running around the shrines.

**The Virupaksha temple** was built over centuries: inscriptions suggest the **earliest shrine dated to the ninth–tenth centuries**, and it was **substantially enlarged with the establishment of the empire**. The **hall in front of the main shrine was built by Krishnadeva Raya to mark his accession**, decorated with delicately carved pillars, and he is also credited with the **eastern gopuram** — these additions meant the central shrine came to occupy a relatively small part of the complex. The halls served many purposes: some housed **images of gods placed to witness special programmes of music, dance and drama**; others celebrated **the marriages of deities** (the **kalyana mandapa**); others were meant **for the deities to swing in** (swing pavilions) — and **special images, distinct from those in the central shrine, were used on these occasions**.

**The Vitthala temple:** its principal deity was **Vitthala, a form of Vishnu generally worshipped in Maharashtra** — its introduction in Karnataka shows how the rulers **drew on different traditions to create an imperial culture**. The temple has several halls and a **unique shrine designed as a chariot**. A characteristic feature of the complexes is the **chariot streets** that extended **from the temple gopuram in a straight line**, **paved with stone slabs and lined with pillared pavilions in which merchants set up their shops**. (Some of the most spectacular gopurams were also built by the local **nayakas**, e.g. at Madurai.)

## 6. Plotting Palaces, Temples and Bazaars — How the Map Was Made
After Mackenzie's initial surveys, information was pieced together from **travellers' accounts and inscriptions**; through the twentieth century the site was preserved by the **Archaeological Survey of India and the Karnataka Department of Archaeology and Museums**. In **1976, Hampi was recognised as a site of national importance**, and in the **early 1980s a major project** documented the material remains through **extensive and intensive surveys** — over **nearly twenty years, dozens of scholars** from all over the world worked on it. **Mapping** went in steps: the area was divided into **25 squares designated by letters of the alphabet** (one letter was deliberately not used), then each square was **subdivided, and subdivided again**. These painstaking surveys recovered **thousands of structures — from tiny shrines and residences to elaborate temples — plus roads, paths and bazaars**, the bazaars located through **finds of pillar bases and platforms, all that remain of thriving markets**. The archaeologists **John M. Fritz, George Michell and M.S. Nagaraja Rao** remind us to "**imagine a whole series of vanished wooden elements — columns, brackets, beams, ceilings, overhanging eaves, and towers — decorated with plaster and painted, perhaps brightly.**"

**Source box — the bazaars:** **Paes** wrote of streets full of shops with "**diamonds, rubies, emeralds, pearls, seed-pearls and cloths**," and of an **evening fair** selling horses, citrons, limes, oranges and grapes; he called Vijayanagara "**the best-provided city in the world**," with markets "**stocked with provisions such as rice, wheat, grains, India corn and a certain amount of barley and beans, moong, pulses and horse-gram**," all **cheap and abundant**. **Fernao Nuniz** found the markets "**overflowing with abundance of fruits, grapes, oranges, limes, pomegranates, jackfruit and mangoes and all very cheap**," and describes meat on sale — "**mutton, pork, venison, partridges, hares, doves, quail and all kinds of birds, sparrows, rats and cats and lizards**" — in the market of **Bisnaga** (Vijayanagara).

## 7. Questions in Search of Answers
Buildings surviving today tell us **how spaces were organised and used, how they were built, and with what materials and techniques**; fortifications reveal **defence requirements and military preparedness**; comparisons with buildings elsewhere reveal the **spread of ideas and cultural influences**; and buildings convey the **ideas and symbols their builders or patrons wished to project** — symbols we can understand only by **combining information from literature, inscriptions and popular traditions**. But investigations of architecture **cannot tell us what ordinary men, women and children thought** about these impressive buildings, whether they had access to the royal or sacred centres, or what the **workers on these colossal construction projects** felt. Nor do we know **who drew up the plans, where the masons, stonecutters and sculptors came from, or what wages they got** — these questions remain open, and the honest historian admits it.
