# 🟤 THEME 1: BRICKS, BEADS AND BONES — THE HARAPPAN CIVILISATION (5 Marks)

## 🎬 The story of the chapter
A civilisation of planned cities rose along the Indus around 2600 BCE and faded by 1900 BCE. Nobody could read its script, so everything we know comes from archaeology — bricks, drains, beads, burials and seals. The chapter is really two stories: how the Harappans lived, and how archaeologists argued over what their remains meant.

## 1. Terminology, Places and Time
The Harappan Civilisation is also known as the **Indus Valley Civilisation**, and the name Harappa comes from the place where the civilisation was first identified. The total time span runs from **6000 BCE to 1300 BCE**, divided into three phases. The **Early Harappan phase (6000–2600 BCE)** was the formative period. The **Mature Harappan phase (2600–1900 BCE)** was the urban and most prosperous period. The **Late Harappan phase (1900–1300 BCE)** marks the decline. Distinctive Harappan artefacts — pottery, baked and unbaked bricks, seals, weights, beads, and copper and bronze articles — have been found as far apart as **Afghanistan, Baluchistan, Sind and Punjab in Pakistan**, and **Jammu and Kashmir, Punjab, Haryana, Rajasthan, Uttar Pradesh, Gujarat and Maharashtra** in India.

**Key Mature sites to know for maps:** Harappa, Mohenjodaro, Chanhudaro, Lothal, Dholavira, Kalibangan, Rakhigarhi, Banawali, Nageshwar, Balakot, Sutkagendor, Amri, Kot Diji, Manda.

## 2. Subsistence and Agriculture
The prevalence of agriculture is indicated by finds of grain, although reconstructing actual practices is harder. Representations on seals and terracotta sculpture show that the **bull** was known, so archaeologists believe **oxen were used for ploughing**. Terracotta **models of the plough** have been found at sites in **Cholistan and at Banawali (Haryana)**. At **Kalibangan (Rajasthan)** archaeologists found a **ploughed field with two sets of furrows at right angles**, suggesting that **two different crops were grown together**. For harvesting, the Harappans probably used **stone blades set in wooden handles or metal tools**. Since most sites lie in **semi-arid lands**, irrigation was probably needed: **traces of canals exist at Shortughai in Afghanistan** (but not in Punjab or Sind), and **water from wells** was likely used. Dietary practices are reconstructed from **charred grains and seeds** and from the study of animal bones.

## 3. Mohenjodaro: A Planned City
Mohenjodaro was divided into two sections. The **Citadel** lay to the **west** and was built on higher ground, while the **Lower Town** lay to the **east** and was also walled. Buildings were constructed on **baked-brick platforms**, which shows that the **settlement was planned first and the buildings raised afterwards**.

The **drainage system** impressed the archaeologist Ernest Mackay, who called it "**certainly the most complete ancient system as yet discovered**." Every house was connected to the street drains. The main channels were made of **bricks set in mortar and covered with loose bricks or limestone slabs** that could be removed for cleaning. House drains first emptied into a **sump or cesspit** where solid matter settled, while the waste water flowed into the street drains. Long drainage channels had **sumps at intervals**, and "little heaps of sand" found beside them show that the debris was not always carted away. Drainage was **not unique to big cities** — at **Lothal**, houses were of mud brick but the drains were of **burnt bricks**.

## 4. Domestic Architecture
Most residential houses were **centred on a courtyard**, with rooms on all sides; the courtyard was the centre of activities like **cooking and weaving**, especially in hot, dry weather. There was an apparent **concern for privacy**: there were **no windows at ground level**, and the **main entrance did not give a direct view** of the interior or courtyard. **Every house had its own bathroom, paved with bricks**, with drains connected through the wall to the street drains. Some houses had **staircases** to a second storey or the roof. Many houses had **wells**, often in a room reachable **from the outside**, perhaps for passers-by — scholars estimate **about 700 wells in Mohenjodaro**.

## 5. The Citadel
On the Citadel stood structures used for **special public purposes**. One was the **warehouse**, a massive structure whose lower brick portions survive, its upper wooden parts having decayed. The other was the **Great Bath**, a large **rectangular tank in a courtyard surrounded by a corridor on all four sides**. There were **two flights of steps on the north and south** leading into the tank, which was made **watertight by setting bricks on edge with gypsum mortar**. Rooms stood on three sides, one containing a **large well**, and the water from the tank flowed out into a drain. Scholars believe the Bath was used on special ritual occasions.

## 6. Burials and Social Differences
To detect social differences, archaeologists study **burials**. At Harappan sites the dead were **generally laid in pits**, though sometimes the pits were **lined with bricks** — possibly indicating social differences, though we cannot be sure. Some graves contained **pottery and ornaments**, perhaps reflecting a belief in their use in the **afterlife**. **Jewellery was found in burials of both men and women.** In the mid-1980s, excavations at the Harappa cemetery yielded an ornament of **three shell rings, a jasper bead and hundreds of micro beads** near the skull of a male. Some dead were buried with **copper mirrors**. On the whole, however, the Harappans **did not believe in burying precious things with the dead**.

## 7. Utilitarian Objects and Luxuries
Archaeologists classify artefacts as **utilitarian** (daily-use objects of ordinary materials like **querns, pottery, needles, flesh-rubbers**, found throughout settlements) and **luxuries** (rare objects of **costly, non-local materials** or **complicated technologies**, like little pots of **faience** — ground silica mixed with colour and gum and fired). The **distribution** tells the real story: rare and valuable objects are **concentrated in large settlements like Mohenjodaro and Harappa** and are rarely found in smaller ones. **Miniature faience pots**, perhaps perfume bottles, are found only in the big cities, with **none from Kalibangan**. **Gold was rare**, and all the gold jewellery recovered came from **hoards**.

## 8. Craft Production
**Chanhudaro**, a tiny settlement of **less than 7 hectares** (compared to Mohenjodaro's 125), was **almost exclusively devoted to craft production**: bead-making, shell-cutting, metal-working, seal-making and weight-making. Beads were made from a remarkable variety of materials — **carnelian (beautifully red), jasper, crystal, quartz, steatite**, metals like **copper, bronze and gold**, plus **shell, faience and terracotta**. Their shapes were disc-shaped, cylindrical, spherical, barrel-shaped and segmented. **Steatite is very soft** and was easily worked, and some beads were moulded from a paste of steatite powder. The **red colour of carnelian was obtained by firing** the yellowish raw material and the beads at various stages. **Specialised drills** have been found at **Chanhudaro, Lothal and Dholavira**. Different centres specialised in different crafts — **Nageshwar and Balakot in shell work, Chanhudaro and Lothal in beads** — which is how archaeologists identify production centres.

## 9. Seals, Script and Weights
**Seals and sealings** facilitated **long-distance communication**: the mouth of a goods bag was tied with rope, wet clay was pressed on the knot, and a seal stamped on it. An **intact sealing proved the bag had not been tampered with** and also **conveyed the identity of the sender**.

The **Harappan script remains undeciphered** to this day. Most inscriptions are short — the **longest contains about 26 signs** — and the script was clearly **not alphabetical**, because it has **somewhere between 375 and 400 signs**. It was evidently **written from right to left**, since some seals show wider spacing on the right and cramping on the left. Writing has been found on seals, copper tools, rims of jars, tablets, jewellery, bone rods, and even an ancient **signboard from Dholavira**.

Exchanges were regulated by a **precise system of weights**, usually made of the stone **chert**, generally **cubical and unmarked**. The **lower denominations were binary (1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32)** while the **higher ones were decimal (160, 200, 320, 640)**. Metal scale-pans have also been found.

## 10. Ancient Authority — Who Ruled?
There is clear evidence of **complex decision-making**: the extraordinary **uniformity of artefacts** (pottery, seals, weights, bricks), the fact that **bricks were of a uniform ratio "from Jammu to Gujarat"** despite being produced locally, strategically located settlements, and the mobilisation of labour for walls and platforms. But who organised all this? A large building at Mohenjodaro was labelled a **"palace"** though no spectacular finds were associated with it, and a stone statue is still called the **"priest-king"** — a name borrowed from Mesopotamian parallels. Archaeologists remain divided: some think Harappan society had **no rulers at all**; others think there were **several rulers** (Mohenjodaro one, Harappa another); still others argue for a **single state**, pointing to standardised artefacts and planned settlements.

## 11. The End of the Civilisation
By **c. 1800 BCE most Mature Harappan sites in regions like Cholistan had been abandoned**, while population **expanded into new settlements in Gujarat, Haryana and western Uttar Pradesh**. In the sites that survived, a transformation of material culture is visible: the distinctive artefacts — **weights, seals, special beads** — disappeared, and **writing, long-distance trade and craft specialisation vanished**. House construction deteriorated and large public structures were no longer produced. Suggested explanations include **climatic change, deforestation, excessive floods, the shifting or drying-up of rivers, and the overuse of the landscape**, but the end was certainly **not sudden or uniform**.

**Source box — "Evidence of an invasion":** In 1925, **sixteen skeletons** wearing their ornaments were found at Mohenjodaro. In 1947, **R.E.M. Wheeler** linked this to the **Rigveda**, noting that **Indra** is called **puramdara** ("fort-destroyer"), and famously concluded that "**Indra stands accused**." But in the 1960s, **George Dales** proved this a "**mythical massacre**": the skeletons did not belong to one period, there was **no destruction level, no burning, no armour or weapons**, and no evidence of a final defence on the Citadel. This is a classic example of **interpretation being reversed by re-examination**.

**Source box — Rakhigarhi DNA:** The recent **archaeogenetic study of Rakhigarhi** (the **biggest Harappan city, 550 hectares, in Hisar district, Haryana**) was carried out by the **Deccan College (Pune), the Centre for Cellular and Molecular Biology (Hyderabad) and the Harvard Medical College**. DNA extracted from skeletal remains shows the Harappans were the **indigenous people of the region**, with genetic roots going back to **10,000 BCE**; a **majority of South Asians today are their descendants**. The unbroken genetic and cultural continuity **rules out large-scale immigration of so-called Aryans**, and the reconstructed 3D facial features **resemble the modern population of Haryana** — an unbroken continuity of **5,000 years** (Vasant Shinde et al., *Cell*, 2019).

## 12. Discovering the Civilisation
**Alexander Cunningham**, the **first Director-General of the ASI**, worked with written accounts of Chinese Buddhist pilgrims and missed the Harappan significance of the seals — a phase the textbook calls "**Cunningham's confusion**." **Daya Ram Sahni** first excavated the seals at Harappa in **1921**. **John Marshall**, the first professional Director-General, excavated along **regular horizontal units, ignoring stratigraphy**, so valuable contextual information was **irretrievably lost**. It was **R.E.M. Wheeler (Director-General from 1944)** who **followed the stratigraphy of the mound** and brought **military precision** to archaeology. After **Partition**, most major sites went to Pakistan, spurring Indian archaeologists to find sites in India: **Kalibangan, Lothal, Rakhigarhi and Dholavira**. Since the **1980s**, international teams have worked jointly at Harappa and Mohenjodaro using **modern scientific techniques**.

**🧠 Memory hooks:** Phases — "**6-26-19-13**" (6000, 2600, 1900, 1300 BCE). Weights — "**Binary below, decimal above**". End reasons — "**C-D-F-R-O**" (Climate, Deforestation, Floods, Rivers, Overuse). Discoverers in order — "**Cunningham → Marshall → Wheeler**" ("C-M-W: Confused, Mechanical, Wise").

# 🟤 THEME 2: KINGS, FARMERS AND TOWNS (13 Marks — THE BIGGEST THEME)

## 🎬 The story of the chapter
In the sixth century BCE, states, cities, coins and iron appeared together. Magadha rose first, then the Mauryas built an empire inscribed by Asoka. Meanwhile kings, chiefs and devaputras competed for divine status, farmers pushed up production with iron ploughs and irrigation, and cities hummed with trade from Rome to Southeast Asia. Finally, epigraphists like Prinsep taught us to read all of this from stones.

## 1. The Sixteen Mahajanapadas
The **sixth century BCE** is regarded as a major turning point in early Indian history, associated with **early states, cities, the growing use of iron and the development of coinage**, as well as the rise of **Buddhism and Jainism**. Early Buddhist and Jaina texts list **sixteen states called mahajanapadas**; the most frequently mentioned are **Vajji, Magadha, Koshala, Kuru, Panchala, Gandhara and Avanti**. Most were ruled by kings, but some were **ganas or sanghas — oligarchies** where power was shared by many men collectively called rajas; **both Mahavira and the Buddha belonged to such ganas**, and in the Vajji sangha the rajas probably controlled resources like land collectively. Each mahajanapada had a **fortified capital city**, and maintaining forts, armies and bureaucracies required resources, which is why **taxes were introduced from c. the sixth century BCE — the usual rate was one-sixth of the produce**, levied as **bali and bhaga**. Remember that a **janapada** means "the land where a jana (a people or clan) sets its foot."

## 2. Magadha — First Among the Sixteen
Between the **sixth and fourth centuries BCE**, **Magadha (in present-day Bihar)** became the most powerful mahajanapada. **Modern historians** explain its rise through geography: **especially productive agriculture**, **iron mines in present-day Jharkhand** providing tools and weapons, **elephants from the regional forests** for the army, and the **Ganga and its tributaries** providing cheap and convenient communication. **Early Buddhist and Jaina writers**, on the other hand, credited **ruthlessly ambitious kings** — **Bimbisara, Ajatasattu and Mahapadma Nanda** — and their capable ministers. The capital was first **Rajagaha** (Prakrit for modern **Rajgir**, meaning "house of the king," a fortified settlement among hills), and was shifted in the **fourth century BCE to Pataliputra** (modern Patna), commanding the Ganga routes.

## 3. The Mauryan Empire
The growth of Magadha culminated in the Mauryan Empire, founded by **Chandragupta Maurya c. 321 BCE**, who extended control as far northwest as **Afghanistan and Baluchistan**. His grandson **Asoka, arguably the most famous ruler of early India, conquered Kalinga** (present-day coastal Odisha). Historians reconstruct Mauryan history from **archaeological finds especially sculpture**; the **fragments of Megasthenes**, a **Greek ambassador to Chandragupta's court**; the **Arthashastra**, parts of which were probably composed by **Kautilya or Chanakya**, traditionally Chandragupta's minister; **Buddhist, Jaina and Puranic literature**; and above all the **inscriptions of Asoka (c. 272/268–231 BCE)** on rocks and pillars, regarded as among the most valuable sources. **Asoka was the first ruler to inscribe his messages to subjects and officials on natural rocks and polished pillars**, proclaiming what he understood as **dhamma** — **respect towards elders, generosity towards Brahmanas and renouncers, kind treatment of slaves and servants, and respect for religions and traditions other than one's own**.

## 4. Administering the Empire
There were **five major political centres**: the capital **Pataliputra** and the provincial centres of **Taxila, Ujjayini, Tosali and Suvarnagiri**, all mentioned in Asokan inscriptions. The **same message was engraved everywhere** — from the North-West Frontier to Andhra, Odisha and Uttarakhand — yet historians increasingly realise that the empire could not have had a **uniform administrative system**; the inscriptions reflect imperial communication more than actual uniform control. According to **Greek sources**, the Mauryan ruler maintained a standing army of **600,000 foot-soldiers, 30,000 cavalry and 9,000 elephants**, though some historians consider this exaggerated. The **Arthashastra** even lays down how to **capture wild elephants**, with guards following the course of their **urine and dung**. Most Asokan inscriptions were in **Prakrit written in Brahmi**, but northwestern inscriptions used **Aramaic and Greek** languages, and some Prakrit inscriptions there used the **Kharosthi** script.

## 5. Chiefs and Kings in the South
New kingdoms emerged in the Deccan and the south, including the chiefdoms of the **Cholas, Cheras and Pandyas in Tamilakam** (the ancient Tamil country, including parts of present-day Andhra Pradesh and Kerala). A **chief** was a powerful man whose position **may or may not be hereditary**; he derived support from **kinfolk**, performed **special rituals**, provided **leadership in warfare** and **arbitrated disputes**. Unlike kings, he **received gifts rather than collecting taxes**, and **distributed these among his supporters**; chiefdoms had **no regular armies or officials**. Our knowledge comes largely from the **Sangam texts**. Later, the **Satavahanas** (western and central India, c. second century BCE–second century CE) and the **Shakas** (Central Asian origin, north-west and west) **derived revenues from long-distance trade**, and despite **obscure social origins** claimed high status once in power.

## 6. Divine Kings
Rulers claimed high status by **identifying with deities**. The **Kushanas** (c. first century BCE–first century CE), who ruled from **Central Asia to northwest India**, are the best example: **colossal statues of Kushana rulers were installed in a shrine at Mat near Mathura**, with similar statues in a shrine in **Afghanistan**, leading many historians to believe they **considered themselves godlike**. Many Kushana rulers adopted the title **devaputra ("son of god")**, possibly inspired by **Chinese rulers who called themselves "sons of heaven."** By the **fourth century**, larger states like the **Gupta Empire** depended on **samantas** — men who maintained themselves through **local resources including control over land** and offered **homage and military support** to rulers; **powerful samantas could become kings, while weak rulers were reduced to subordination**. Gupta history is reconstructed from literature, coins and inscriptions including **prashastis** — praise-poems composed by court poets, treasured as poetry even when mined for facts.

## 7. Popular Perceptions of Kings
Ordinary people rarely left accounts, so historians use stories from the **Jatakas and the Panchatantra**. The **Gandatindu Jataka** (in Pali, around the middle of the first millennium CE) tells of a **wicked king** who went out in disguise to hear what his subjects thought: **elderly men and women, cultivators, herders, village boys and even animals cursed him**, complaining that they were **attacked by robbers at night and by tax collectors during the day**, and that people **abandoned their villages to live in the forest**. The story shows how strained the relationship between kings and rural subjects could be — kings demanded high taxes, and **escaping to the forest remained a peasant's option**.

## 8. Strategies for Increasing Production
From c. the sixth century BCE, **plough agriculture spread in the fertile alluvial river valleys of the Ganga and the Kaveri**. The **iron-tipped ploughshare** turned the alluvial soil in high-rainfall areas, and in parts of the Ganga valley **paddy transplantation** dramatically increased production — seeds were first broadcast, then the saplings transplanted into waterlogged fields, which ensured **a higher survival ratio and higher yields** but meant **back-breaking work**. The iron ploughshare's use was **restricted**: semi-arid **Punjab and Rajasthan** did not adopt it until the twentieth century, and the **hilly north-east and central India practised hoe agriculture**, better suited to the terrain. The second strategy was **irrigation through wells, tanks and, less commonly, canals**, organised by both **communities and individuals** — usually powerful men and kings, who **recorded such works in inscriptions**. The classic example is the **Sudarshana lake** in Gujarat: an artificial reservoir built by a local governor during Mauryan rule, **broken by a storm and repaired by the Shaka ruler Rudradaman without imposing any tax on his subjects** (recorded in his Sanskrit inscription of c. second century CE), and repaired again by a **Gupta ruler** in the fifth century.

## 9. Differences in Rural Society and Land Grants
The benefits of these technologies were uneven, and **growing differentiation** appeared in the countryside. From the **early centuries of the Common Era**, we find **grants of land recorded in inscriptions — some on stone, most on copper plates** — generally of grants **to religious institutions or Brahmanas**, mostly in **Sanskrit** (from the seventh century, sometimes partly in a local language like Tamil or Telugu). The most cited example is **Prabhavati Gupta**, daughter of **Chandragupta II (c. 375–415 CE)**, married into the **Vakataka** dynasty of the Deccan. Although **Sanskrit legal texts denied women independent access to land**, Prabhavati **made a land grant** — possible because she was a queen, an exceptional situation, or because legal provisions were not uniformly implemented. Her inscription shows rural populations of **Brahmanas, peasants and others** who were expected to **provide produce and obey the new lord of the village**. Historians debate the impact of land grants: some see them as a **strategy to extend agriculture to new areas**, others as **evidence of weakening political power**, with kings granting land to **win allies as their control over samantas weakened**.

## 10. Cities and Urban Populations
New cities emerged across the subcontinent from the **sixth century BCE**, many as capitals of mahajanapadas. Virtually all major towns stood along **routes of communication**: **Pataliputra on riverine routes, Ujjayini on land routes, Puhar near the coast**, and **Mathura** as a bustling **commercial, cultural and political** centre. Urban finds include the fine glossy pottery called **Northern Black Polished Ware (NBPW)**, probably used by the rich, and ornaments, tools, weapons and figurines of **gold, silver, copper, bronze, ivory, glass, shell and terracotta**. Each city had its own history: **Pataliputra began as the village Pataligrama**, became the Magadhan capital in the fifth century BCE, grew into one of the **largest cities in Asia** under the Mauryas, and by the time the Chinese pilgrim **Xuan Zang** visited in the **seventh century CE** it was **in ruins with a very small population**.

## 11. Trade, Merchants and Coins
From the sixth century BCE, **land and river routes** criss-crossed the subcontinent and extended **overland into Central Asia**, and **overseas from coastal ports across the Arabian Sea to East and North Africa and West Asia, and through the Bay of Bengal to Southeast Asia and China**. Rulers tried to **control these routes, offering protection for a price**. Those who travelled included **peddlers on foot, merchants with caravans of bullock carts and pack animals, and seafarers** whose ventures were risky but highly profitable. Successful merchants were called **masattuvan in Tamil** and **setthis and satthavahas in Prakrit**. Goods traded included **salt, grain, cloth, metal ores and finished products, stone, timber and medicinal plants**; **spices, especially pepper**, were in high demand in the **Roman Empire**, along with textiles and medicinal plants.

**Coins** facilitated exchange. **Punch-marked coins of silver and copper (c. sixth century BCE onwards)** were the earliest, and while some symbols can be linked to kings, it is likely that **merchants, bankers and townspeople also issued some**. The **first coins bearing the names and images of rulers were issued by the Indo-Greeks** (north-west, c. second century BCE). The **Kushanas issued the first gold coins** (c. first century CE) and the largest hoards of gold coinage. The **Periplus of the Erythraean Sea** — a Greek sailor's manual of c. first century CE, where "Periplus" means "sailing around" and "Erythraean" is the Greek name for the **Red Sea** — describes the **Malabar coast**: large ships came for **pepper and malabathrum**; imports included **coins, topaz, antimony, coral, crude glass, copper, tin and lead**; exports included **pepper, fine pearls, ivory, silk cloth, transparent stones, diamonds, sapphires and tortoise shell**. Archaeologists have found a **bead-making industry at Kodumanal (Tamil Nadu)** using precious and semi-precious stones.

## 12. How Inscriptions Were Deciphered
Most modern Indian scripts derive from **Brahmi**, used in most Asokan inscriptions. From the **late eighteenth century**, European scholars aided by **Indian pandits** worked backwards from **Bengali and Devanagari manuscripts**, comparing letters with older specimens. They wrongly assumed the early inscriptions were in **Sanskrit**, when the earliest were in **Prakrit**. It was **James Prinsep, an officer of the East India Company, who deciphered Asokan Brahmi in 1838**. **Kharosthi** was cracked differently: **coins of Indo-Greek kings carried their names in both Greek and Kharosthi**, so scholars compared letters — for example the symbol for "a" in names like **Apollodotus** — and once Prinsep identified the language as **Prakrit**, longer inscriptions became readable.

## 13. Historical Evidence from Inscriptions — and Its Limits
Asokan inscriptions usually **do not name the ruler**, using instead the titles **devanampiya ("beloved of the gods")** and **piyadassi ("pleasant to behold")**. Epigraphists concluded all such inscriptions belonged to the same ruler by **matching content, style, language and palaeography**. Historians must constantly assess claims — Asoka says earlier rulers had **no arrangements for receiving reports**, which may be an exaggeration; his edict orders **pativedakas** (reporters) to inform him at all times, **whether he was eating or in the inner apartments**. Words in **brackets** are epigraphists' additions to clarify meaning. The **limits** of epigraphy are real: not everything was recorded (routine agricultural practices are rarely mentioned), inscriptions can be **faint and ambiguous**, technical words are hard to interpret, many inscriptions remain undeciphered or unpublished, and **not all inscriptions have survived** — so they must always be used alongside other sources.

# 🟤 THEME 3: KINSHIP, CASTE AND CLASS (10 Marks)

## 🎬 The story of the chapter
The Mahabharata is not just an epic — it is a moving archive of how ancient Indians imagined family, caste, gender and power. The chapter uses it, alongside the Dharmasutras, Manusmriti, Satavahana inscriptions and Sangam poems, to reconstruct ideals of patriliny, the rules of marriage and gotra, the realities of kings who were not Kshatriyas, women's property rights, and finally how archaeologists tried to match the epic with Hastinapura's mud.

## 1. Finding Out About Families
Families usually **share food and other resources, and live, work and perform rituals together**, and they are embedded in larger networks of **kinfolk**. Familial ties are often treated as "natural," but they are **defined in many different ways** — some societies count cousins as blood relations, others do not. For early societies, historians retrieve information about **elite families fairly easily**, but reconstructing the family lives of **ordinary people is far more difficult**. Historians also analyse **attitudes towards family and kinship**, because ideas shape actions and actions reshape ideas.

**Vocabulary box:** **kula** = family; **jnati** = the larger network of kinfolk; **vamsha** = lineage. **Patriliny** = tracing descent from father to son and grandson; **matriliny** = descent traced through the mother.

## 2. The Ideal of Patriliny
The Mahabharata describes a **feud over land and power between two groups of cousins — the Kauravas and the Pandavas** — belonging to the **Kuru** lineage of one of the janapadas. The Pandavas won the battle, and **patrilineal succession was proclaimed**. Although patriliny existed before the epic, its central story **reinforced the idea that it was valuable**. Under patriliny, **sons could claim the resources of their fathers, including the throne**, when the fathers died. Most ruling dynasties from **c. the sixth century BCE** claimed to follow it, though with variations: sometimes brothers succeeded, sometimes other kinsmen, and in exceptional cases **women such as Prabhavati Gupta exercised power**. The concern with patriliny was **not unique to ruling families** — it appears in **Rigveda mantras** and was probably shared by wealthy men claiming high status.

**Source box — "Producing fine sons":** a **Rigveda mantra, probably inserted around c. 1000 BCE**, chanted by the priest in the marriage ritual and **still used in many Hindu weddings**: "I free her from here, but not from there. I have bound her firmly there, so that through the grace of **Indra** she will have **fine sons** and be fortunate in her husband's love." Here "here" is the father's house and "there" the husband's.

## 3. Rules of Marriage
While sons carried the patrilineage forward, **daughters were to be married outside the kin** — the system called **exogamy**. The lives of young women of high-status families were **carefully regulated** to marry the "right" person at the "right" time, giving rise to the belief that **kanyadana, the gift of a daughter in marriage, was an important religious duty of the father**. With the growth of new towns, social life became more complex, and the **Brahmanas responded by laying down codes of social behaviour** in detail from **c. 500 BCE** — the **Dharmasutras and Dharmashastras**. The **Manusmriti** lists **eight forms of marriage**, four of which the NCERT quotes: the first, a daughter gifted to "a man learned in the Veda"; the fourth, gifted with the blessing "may both of you perform your duties together"; the fifth, after the bridegroom **gives wealth** to the kinsmen and bride; and the sixth, "the voluntary union of a maiden and her lover... which springs from desire." Remember the definitions: **endogamy** = marriage within the unit; **polygyny** = a man with several wives; **polyandry** = a woman with several husbands.

## 4. The Gotra of Women
From **c. 1000 BCE**, Brahmanical practice classified people, **especially Brahmanas, into gotras**, each named after a **Vedic seer**, with members regarded as his descendants. **Two rules mattered**: women were expected to **give up their father's gotra and adopt their husband's** at marriage, and **members of the same gotra could not marry**. The **Satavahana inscriptions** test how far these rules were followed. Their kings' names carry **metronymics** — names derived from the mother: **raja Gotami-puta Siri-Satakani** means "king Satakarni, **son of Gotami**" (Gotami and Vasithi are feminine forms of the seer names Gotama and Vasistha). This shows the influence of **mothers** in royal nomenclature — even though Brahmanical norms preferred the father's line. The **Brihadaranyaka Upanishad** too records people known by **metronymic** names. Yet the Satavahanas also practised **endogamy**, and **differentiated between wives**, complicating any simple conclusion.

## 5. Varna — The "Proper" Order
Sanskrit texts laid out a four-fold **varna** order — **Brahmanas, Kshatriyas, Vaishyas and Shudras** — with the claim that each varna's duties followed from **guna (qualities) and karma (actions)** rather than birth. The **Dharmasutras and Dharmashastras** prescribed occupations: the **Brahmana** was to study and teach the Vedas, perform sacrifices and sacrifice for others, and **receive gifts**; the **Kshatriya** was to bear arms and protect people; the **Vaishya** was to engage in **agriculture, pastoralism and trade**; and the **only "occupation" prescribed for the Shudra was servitude** to the three higher varnas. A Rigveda hymn is often cited for **early flexibility**: "I am a poet, my father is a physician, and my mother grinds corn" — occupational diversity within one family.

**Source box — Ekalavya (from the Adi Parvan):** **Drona**, a Brahmana who taught archery to the Kuru princes, **refused to teach Ekalavya**, a **nishada** (forest-dwelling hunting community), because he "knew the dharma." Ekalavya practised before a clay image of Drona and became a superb archer — even shooting **seven arrows into a barking dog's mouth**. When Drona, reminded by **Arjuna** of his promise that Arjuna would be unrivalled, demanded Ekalavya's **right thumb as his fee**, Ekalavya **unhesitatingly cut it off**. Thereafter he was no longer as fast. The story simultaneously celebrates devotion to the guru and **defends the social hierarchy** — Drona "kept his word" at a nishada's cost.

## 6. Non-Kshatriya Kings
According to the **Shastras, only Kshatriyas could be kings** — but reality was messier. The **Mauryas' social origin was hotly debated**: **later Buddhist texts called them Kshatriyas, while Brahmanical texts described them as of "low" origin**. The **Shungas and Kanvas**, the Mauryas' immediate successors, were **Brahmanas**. In effect, **political power was open to anyone who could muster support and resources**. The **Shakas**, Central Asian newcomers, were dismissed as **mlechchhas** (barbarians), yet **Rudradaman**, the best-known Shaka ruler (c. second century CE), **rebuilt the Sudarshana lake** — recorded in one of the **earliest Sanskrit inscriptions**, proving powerful mlechchhas were fluent in Sanskritic traditions. Most deliciously, the great Satavahana ruler **Gotami-puta Siri-Satakani claimed to be a "unique Brahmana" (eka bamhana) and the "destroyer of the pride of Kshatriyas,"** and to have **prevented intermarriage among the four varnas** — while **entering a marriage alliance with the kin of Rudradaman, a mlechchha**! Integration into the caste framework was clearly a **complicated, contradictory process**.

## 7. Jatis, Guilds and Beyond the Varnas
While the **varnas were fixed at four**, the number of **jatis was unlimited** and kept growing as society met new groups and occupations. Jatis sharing a profession organised into **shrenis (guilds)**. A **stone inscription from Mandasor (Madhya Pradesh, c. fifth century CE)** records the history of a **guild of silk weavers** who migrated from **Lata (Gujarat)** to **Dashapura**, journeying with children and kinfolk because they had heard of the local king's greatness. Though bound by a shared craft, members pursued **other pursuits too** — music, religious discourses, even **Vedic astronomy** — and the guild **collectively invested its wealth in building a splendid temple to the sun god**.

Beyond the varna fold lived populations whose practices owed nothing to Brahmanical ideas: **forest-dwellers** (like the **nishadas**) for whom hunting-gathering remained central, and **nomadic pastoralists**, viewed with suspicion and described in Sanskrit texts as "odd, uncivilised, or even animal-like." At the very bottom, the **"untouchable" labourers** of Theme 8's picture existed in social reality even as texts debated where they belonged.

## 8. Gendered Access to Property
In the Mahabharata's dice episode, **Duryodhana invited Yudhisthira to a game of dice**. Deceived, Yudhisthira staked his **gold, elephants, chariots, slaves, army, treasury, kingdom, the property of his subjects, his brothers, himself — and finally Draupadi**, losing everything. **Draupadi's question — had he lost himself before staking her? — split the court**: one side said a wife remained under the husband's control; the other said **a man who was not free could not stake another person**. The matter was unresolved, and **Dhritarashtra finally restored the Pandavas' and Draupadi's freedom**. The episode raises exactly the question the NCERT asks: could wives be treated as property?

The legal framework: under the **smriti tradition**, paternal estate was divided **equally among children after the parents' death, with a special share for the eldest**, and women kept the gifts received at marriage as **stridhana — "a woman's wealth" — which her children could inherit without any claim from the husband**. The **Manusmriti** lists **seven means of acquiring wealth for men** (inheritance, finding, purchase, conquest, investment, work, and acceptance of gifts from good people) and **six for women** (what was given before the marriage fire, in the bridal procession, as a token of affection, from her brother, mother or father, plus later gifts and whatever her "affectionate husband" gave her). The cumulative evidence of texts and inscriptions suggests that **upper-class women could access resources, but land, cattle and money were generally controlled by men**, sharpening gender differences.

## 9. Varna and Property — and an Alternative Ethic
If Brahmanical prescriptions had been followed, **the wealthiest men would have been Brahmanas and Kshatriyas**, with the Shudra serving; descriptions of rich priests and kings show this **corresponded to some extent with reality**. But ancient Tamilakam offered an **alternative social scenario**: in the chiefdoms of the Sangam age, **generosity was prized and miserliness despised**. Chiefs were **patrons of bards and poets** who sang their praise, and the **Sangam anthologies** show that those who controlled resources were **expected to share them** — wealth measured not by accumulation but by the glory of giving.

## 10. The Mahabharata as a Source
The text's **Sanskrit is far simpler than that of the Vedas or the prashastis**, so it was probably widely understood. Historians classify its content into the **narrative** (stories) and the **didactic** (prescriptions about social norms) — a division that is **not watertight**, since didactic sections contain stories and narratives carry messages. The most important didactic section is the **Bhagavad Gita**, Krishna's advice to Arjuna. The text calls itself an **itihasa** — literally "**thus it was**," hence "history." **Was there a real war?** Some historians think the epic preserves the memory of an actual kin conflict; others point out there is **no corroborative evidence** — we cannot be sure.

**Authors and dates:** the original story was probably composed by **charioteer-bards called sutas**, who accompanied Kshatriya warriors to battle and sang their victories, circulating orally. From the **fifth century BCE**, **Brahmanas took over the story and began writing it down** — perhaps because the Kurus and Panchalas were becoming kingdoms whose new kings wanted an itihasa. Between **c. 200 BCE and 200 CE**, as **worship of Vishnu grew, Krishna came to be identified with Vishnu**. Between **c. 200 and 400 CE**, large **didactic sections resembling the Manusmriti** were added. A text that may have begun with **fewer than 10,000 verses grew to about 100,000** — traditionally attributed to the sage **Vyasa**, who, tradition says, **dictated it to Ganesha** the scribe.

**The search for convergence:** in **1951–52 the archaeologist B.B. Lal excavated at Hastinapura (Meerut district, UP)** in the Upper Ganga doab, where the Kuru kingdom lay. He found **five occupational levels**; the houses of the second phase (c. twelfth–seventh centuries BCE) were of **mud and baked brick**, and — strikingly — evidence suggested **flood damage**, matching the epic's tradition that Hastinapura was **swept away by a flood**. This is the classic exercise in **convergence**: using archaeology to check the epic's world — while remembering that similarity in names can be coincidental.

# 🟤 THEME 4: THINKERS, BELIEFS AND BUILDINGS (5 Marks)

## 🎬 The story of the chapter
Around the sixth century BCE, thinkers across the Ganga valley questioned sacrifice, death and destiny. Jainism and Buddhism answered them and built communities of monks and nuns. Their devotion turned to stone: stupas like Sanchi rose over relics, funded by kings, guilds and ordinary women and men. The chapter is also about rescue — how the Begums of Bhopal saved Sanchi while Amaravati was carted off to London gardens.

## 1. A Glimpse of Sanchi
The **stupa at Sanchi** (on a hill near Bhopal railway line) is one of the **best-preserved monuments** of the time and the chapter's centre of gravity. In the nineteenth century, the **French sought permission to take away the eastern gateway** — the best preserved — for a museum in France, and some Englishmen wanted the same; fortunately both settled for **plaster-cast copies**, and the **originals remained at the site**. The rulers of Bhopal, **Shahjehan Begum (ruled 1868–1901) and her successor Sultan Jehan Begum, funded the preservation of the site**; **John Marshall dedicated his volumes on Sanchi to Sultan Jehan**, who funded the **museum, the guesthouse where he lived and wrote, and the publication** of the volumes. Sanchi survived "**in no small measure due to wise decisions and good luck**" — escaping railway contractors and collectors — and today testifies to successful **restoration by the Archaeological Survey of India**.

## 2. The Sacrificial Tradition and New Questions
The early Vedic tradition is known from the **Rigveda, compiled between c. 1500 and 1000 BCE** — hymns in praise of deities, especially **Agni, Indra and Soma**, chanted at **sacrifices** where people prayed for **cattle, sons, good health and long life**. **Agni**, the god of fire, was identified with the **sacrificial fire** into which offerings were made so as to reach the other deities. At first sacrifices were performed **collectively**; later, from **c. 1000–500 BCE**, the **heads of households** performed some for the well-being of the domestic unit, while elaborate ones — the **rajasuya and the ashvamedha** — were performed by **chiefs and kings** who depended on **Brahmana priests**. The hymns were composed in **Vedic Sanskrit** and taught **orally** to men of priestly families.

From the **sixth century BCE**, the **Upanishads** show people asking new questions: the **meaning of life, the possibility of life after death, rebirth**, and whether rebirth was due to past actions. Thinkers speculated on the **nature of the ultimate reality**, and those **outside the Vedic tradition** even asked whether there was a **single ultimate reality at all**.

## 3. Debates and Discussions
Buddhist texts mention as many as **64 sects or schools of thought**. Teachers **travelled from place to place** convincing rivals and laypersons; debates took place in the **kutagarashala** — literally "**a hut with a pointed roof**" — or in **groves** where mendicants halted. When a philosopher convinced a rival, the rival's **followers often switched sides**. **Magadha** mattered: its kings **Bimbisara, Ajatasattu** — and later royal support — sustained these discussions, and **both the Buddha and Mahavira belonged to this milieu**.

## 4. The Message of Mahavira
The basic **Jaina** philosophy already existed in north India before **Vardhamana**, known as **Mahavira**, was born in the **sixth century BCE**. Jaina tradition holds that he was preceded by **23 tirthankaras** — literally, those who **guide men and women across the river of existence**. Jainism's most important idea is that the **entire world is animated: even stones, rocks and water have life**. Hence **ahimsa** — non-injury to **humans, animals, plants and insects** — is central, and Jaina emphasis on ahimsa has **left its mark on Indian thinking as a whole**. The **cycle of birth and rebirth is shaped through karma**; **asceticism and penance** are required to free oneself, which can be achieved only by **renouncing the world** — so **monastic existence is a necessary condition of salvation**. Jaina monks and nuns took **five vows**: to **abstain from killing, stealing and lying, to observe celibacy, and to abstain from possessing property**. Jainism **spread to many parts of India**; Jaina scholars wrote in **Prakrit, Sanskrit and Tamil**, manuscripts were preserved in **temple libraries**, and some of the **earliest stone sculptures** of the tirthankaras (e.g., from **Mathura, c. third century CE**) were made by devotees. In the **Uttaradhyayana Sutta**, the queen **Kamalavati** urges her husband to renounce the world: "As a bird dislikes the cage, so do I dislike the world."

## 5. The Buddha and His Teachings
**Siddhartha**, of the **Sakya clan**, grew up sheltered in the palace. His first outing was traumatic: he saw an **old man, a sick man and a corpse**, realised decay was inevitable, then saw a **homeless mendicant** at peace — and decided to follow that path. He tried **bodily mortification** to the point of near death, abandoned extremes, **meditated and attained enlightenment**, becoming the **Buddha, the Enlightened One**, and for the rest of his life taught **dhamma — the path of righteous living**. His message spread **through Central Asia to China, Korea and Japan, and through Sri Lanka to Myanmar, Thailand and Indonesia**.

His teachings are reconstructed mainly from stories in the **Sutta Pitaka**. Though some stories describe miraculous powers, most show the Buddha convincing people **through reason and persuasion** — as with the **grief-stricken woman whose child had died**: he gently convinced her of the inevitability of death rather than performing a miracle. He taught in the **language of ordinary people**. Buddhist philosophy holds that the world is **transient (anicca)** and constantly changing, and that **sorrow (dukkha)** is intrinsic to it; the **middle path** between severe indulgence and severe asceticism leads towards **nibbana**, the extinguishing of the ego and desire.

**The sangha:** an organisation of monks (**bhikkhus**) and nuns (**bhikkhunis**), open to men **and** women. The **Vinaya Pitaka** records rules of monastic life — a new felt blanket must be kept **six years**; a bhikkhu may accept only **two or three bowlfuls** of cakes and must share them; bedding left unattended must be confessed. The famous story of **Punna, the dasi (slave) and water-carrier**, captures Buddhist logic: asked whether washing in water frees one from evil, she replies that if so, "**all the frogs and turtles would go to heaven, and so would the water snakes and crocodiles!**" Buddhism grew rapidly because it appealed to people **dissatisfied with existing religious practices and confused by rapid social change**, valuing **conduct and character over birth**, and stressing **metta (fellow feeling) and karuna (compassion)** — especially towards the young and the weak.

## 6. Stupas — Why, How and What
**Stupas** are mounds containing **relics of the Buddha — his bodily remains or objects used by him**. The tradition may be **pre-Buddhist**, but stupas came to be venerated as **emblems of the Buddha and Buddhism**. The **Ashokavadana** says **Asoka distributed portions of the Buddha's relics to every important town and ordered stupas built over them**; by the **second century BCE**, stupas stood at **Bharhut, Sanchi and Sarnath**. The **Mahaparinibbana Sutta** explains the origin: dying, the Buddha told **Ananda** that at the **four crossroads a thupa** should be erected to the Tathagata, and that garlands, perfume or a salutation there would bring "profit and joy."

Stupas were **built through donations**, recorded on their railings and pillars: **kings like the Satavahanas**, **guilds — including the ivory workers who financed part of one Sanchi gateway**, and **hundreds of ordinary women and men** who named themselves, their homes and their occupations, along with **bhikkhus and bhikkhunis** themselves.

**Structure:** the word **stupa** is Sanskrit for "heap." It began as a simple **semi-circular earth mound called the anda**. Above it rose the **harmika**, a balcony-like structure representing the **abode of the gods**; from the harmika rose a mast, the **yashti**, often topped by a **chhatri (umbrella)**. A **railing around the mound separated the sacred from the secular world**. The early stupas at **Sanchi and Bharhut were plain**, with stone railings imitating a bamboo or wooden fence, and richly carved **gateways at the four cardinal points**. Worshippers entered through the **eastern gateway** and walked **clockwise around the mound, keeping it on their right**, imitating the **sun's course through the sky**. Later stupas, as at **Amaravati and Shah-ji-ki-Dheri (Peshawar)**, were elaborately carved with niches and sculptures.

## 7. The Fate of Amaravati and Sanchi
In **1796** a local raja, hoping for treasure, stumbled on Amaravati's ruins and wanted to build a temple from its stone. **Colin Mackenzie** visited, made detailed drawings — **but never published them**. In **1854, Walter Elliot, the commissioner of Guntur**, collected sculpture panels and took them to **Madras**; they became known as the **"Elliot marbles."** By the 1850s, slabs had travelled to the **Asiatic Society of Bengal in Calcutta, the India Office in Madras, and even London** — and some adorned **the gardens of British administrators**. The archaeologist **H.H. Cole** protested: it was "**a suicidal and indefensible policy to allow the country to be looted of original works of ancient art**"; museums should keep **plaster-cast facsimiles**, and **originals should remain where they were found**. He lost the battle for Amaravati but his **in-situ principle saved Sanchi**.

## 8. Reading Buddhist Sculpture
Art historians needed the **hagiographies** to decode early sculpture, because the Buddha was **not shown in human form**; his presence was indicated through **symbols**: the **empty seat** for meditation, the **stupa** for the **mahaparinibbana** (final liberation), and the **wheel** for the **first sermon at Sarnath** — while the **tree** symbolised enlightenment under the **Bodhi tree**. Other Sanchi sculptures come from **popular traditions**: the **shalabhanjika**, a woman **whose touch caused trees to flower and bear fruit**, an auspicious symbol absorbed into stupa decoration, alongside yakshas and yakshinis — proof that Buddhist art **integrated popular beliefs** rather than rejecting them.

## 9. The Growth of Mahayana Buddhism and Puranic Hinduism
By the **first century CE**, Buddhist ideas changed. Early teaching stressed **self-effort** towards nibbana, with the Buddha a human being who attained it by his own efforts. Now the idea of a **saviour** emerged, along with the concept of the **Bodhisatta (Bodhisattva)** — compassionate beings who **accumulated merit not to abandon the world but to help others**. The **worship of images of the Buddha and Bodhisattas** became central to this new way, called **Mahayana — the "great vehicle"**; its followers called the older tradition **Hinayana ("lesser vehicle")**, though the older Buddhists called themselves **theravadins**, followers of the respected old teachers, the **theras**.

Parallel changes transformed Brahmanical religion: **Vaishnavism** (Vishnu as principal deity) and **Shaivism** (Shiva as chief god) grew around **worship of a chosen deity**, with the god-devotee bond imagined as **love and devotion — bhakti**. In Vaishnavism, **cults developed around avatars (incarnations)**: **ten avatars** were recognised — forms the deity assumed to **save the world whenever evil threatened it** — and **different avatars were popular in different regions**, which helped local deities fold into the Puranic fold.

**Temples:** around the time stupas took their present form, the **first temples to house images of deities** were built. The early temple was a **small square room, the garbhagriha**, with a **single doorway**; gradually a tall structure, the **shikhara**, rose over the central shrine; walls were **decorated with sculpture**; later temples added **assembly halls, huge walls, gateways and water arrangements**. A unique feature: some of the earliest temples were **hollowed out of huge rocks as artificial caves** — the earliest, in the **third century BCE**, ordered by **Asoka for renouncers of the Ajivika sect**. This tradition culminated in the **eighth century** with the entire **Kailashnatha temple (a name of Shiva) carved out of rock at Ellora** — after which, a copperplate inscription records, the chief sculptor exclaimed, "**Oh, how did I make it!**" Remember the **Deogarh temple (UP, c. fifth century CE)** with its famous sculpture of **Vishnu reclining on the serpent Sheshnag**.

**Text vs image:** when text and sculpture do not match, historians must remember that **sculptors worked with their own visual traditions** — art is evidence of belief but not a photograph of texts; and visual sources **cannot show everything** of life.
