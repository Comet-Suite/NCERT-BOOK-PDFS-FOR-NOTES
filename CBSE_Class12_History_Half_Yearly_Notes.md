# THEME 5: THROUGH THE EYES OF TRAVELLERS
## Perceptions of Society (c. Tenth to Seventeenth Century) | ~12 Marks — 🔴 HIGH PRIORITY

---

### 1. CBSE VALUE POINTS (Examiner Keywords)

#### Al-Biruni (973–1048) — The Kitab-ul-Hind

- Born **973 CE** in **Khwarizm** (present-day **Uzbekistan**) — an important centre of learning
- Versed in **Syriac, Arabic, Persian, Hebrew, and Sanskrit**; familiar with Greek philosophy (Plato) through Arabic translations
- Brought to **Ghazni** in **1017** as a hostage by **Sultan Mahmud**; spent rest of life there
- Developed interest in India through Sanskrit works already translated into Arabic (astronomy, mathematics, medicine — from 8th century onwards)
- Spent years with **Brahmana priests and scholars**, learning Sanskrit and studying religious/philosophical texts
- **Kitab-ul-Hind**: written in **Arabic**, described as **simple and lucid**
  - **Voluminous text** divided into **80 chapters** on: religion, philosophy, festivals, astronomy, alchemy, manners & customs, social life, weights & measures, iconography, laws & metrology
  - **Distinctive structure**: Each chapter begins with a **question** → followed by a **description based on Sanskritic traditions** → concludes with a **comparison with other cultures**
  - This **geometric structure** owed to his **mathematical orientation**
- Translated **Patanjali's** work on grammar into Arabic; translated **Euclid** into Sanskrit for his Brahmana friends
- **Objectives** (Source 1): "A help to those who want to discuss religious questions with them (the Hindus), and as a repertory of information to those who want to associate with them"
- **Barriers to understanding** identified by Al-Biruni:
  1. **Language barrier** — Sanskrit was so different from Arabic/Persian that ideas could not be easily translated
  2. **Difference in religious beliefs** and practices
  3. **Self-absorption and insularity** of the local population
- Depended **almost exclusively on Brahmana texts** — Vedas, Puranas, Bhagavad Gita, works of Patanjali, Manusmriti
- **On the caste system**:
  - Tried to explain it by looking for **parallels in other societies** — noted four social categories in ancient Persia: knights/princes; monks/fire-priests/lawyers; physicians/astronomers/scientists; peasants/artisans
  - Argued social divisions were **not unique to India**; within Islam, all men considered equal
  - **Disapproved of the notion of pollution** — remarked everything falling into impurity strives to regain original purity; the sun cleanses the air, salt prevents sea water from becoming polluted
  - Said conception of social pollution was **contrary to the laws of nature**
  - Described the four varnas: Brahmana (from head of Brahman), Kshatriya (shoulders/hands), Vaishya (thigh), Shudra (feet)
  - Noted that while classes differed, they "live together in the same towns and villages, mixed together in the same houses and lodgings"
- **Critical about translations** of Sanskrit/Pali/Prakrit texts into Arabic — wanted to improve them
- **Did not accept everything uncritically** — questioned the story of a wooden idol lasting 216,432 years: "How, then, could wood have lasted such a length of time, and particularly in a place where the air and the soil are rather wet? God knows best!"

#### Ibn Battuta (1304–1377) — The Rihla

- Born in **Tangier** (Morocco) into a respected family known for expertise in **Islamic religious law (shari'a)**
- Believed **experience through travel** was more important source of knowledge than books
- Before reaching India, had made pilgrimage to **Mecca** and travelled extensively in Syria, Iraq, Persia, Yemen, Oman, East African ports
- Reached **Sind in 1333** (overland through Central Asia)
- Reached Delhi, lured by reputation of **Muhammad bin Tughlaq** as generous patron
- Appointed **qazi (judge)** of Delhi; remained several years until he fell out of favour and was imprisoned
- Ordered in **1342** to proceed to **China** as Sultan's envoy to the Mongol ruler
- Travelled to **Malabar coast** through central India → **Maldives** (stayed 18 months as qazi) → **Sri Lanka** → **Bengal and Assam** → **Sumatra** → Chinese port **Zaytun (Quanzhou)** → travelled to **Beijing**
- Returned home in **1347**, about **30 years** after setting out
- **Rihla** (book of travels): written in **Arabic**, provides rich details about social and cultural life in the subcontinent in the 14th century
- Dictated his account to **Ibn Juzayy** (deputed by the local ruler) on his return to Morocco
- **Strategies of representation**:
  - Described the **coconut**: compared it to a date-palm; noted the nut "resembles a man's head, for in it are what look like two eyes and a mouth"
  - Described the **paan (betel)**: detailed the method of chewing areca nut with betel leaves and chalk
  - Used **familiar comparisons** to describe unfamiliar things to his audience
- **On Indian cities**:
  - Cities were **densely populated and prosperous** (except occasional disruptions from wars/invasions)
  - **Delhi**: vast city, **largest in India**, great population
  - **Daulatabad**: rivalled Delhi in size
  - **Rampart of Delhi**: wall breadth 11 cubits; storehouses for edibles, ammunition, ballistas; grains could last long without rotting; horsemen and infantry could move inside; 28 gates (darwaza); fine cemetery with domed graves, flowers sown in it
  - **Bazaars**: not just economic hubs but also **social and cultural centres**; had mosque and temple; spaces for dancers, musicians, singers
  - **Tarababad** in Daulatabad: marketplace for male and female singers with swings, decorated shops
- **Agriculture and trade**:
  - Indian agriculture very **productive** due to soil fertility — **two crops a year** possible
  - Subcontinent well integrated with **inter-Asian trade networks**
  - Indian textiles in great demand: **cotton cloth, fine muslins, silks, brocade, satin**
  - Fine muslins so expensive — worn only by nobles and very rich
- **Postal system** (two kinds):
  1. **Horse-post (uluq)**: royal horses stationed every 4 miles
  2. **Foot-post (dawa)**: three stations per mile; runners with copper bells on rods; **quicker than horse-post** — news from Sind reached Sultan in 5 days vs 50 days by travel
  - Used to transport fruits from Khurasan
- **Travel hazards**: attacked by robbers multiple times; caravan from Multan to Delhi attacked with many killed; journeyed 40 days Multan→Delhi, 50 days Sind→Delhi
- **On slavery**:
  - Slaves openly **sold in markets** like commodities; exchanged as gifts
  - Purchased "horses, camels and slaves" as gifts for Sultan
  - Female slaves — some were **experts in music and dance**; some employed to **spy on nobles**
  - Female slaves used for **domestic labour**; price was very low
  - Considerable **differentiation among slaves** — from domestic servants to musical performers

#### François Bernier (1620–1688)

- **Frenchman**: doctor, political philosopher, and historian
- In India for **12 years (1656–1668)**
- Closely associated with Mughal court: **physician to Prince Dara Shukoh** (eldest son of Shah Jahan); later intellectual/scientist with **Danishmand Khan** (Armenian noble)
- **Travels in the Mughal Empire**: marked by **detailed observations, critical insights, and reflection**
- Constantly **compared Mughal India with Europe** (especially France), generally **emphasizing superiority of the latter**
- Used model of **binary opposition**: India presented as the **inverse of Europe**
- Dedicated major writing to **Louis XIV**, king of France
- Works published in France **1670–71**; translated into English, Dutch, German, Italian within 5 years; **reprinted 8 times in French** by 1725, **3 times in English** by 1684
- **On land ownership** (most fundamental difference according to Bernier):
  - Claimed **no private property in land** in Mughal India
  - Emperor **owned all land** and distributed among nobles
  - This had **disastrous consequences**: landholders averse to long-term investment → uniform ruination of agriculture → excessive oppression of peasantry → decline in living standards
  - Claimed **"There is no middle state in India"** — undifferentiated masses of impoverished people vs small minority of rich/powerful ruling class
  - Described king as king of **"beggars and barbarians"**
- **However, Bernier's picture was oversimplified**:
  - **Abu'l Fazl** described land revenue as **"remunerations of sovereignty"** — a claim for protection, not rent on owned land
  - Revenue was actually a **tax on the crop**, not rent
  - **Bernier was influenced by contemporary European debates** about nature of state and society — intended his description as a **warning** against European kings following the Mughal model
  - His ideas influenced **Montesquieu** (developed idea of **oriental despotism**) and **Karl Marx** (developed concept of **Asiatic mode of production**)
- **On towns**:
  - ~**15% population lived in towns** in 17th century — **higher** than proportion in Western Europe
  - Bernier described cities as **"camp towns"** — owed existence to imperial camp
  - Reality: all kinds of towns existed — manufacturing, trading, port, sacred, pilgrimage
- **On merchants and artisans**:
  - Merchants had strong community/kin ties; in western India called **mahajans**; chief = **sheth**; in Ahmedabad, **nagarsheth**
  - **Imperial karkhanas** (workshops): embroiderers, goldsmiths, painters, varnishers, joiners, tailors, shoe-makers, silk/brocade/muslin makers
  - Vast quantities of **precious metals flowed into India** as manufactures were exported for gold and silver
  - Prosperous **merchant community** engaged in long-distance exchange
- **On sati**:
  - Highlighted treatment of women as marker of difference between Western and Eastern societies
  - Described a **child sati** in Lahore — a widow of about **twelve years** forced to be burnt alive; "three or four of the Brahmanas, assisted by an old woman... forced the unwilling victim toward the fatal spot"
  - Some women embraced death cheerfully; others were forced
- **On women more broadly**:
  - Women's labour crucial in agricultural and non-agricultural production
  - Women from merchant families participated in commercial activities, sometimes taking disputes to court
  - Travellers' observations were **shaped by their own contexts** and missed many aspects of social life

### 3. SOURCE & CASE-BASED EXTRACTS (High-Probability for 4-mark questions)

#### Extract A: Al-Biruni on the Caste System (Source 5 — Extremely Important)

> *"The highest caste are the Brahmana, of whom the books of the Hindus tell us that they were created from the head of Brahman. And as the Brahman is only another name for the force called nature, and the head is the highest part of the body, the Brahmana are the choice part of the whole genus. Therefore the Hindus consider them as the very best of mankind. The next caste are the Kshatriya, who were created from the shoulders and hands of Brahman... After them follow the Vaishya, who were created from the thigh of Brahman. The Shudra, who were created from his feet..."*

**Key CBSE Points to Extract:**
- Al-Biruni's description of **varna hierarchy** based on Purusha Sukta (Brahman's body)
- He used **cross-cultural comparison** (Persian social categories) to contextualise
- **Disapproved of pollution** concept — cited natural examples (sun cleanses air, salt prevents water pollution)
- Relied on **Brahmana sources** — normative texts presenting idealised view
- **Reality was different**: antyaja (born outside system) provided labour but were included in economic networks

**Predicted 4-mark question:** *"Analyse Al-Biruni's approach to understanding the caste system. How reliable is his account?"*

#### Extract B: Bernier on the Peasantry (Source 11 — Critical for comparison questions)

> *"Of the vast tracts of country constituting the empire of Hindustan, many are little more than sand, or barren mountains, badly cultivated, and thinly populated. Even a considerable portion of the good land remains untilled for want of labourers... The poor people, when they become incapable of discharging the demands of their rapacious lords, are not only often deprived of the means of subsistence, but are also made to lose their children, who are carried away as slaves..."*

**Key CBSE Points:**
- Bernier depicted **uniform poverty and tyranny**
- Used **emotional language** ("rapacious lords," "children carried away as slaves") to strengthen argument
- His real purpose: **warning for Europe** — against following the Mughal model
- **Contradicted by Source 13** (same chapter) where Bernier himself acknowledges Bengal's fertility, silk/cotton/indigo production, and gold/silver flowing into India
- **Modern historians disagree**: rural society had **considerable social and economic differentiation** (big zamindars at one end, landless labourers at other, big peasants with hired labour in middle)

### 5. PREDICTED LONG ANSWERS

#### Q1 (6 marks): "Discuss Al-Biruni's understanding of Indian society. What were the barriers he identified and how did he overcome them?"

**Answer Structure:**

**Introduction:**
Al-Biruni (973–1048), a scholar from Khwarizm (Uzbekistan), came to India in 1017 and spent years studying Sanskrit texts and interacting with Brahmana scholars. His magnum opus, the *Kitab-ul-Hind*, remains one of the most important sources for understanding medieval Indian society.

**Body — Barriers to Understanding:**
- **Language barrier**: Sanskrit was "of an enormous range" — so different from Arabic/Persian that concepts could not be easily translated
- **Religious difference**: Hindu beliefs were vastly different from Islamic monotheism
- **Insularity of local population**: Self-absorption and resistance to outside understanding

**Body — How he overcame them:**
- Learned Sanskrit through interaction with Brahmana priests and scholars
- Studied religious and philosophical texts — Vedas, Puranas, Bhagavad Gita, Patanjali's works, Manusmriti
- Adopted a **comparative methodology**: Question → Sanskritic description → Comparison with other cultures
- Compared caste system with **Persian social categories** (four categories) to show social divisions were not unique to India
- **Critical approach**: Did not accept everything uncritically (e.g., questioned the story of a 216,432-year-old wooden idol)

**Body — His understanding of caste:**
- Described four varnas from Puranic perspective (Brahmana from head, Kshatriya from shoulders, Vaishya from thigh, Shudra from feet)
- **Disapproved of pollution** — argued it was "contrary to the laws of nature"
- Noted that despite class differences, people "live together in the same towns and villages"
- Limitation: depended almost exclusively on Brahmana texts; did not capture the fluidity of the actual system

**Conclusion:**
Al-Biruni's approach was unique for its time — combining scholarly rigour with cross-cultural empathy. While limited by his dependence on elite Sanskritic sources, his comparative framework and critical method made the *Kitab-ul-Hind* a pioneering work in cross-cultural understanding.

#### Q2 (6 marks): "Compare and contrast the perspectives of Ibn Battuta and Bernier. How did their backgrounds shape their descriptions of India?"

**Answer Structure:**

**Introduction:**
Ibn Battuta (14th century, Morocco) and Bernier (17th century, France) belonged to vastly different intellectual traditions. Their accounts reflect their distinct purposes, backgrounds, and audiences.

**Body — Ibn Battuta's Perspective:**
- **Background**: Trained in Islamic law (shari'a); believed experience through travel was superior to bookish knowledge
- **Purpose**: Record everything unfamiliar for "entertainment to the mind and delight to the ears and eyes"
- **Method**: Detailed description using familiar comparisons (coconut = man's head; betel tree = grape-vine)
- **Focus**: Cities, trade, postal system, slavery — everything that was novel and exciting
- **Tone**: Enthusiastic, curious, impressed by Indian prosperity
- **Audience**: Local Moroccan ruler who deputed Ibn Juzayy to record his dictation

**Body — Bernier's Perspective:**
- **Background**: Doctor, political philosopher; part of European intellectual tradition
- **Purpose**: Compare East and West to influence European policy-makers
- **Method**: Binary opposition — India presented as inverse of Europe
- **Focus**: Land ownership, poverty, sati — situations he found "depressing"
- **Tone**: Negative, pessimistic; India as "bleak" compared to Europe
- **Audience**: Louis XIV and French intelligentsia
- **Published and widely circulated** vs Ibn Battuta's account dictated to a scribe

**Body — Key Similarities:**
- Both were outsiders who noticed things natives took for granted
- Both provided invaluable information about Indian social life
- Both were shaped by their cultural frameworks
- Both missed aspects of social life (e.g., women's productive labour)

**Conclusion:**
While Ibn Battuta celebrated India's diversity and prosperity with wonder, Bernier constructed a narrative of decline and despotism to serve European intellectual debates. Modern historians use both critically, recognising that every traveller's account is as much about the observer as about the observed.

#### Q3 (8 marks): "Analyse Bernier's description of Mughal India. To what extent is his account reliable for reconstructing agrarian society?"

**Answer Structure:**

**Introduction:**
François Bernier, a French physician and philosopher, spent 12 years (1656–1668) in Mughal India. His *Travels in the Mughal Empire*, published 1670–71, became extremely popular in Europe and profoundly shaped Western perceptions of India.

**Body — Bernier's Central Argument:**
- **No private property in land**: Emperor owned all land; distributed to nobles
- This led to: no long-term investment in agriculture → uniform ruination → peasantry oppressed → "no middle state"
- King of **"beggars and barbarians"**; cities ruined, contaminated with "ill air"; fields "overspread with bushes"

**Body — Evidence of Bias and Oversimplification:**
- **Abu'l Fazl's evidence contradicts Bernier**: Land revenue was "remunerations of sovereignty" — a tax on crops for protection, NOT rent on owned land
- Bernier was participating in **European debates** about state and society — his description was a **warning** against emulating Mughal practices
- His own Source 13 contradicts Source 11: acknowledges Bengal's fertility, silk/cotton production, gold/silver flowing into India
- **Modern historians disagree**: rural society had considerable differentiation — big zamindars, big peasants with hired labour, smaller peasants, landless labourers

**Body — What Bernier Got Right:**
- **High land revenue demands** — European travellers may have seen these as "rent" because demands were indeed very high
- **Existence of imperial karkhanas** — detailed descriptions of workshops are valuable
- **Precious metals flowing into India** — correct and significant observation
- **Prosperous merchant communities** with mahajans and nagarsheth — acknowledges their existence
- **Urban population of ~15%** — actually higher than Western Europe
- **Practice of sati** — valuable eyewitness account, though selectively focused

**Body — Legacy and Impact:**
- Influenced **Montesquieu** → theory of **oriental despotism**
- Influenced **Karl Marx** → concept of **Asiatic mode of production**
- His "camp towns" thesis oversimplified urban complexity — there were manufacturing towns, trading towns, port towns, sacred centres, pilgrimage towns

**Conclusion:**
Bernier's account, while rich in observation, was filtered through European intellectual preoccupations. It is valuable as evidence of what a European philosopher *perceived* about Mughal India, but cannot be taken at face value for reconstructing agrarian reality. Modern historians supplement Bernier with indigenous sources (Ain-i Akbari, regional records) to build a more nuanced picture of Mughal rural society — one characterised by differentiation, not uniform poverty.

# THEME 6: BHAKTI-SUFI TRADITIONS
## Changes in Religious Beliefs and Devotional Texts (c. Eighth to Eighteenth Century) | ~14 Marks — 🔴 HIGHEST PRIORITY

---

### 1. CBSE VALUE POINTS (Examiner Keywords)

#### Section A: A Mosaic of Religious Beliefs — Integration of Cults

- **Most striking feature** of this period (c. 8th–18th century): **increasing visibility of wide range of gods and goddesses** in sculpture and texts
- Continued worship of major deities: **Vishnu, Shiva, and the Goddess (Devi)** — each visualised in variety of forms
- **Two processes** at work:
  1. **Disseminating Brahmanical ideas**: Compilation of Puranic texts in **simple Sanskrit verse**, meant to be accessible to **women and Shudras** (excluded from Vedic learning)
  2. **Brahmanas accepting/reworking beliefs and practices** of other social categories
- Result: continuous **dialogue** between **"great" (Sanskritic Puranic) traditions** and **"little" (local) traditions**
- Key terms coined by sociologist **Robert Redfield**: "great" and "little" traditions
- **Example of integration**: **Puri, Orissa** — principal deity identified as **Jagannatha** (literally "lord of the world"), a form of Vishnu
  - Local deity whose image was **made of wood** by **local tribal specialists** was recognised as a form of Vishnu
  - Vishnu was visualised **differently** from other parts of the country
- **Goddess cults**: local deities (stone smeared with ochre) incorporated into Puranic framework as wives of male deities — equated with **Lakshmi** (wife of Vishnu) or **Parvati** (wife of Shiva)
- **Tantric practices**: widespread; open to women and men; **ignored caste and class** differences; influenced Shaivism and Buddhism
- **Vedic vs Puranic traditions**: Vedic deities (Agni, Indra, Soma) became marginal; Vishnu, Shiva, Goddess became central; **Vedas continued to be revered as authoritative** despite divergence
- **Conflicts**: Between those who valued Vedic tradition vs Tantric practitioners; between devotees of Vishnu/Shiva (each projecting their deity as supreme); with Buddhism and Jainism

#### Section B: Early Bhakti Traditions — Alvars and Nayanars

- **Bhakti traditions classified into two categories**:
  1. **Saguna** (with attributes): worship of specific deities — Shiva, Vishnu, his avatars, forms of Goddess — in **anthropomorphic forms**
  2. **Nirguna** (without attributes): worship of **abstract form** of God
- **Alvars** (literally: "those immersed in devotion to **Vishnu**") and **Nayanars** (literally: "leaders who were devotees of **Shiva**")
  - **c. 6th century** — earliest bhakti movements in **Tamil Nadu**
  - Travelled from place to place singing hymns in **Tamil**
  - Identified certain shrines as abodes of their chosen deities → later large temples built → **centres of pilgrimage**
- **Compositions**:
  - **Nalayira Divyaprabandham**: "Four Thousand Sacred Compositions" — anthology of 12 Alvars' compositions; frequently called the **"Tamil Veda"** (claiming equal importance to four Sanskrit Vedas)
  - **Tevaram**: collection of poems of **Appar, Sambandar, and Sundarar** (Nayanars); compiled and classified in 10th century
- **Attitude towards caste**:
  - Bhaktas from **diverse social backgrounds**: Brahmanas, artisans, cultivators, even "untouchable" castes
  - **Tondaradippodi** (Alvar, a Brahmana): "You manifestly like those 'servants' who express their love for your feet, though they may be born outcastes, more than the Chaturvedins"
  - **Appar** (Nayanar): "O rogues who quote the law books, of what use are your gotra and kula?"
- **Women devotees** — most striking feature:
  - **Andal** (Alvar): saw herself as beloved of Vishnu; compositions widely sung even today
  - **Karaikkal Ammaiyar** (Nayanar, Shiva devotee): adopted extreme asceticism; described herself as a female demon (pey) with bulging veins, protruding eyes; renounced social obligations; did NOT become a nun
  - Women's very existence and compositions posed a **challenge to patriarchal norms**
- **Relations with state**:
  - Nayanars showed **hostility to Buddhism and Jainism** — due to competition for **royal patronage**
  - **Chola rulers** (9th–13th centuries) supported Brahmanical and bhakti traditions:
    - Made **land grants**, constructed **temples** for Vishnu and Shiva
    - Built magnificent Shiva temples: **Chidambaram, Thanjavur, Gangaikondacholapuram**
    - Produced spectacular **bronze sculpture** of Shiva (Nataraja)
    - **Parantaka I** (c. 945) consecrated **metal images of Appar, Sambandar, Sundarar** in Shiva temple — carried in processions during festivals
    - Introduced **singing of Tamil Shaiva hymns** in royal-patronised temples
    - Collected and organised hymns into **Tevaram** text

#### Section C: Virashaiva Tradition in Karnataka

- Emerged in **12th century** in Karnataka
- Led by **Basavanna** (1106–68), a Brahmana who was minister in court of **Kalachuri ruler**
- Followers: **Virashaivas** (heroes of Shiva) or **Lingayats** (wearers of the linga)
- Worship Shiva as **linga**; men wear small linga in silver case on loop over left shoulder
- **Jangama** (wandering monks) are revered
- Believe: on death, devotee **united with Shiva** — will NOT return to this world → do NOT practise funerary rites like cremation; instead **ceremonially bury their dead**
- **Challenged caste** and "pollution" attributed to certain groups by Brahmanas
- **Questioned theory of rebirth**
- Won followers among **marginalised groups** within Brahmanical social order
- Encouraged practices disapproved in Dharmashastras: **post-puberty marriage**, **remarriage of widows**
- Understanding derived from **vachanas** (sayings) composed in **Kannada** by women and men

#### Section D: Islamic Traditions — New Strands

- **Contact with lands beyond seas** existed for millennia — Arab merchants on western coast (1st millennium CE); Central Asian peoples settled in north-west
- From 7th century: regions became part of **Islamic world**
- **Key political developments**:
  - **711**: Arab general **Muhammad Qasim** conquered **Sind** → became part of Caliph's domain
  - **c. 13th century**: Turks and Afghans established **Delhi Sultanate**
  - Formation of Sultanates in Deccan and elsewhere
  - **16th century**: **Mughal Empire**
- **Theoretical framework**: Muslim rulers to be guided by **ulama** (scholars of Islamic studies) to rule according to **shari'a**
  - Complicated because majority of subjects were non-Muslims
  - Category of **zimmi** developed (from Arabic **zimma** = protection) — for people following revealed scriptures (Jews, Christians); later extended to **Hindus**
  - Zimmis paid tax called **jizya**
- **Five pillars of Islam**:
  1. **Shahada** — One God (Allah), Muhammad is his messenger
  2. **Namaz/Salat** — Prayers five times a day
  3. **Zakat** — Giving alms
  4. **Sawm** — Fasting during Ramzan
  5. **Hajj** — Pilgrimage to Mecca
- **Diversities within Islam**: Sectarian affiliations (Sunni, Shi'a); influence of local customs
  - **Khojahs** (Ismaili/Shi'a sect): developed **ginan** (from Sanskrit *jnana* = knowledge) — devotional poems in Punjabi, Multani, Sindhi, etc., sung in special ragas
  - Arab Muslim traders on **Malabar coast** adopted **Malayalam**, local customs like **matriliny** and **matrilocal residence**
- **Architecture of mosques**: universal features (orientation towards Mecca, **mihrab** = prayer niche, **minbar** = pulpit) + local variations in roofs and building materials
- **Names for communities**: Terms "Hindu" and "Muslim" did not gain currency for long time
  - **Turkish rulers**: designated **Turushka**; people from Tajikistan = **Tajika**; from Persia = **Parashika**
  - Turks/Afghans referred to as **Shakas, Yavanas** (Greeks), or **mlechchha**
  - These terms rarely denoted a distinct religious community of Muslims in opposition to Hindus

#### Section E: Growth of Sufism

- **Sufis** turned to **asceticism and mysticism** in protest against growing **materialism of the Caliphate**
- Critical of **dogmatic definitions** and **scholastic methods** of interpreting Qur'an and sunna
- Emphasised: seeking salvation through **intense devotion and love for God**; following example of Prophet Muhammad as perfect human being
- Interpretation of Qur'an based on **personal experience** (tasawwuf)
- Word derivation debated: **suf** (wool), **safa** (purity), **suffa** (platform outside Prophet's mosque)

**Khanqahs and Silsilas:**
- By 11th century: Sufism evolved into well-developed movement
- **Khanqah** (Persian = hospice): controlled by teaching master known as **shaikh** (Arabic) or **pir/murshid** (Persian)
  - Enrolled disciples (**murids**); appointed successor (**khalifa**)
  - Established rules for spiritual conduct
- **Silsila** (literally "chain"): continuous link between master and disciple, stretching to Prophet Muhammad
  - Channel for transmitting **spiritual power and blessings**
  - Special **initiation rituals**: oath of allegiance, patched garment, shaving hair
- On shaikh's death: **dargah** (tomb-shrine) became centre of devotion
  - **Ziyarat** (pilgrimage) to grave, especially on **urs** (death anniversary = union with God)
  - Saints united with God in death → closer to Him → sought blessings for material/spiritual benefits
  - Cult of shaikh revered as **wali** (friend of God; plural: auliya)

**Outside the khanqah:**
- Some mystics initiated movements based on **radical interpretation** of sufi ideals
- **Scorned khanqah**; took to mendicancy; observed celibacy; ignored rituals; extreme asceticism
- Known as **Qalandars, Madaris, Malangs, Haidaris**
- Called **be-shari'a** (defied shari'a) in contrast to **ba-shari'a** sufis (complied with shari'a)

#### Section F: The Chishtis — Most Influential Sufi Group

- Of all sufi groups migrating to India in late 12th century, **Chishtis** were most influential
- Adapted successfully to local environment; adopted features of Indian devotional traditions
- Named after town of **Chisht** in central Afghanistan

**Major Chishti Saints:**

| Saint | Year of Death | Location of Dargah |
|-------|--------------|-------------------|
| Shaikh Muinuddin Sijzi | 1235 | **Ajmer** (Rajasthan) |
| Khwaja Qutbuddin Bakhtiyar Kaki | 1235 | Delhi |
| Shaikh Fariduddin Ganj-i Shakar | 1265 | **Ajodhan** (Pakistan) |
| Shaikh Nizamuddin Auliya | 1325 | Delhi |
| Shaikh Nasiruddin Chiragh-i Dehli | 1356 | Delhi |

**Life in the Chishti Khanqah (Shaikh Nizamuddin's at Ghiyaspur, Delhi, c. 14th century):**
- Several small rooms + big hall (**jama'at khana**) for inmates and visitors
- Shaikh lived in small room on roof; met visitors morning and evening
- **Langar** (open kitchen) run on **futuh** (unasked-for charity)
- Visitors from all walks of life: soldiers, slaves, singers, merchants, poets, travellers, rich and poor, **Hindu jogis and qalandars**
- People came seeking: **discipleship, amulets for healing, intercession** of Shaikh
- Notable visitors: poets **Amir Hasan Sijzi**, **Amir Khusrau**; court historian **Ziyauddin Barani**
- **Practices adopted from local traditions**: bowing before Shaikh, offering water to visitors, shaving heads of initiates, **yogic exercises**
- Shaikh Nizamuddin appointed spiritual successors → set up hospices across subcontinent → teachings spread rapidly

**Chishti Devotionalism:**
- **Ziyarat** (pilgrimage to tombs): prevalent for over 7 centuries
  - Most revered: **Khwaja Muinuddin's dargah** at Ajmer — called **"Gharib Nawaz"** (comforter of the poor)
  - First Sultan to visit: **Muhammad bin Tughlaq** (1324–51)
  - **Akbar** visited 14 times; offered huge **degh** (cauldron) in 1568; had mosque built within compound
  - On trade route linking Delhi and Gujarat → attracted travellers
- **Qawwali**: mystical music performed by **qawwals**
  - **Sama'** (literally "audition"): integral to Chishtis; exemplified interaction with indigenous devotional traditions
  - **Amir Khusrau** (1253–1325): gave unique form to Chishti sama'; introduced **qaul** (hymn sung at opening/closing of qawwali)
  - Sufis remember God through **zikr** (Divine Names) or **sama'**

**Languages and Communication:**
- Chishtis adopted **Hindavi** (language of people) in Delhi
- **Baba Farid** composed verses in local language → incorporated in **Guru Granth Sahib**
- **Masnavis** (long poems) using human love as allegory for divine love
- **Malik Muhammad Jayasi's Padmavat**: prem-akhyan (love story) of Padmini and Ratansen — soul's journey to the divine
- **Dakhani** (variant of Urdu) short poems near Bijapur, Karnataka — sung by women while grinding grain and spinning
- **Charkhanama**: song set to spinning wheel rhythm combining zikr with daily work

**Sufis and the State:**
- Chishti tradition: **austerity**, maintaining **distance from worldly power** — but NOT absolute isolation
- Accepted **unsolicited grants** and donations from political elites
- Sultans set up charitable trusts (**auqaf**) and granted **tax-free land (inam)** to hospices
- **Sultans sought sufis' legitimation**:
  - Sufis derived authority **directly from God** (did not depend on jurists to interpret shari'a)
  - Kings wanted their **tombs near sufi shrines**
  - **Auliya** believed to intercede with God
- **Conflict**: Both Sultans and sufis expected rituals — prostration, kissing feet; sufi shaikhs addressed with high-sounding titles (e.g., Nizamuddin Auliya addressed as **sultan-ul-mashaikh** = Sultan amongst shaikhs)

#### Section G: New Devotional Paths in Northern India

**Kabir (c. 14th–15th centuries):**
- Outstanding poet-saint; reconstructed through compositions and later hagiographies
- Verses compiled in **three distinct but overlapping traditions**:
  1. **Kabir Bijak** — preserved by Kabirpanth in Varanasi/UP
  2. **Kabir Granthavali** — associated with Dadupanth in Rajasthan
  3. Many compositions in **Adi Granth Sahib**
- All compiled long after Kabir's death; by 19th century, anthologies circulated from Bengal to Maharashtra
- Some poems in **sant bhasha** (special language of nirguna poets)
- **Ulatbansi** (upside-down sayings): everyday meanings inverted — e.g., "lotus which blooms without flower," "fire raging in the ocean"
- Drew on multiple traditions: **Islam** (Allah, Khuda, Hazrat, Pir), **Vedantic** (alakh, nirakar, Brahman, Atman), **yogic** (shabda, shunya)
- Used **monotheism and iconoclasm** to attack Hindu polytheism and idol worship; used **sufi zikr and ishq** to express **nam-simaran**
- Legacy claimed by several groups; hagiographies debated whether he was Hindu or Muslim by birth
- Described as born a Hindu (**Kabirdas**), raised by poor Muslim family of **weavers (julahs)**
- Possibly initiated by **Ramananda** — but historians note difficulty establishing contemporaneity
- Used terms **guru and satguru** but did NOT name any specific preceptor

**Baba Guru Nanak (1469–1539):**
- Born in Hindu merchant family in **Nankana Sahib** near river Ravi in **Punjab**
- Trained as accountant; studied Persian; married young but spent time among sufis and bhaktas; travelled widely
- Advocated **nirguna bhakti**
- **Rejected**: sacrifices, ritual baths, image worship, austerities, scriptures of both Hindus and Muslims
- The Absolute or "rab" had **no gender or form**
- Connected to Divine through **remembering and repeating the Divine Name**
- Expressed ideas through **shabad** (hymns) in **Punjabi**
- Sung compositions in various **ragas** while attendant **Mardana** played the **rabab**
- Organised followers into community; set up rules for **sangat** (congregational worship)
- Appointed **Angad** as successor → practice followed for ~200 years
- **Guru Arjan** (5th preceptor): compiled hymns of Nanak + 4 successors + other poets (Baba Farid, Ravidas, Kabir) in **Adi Granth Sahib**
- Late 17th century: **Guru Gobind Singh** (10th preceptor) included compositions of 9th guru Guru Tegh Bahadur → called **Guru Granth Sahib**
- Laid foundation of **Khalsa Panth** (army of the pure); defined **five symbols**: uncut hair, dagger, pair of shorts, comb, steel bangle

**Mirabai (c. 15th–16th centuries):**
- Best-known woman poet in bhakti tradition
- **Rajput princess from Merta** in Marwar; married to prince of **Sisodia clan of Mewar, Rajasthan**
- **Defied husband**; recognised **Krishna** (avatar of Vishnu) as her lover
- In-laws tried to **poison her**; she escaped to live as **wandering saint**
- Songs characterised by **intense emotional expression**
- "What can Mewar's ruler do to me? If God is angry, all is lost, but what can the Rana do?"
- Preceptor reportedly **Raidas** (a leather worker) — indicating defiance of caste norms
- Donned **white robes of widow** or **saffron robe of renouncer**
- Did not attract sect or group; recognised as source of inspiration for centuries
- Songs continue to be sung by women and men, especially poor and "low caste," in **Gujarat and Rajasthan**

**Shankaradeva** (late 15th century, Assam):
- Leading proponent of **Vaishnavism** in Assam
- Teachings known as **Bhagavati dharma** (based on Bhagavad Gita and Bhagavata Purana)
- Focused on **absolute surrender to supreme deity (Vishnu)**
- Emphasised **naam kirtan** (recitation of names of lord) in **sat sangas** (congregations)
- Established **satras** (monasteries) for transmitting spiritual knowledge
- Established **naam ghars** (prayer halls)
- Major composition: **Kirtana-ghosha**

- **Rejection of religious division**: Distinctions between religions are human inventions
- **Universalist message**: Rejected sectarian divisions; drew from both Islamic and Hindu traditions

#### Q1 (8 marks): "Examine the major beliefs and practices that characterised Sufism. How did the Chishtis adapt to the Indian environment?"

**Answer Structure:**

**Introduction:**
Sufism emerged as a movement of religious-minded people who turned to asceticism and mysticism in protest against the materialism of the Caliphate. By the 11th century, it had evolved into a well-developed movement with distinctive institutions, practices, and literary traditions.

**Body — Core Beliefs:**
- Critical of **dogmatic definitions** and **scholastic methods** of interpreting the Qur'an
- Emphasised **intense devotion and love for God** as path to salvation
- Followed the example of Prophet Muhammad as a **perfect human being**
- Sought interpretation of Qur'an based on **personal experience** rather than rigid textual reading
- Five pillars of Islam accepted in principle, but overlaid with sectarian and local diversities

**Body — Institutions:**
- **Khanqah** (hospice): controlled by **shaikh/pir/murshid**; enrolled **murids**; appointed **khalifa** (successor)
- **Silsila** (chain): continuous spiritual genealogy to the Prophet — channel for transmitting spiritual power
- **Dargah** (tomb-shrine): became centre of devotion after shaikh's death; **ziyarat** (pilgrimage), especially on **urs** (death anniversary = union with God)
- Initiation rituals: oath of allegiance, patched garment, shaving hair

**Body — Chishti Adaptation to India:**
The Chishtis were most influential because they **adapted successfully to the local environment**:
1. **Language**: Conversing in **Hindavi** (Delhi); Baba Farid composed in local language → incorporated in Guru Granth Sahib; masnavis and prem-akhyans (Jayasi's Padmavat)
2. **Musical traditions**: **Sama'** integral to Chishtis; **Amir Khusrau** introduced **qaul** and shaped **qawwali**; interaction with indigenous devotional traditions
3. **Social practices**: Bowing before Shaikh, offering water, shaving heads of initiates, **yogic exercises** — borrowed from local traditions
4. **Open kitchen (langar)**: Run on **futuh** (unasked charity); attracted people from all walks of life — soldiers, slaves, poets, merchants, Hindu jogis and qalandars
5. **Dakhani poetry**: Near Bijapur, Karnataka — short poems sung by women during household work (grinding, spinning) — inspired by pre-existing Kannada vachanas and Marathi abhangs

**Body — Relationship with State:**
- Maintained **austerity and distance** from worldly power — but NOT absolute isolation
- Accepted donations from political elites; Sultans set up **auqaf** (charitable trusts) and granted **inam** (tax-free land)
- Sultans sought **legitimation** from sufis (derived authority directly from God, not from ulama)
- **Conflict** over rituals of deference (prostration, titles)

**Conclusion:**
The Chishtis' genius lay in their flexibility — they maintained core Islamic beliefs while absorbing Indian devotional culture. Their khanqahs became sites of inter-religious dialogue, their sama' integrated with local musical traditions, and their dargahs attracted devotees of all faiths. This adaptability made Sufism one of the most enduring religious traditions of the subcontinent.

#### Q2 (8 marks): "Discuss the major teachings of Kabir. How have these been transmitted and why do they remain relevant?"

**Answer Structure:**

**Introduction:**
Kabir (c. 14th–15th centuries) is one of the most remarkable poet-saints of medieval India. His teachings challenged entrenched religious and social institutions in the quest for the Divine.

**Body — Core Teachings:**
- **Ultimate Reality**: Described using terms from multiple traditions — **Allah, Khuda, Hazrat, Pir** (Islam); **Alakh, Nirakar, Brahman, Atman** (Vedanta); **Shabda, Shunya** (yoga)
- **Monotheism**: One God worshipped under different names — "Gold may be shaped into rings and bangles. Isn't it gold all the same?"
- **Rejection of ritual**: Criticised idol worship, sacrifices, temple/mosque orthodoxy
- **Nirguna tradition**: Worship of abstract form; used **ulatbansi** (upside-down sayings) — "lotus which blooms without flower," "fire raging in the ocean"
- **Social critique**: Challenged caste system, religious division; described as raised by poor Muslim family of **weavers (julahs)**
- **Ishq and zikr**: Used sufi concepts of love and remembrance to express Hindu practice of **nam-simaran**

**Body — Transmission:**
- Compiled in **three distinct but overlapping traditions**:
  1. **Kabir Bijak** (Varanasi — Kabirpanth)
  2. **Kabir Granthavali** (Rajasthan — Dadupanth)
  3. **Adi Granth Sahib** (Sikh scripture)
- Some in **sant bhasha** (special language of nirguna poets)
- Manuscript compilations made **long after Kabir's death**
- By 19th century, anthologies circulated from Bengal to Gujarat to Maharashtra
- **Multiple layers**: Later additions and modifications make it difficult to determine which verses are authentically Kabir's
- Scholars analyse **language, style, and content** to identify authentic verses

**Body — Legacy and Relevance:**
- Hagiographies debated whether Hindu or Muslim by birth — showing his legacy claimed by multiple communities
- Source of inspiration for those who **questioned entrenched religious and social institutions**
- His **syncretic message** remains powerful: rejection of religious division, emphasis on inner devotion over external ritual
- Continues to be sung and quoted across communities

**Conclusion:**
Kabir's enduring significance lies in his radical egalitarianism and his insistence that the Divine transcends all religious labels. His poems, transmitted through multiple traditions, continue to challenge sectarian divisions and remind us that "distinctions are only words we invent."

#### Q3 (6 marks): "Examine how and why rulers tried to establish connections with the traditions of the Nayanars and the Sufis."

**Answer Structure:**

**Introduction:**
Rulers across medieval India recognised the immense popular appeal of bhakti saints and sufi mystics, and sought to establish connections with their traditions as a means of legitimising their authority.

**Body — Cholas and the Nayanars:**
- Built magnificent Shiva temples: **Chidambaram, Thanjavur, Gangaikondacholapuram**
- Produced spectacular **bronze sculptures** of Shiva (Nataraja) — inspired by visions of Nayanars
- Introduced **singing of Tamil Shaiva hymns** under royal patronage
- **Parantaka I** (c. 945) consecrated **metal images** of Appar, Sambandar, Sundarar in Shiva temple — carried in processions
- Collected and organised hymns into **Tevaram**
- Purpose: Claim **divine support**, proclaim power and status by building temples adorned with visions of popular saints

**Body — Mughals and the Sufis:**
- **Akbar** visited Ajmer dargah **14 times** (sometimes 2–3 times a year) until 1580; offered **degh** (cauldron) in 1568; built mosque within compound
- **Jahanara** wrote **Munis al-Arwah** (biography of Shaikh Muinuddin); went barefoot to Ajmer, rubbed face with dust of threshold
- **Shah Jahan** built **dargah of Shaikh Salim Chishti** at Fatehpur Sikri — symbolised bond between Chishtis and Mughals
- Sultans sought **legitimation** from sufis because:
  1. Sufis derived authority **directly from God** (not from ulama)
  2. Avoided imposing shari'a as state law (anticipated opposition from non-Muslim majority)
  3. **Auliya** believed to **intercede with God** for improvement of material/spiritual conditions
  4. Kings wanted **tombs near sufi shrines**
- Sufis accepted **auqaf** and **inam** but Chishtis preferred to use funds immediately rather than accumulate

**Conclusion:**
Rulers sought connections with bhakti and sufi traditions because these saints commanded immense popular devotion. By associating with them — building temples, visiting dargahs, making endowments — rulers gained divine legitimacy and the support of the masses, while the saints sometimes maintained their independence and moral authority.

# THEME 7: AN IMPERIAL CAPITAL: VIJAYANAGARA
## c. Fourteenth to Sixteenth Century | ~12 Marks — 🔴 HIGH PRIORITY

---

### 1. CBSE VALUE POINTS (Examiner Keywords)

#### Discovery and Sources

- **Vijayanagara** = "city of victory" — name of both city and empire
- Founded in **14th century**; stretched from river **Krishna** (north) to extreme **south** of peninsula
- **1565**: city sacked and subsequently deserted
- Remembered as **Hampi** — from local mother goddess **Pampadevi**
- **Colin Mackenzie** (1754–1821): engineer, surveyor, cartographer; **first Surveyor General of India** (1815)
  - Prepared **first survey map** of site in **1800**
  - Information from **priests of Virupaksha temple** and shrine of Pampadevi
  - Believed studying Vijayanagara would help EIC governance
- From **1856**: photographers recorded monuments
- From **1836**: epigraphists collected inscriptions
- Historians collated: inscriptions + accounts of **foreign travellers** + literature in **Telugu, Kannada, Tamil, Sanskrit**
- **1976**: Hampi recognised as site of national importance
- **1986**: Declared **World Heritage Site by UNESCO**
- **1980s**: Major project launched — area divided into **25 squares** (lettered) → further subdivided → meticulous surveys recovering thousands of structures

#### Founding and Dynasties

- Tradition + epigraphic evidence: two brothers **Harihara and Bukka** founded empire in **1336**
- Called **Karnataka samrajyamu** by contemporaries
- Built on traditions of earlier states: **Cholas** (Tamil Nadu — Brihadishvara temple at Thanjavur), **Hoysalas** (Karnataka — Chennakeshava temple at Belur)
- Rulers called themselves **rayas**

**Dynastic succession:**
| Dynasty | Period | Notes |
|---------|--------|-------|
| **Sangama** | till 1485 | First dynasty |
| **Saluvas** | 1485–1503 | Military commanders |
| **Tuluvas** | 1503–end of 17th c. | Krishnadeva Raya belonged to this dynasty |
| **Aravidu** | from 1542 | Last dynasty; ruled from Penukonda, then Chandragiri |

#### Krishnadeva Raya (ruled 1509–29) — MOST IMPORTANT RULER

- Belonged to **Tuluva dynasty**
- **Expansion and consolidation**:
  - Acquired **Raichur doab** (land between Tungabhadra and Krishna rivers) in **1512**
  - Subdued rulers of **Orissa** in **1514**
  - Inflicted severe defeats on Sultan of **Bijapur** in **1520**
- Flourished under conditions of **unparalleled peace and prosperity**
- Built fine temples, added impressive **gopurams** to many temples
- Founded suburban township **Nagalapuram** (after his mother)
- Composed **Amuktamalyada** (work on statecraft in Telugu)
- Described as "of medium height, fair complexion, good figure, rather fat than thin; has on his face signs of smallpox" (Paes)

#### Decline and Fall

- After Krishnadeva Raya's death (**1529**): strain; rebellious **nayakas**
- **1565**: **Battle of Rakshasi-Tangadi** (also called **Talikota**)
  - **Rama Raya** (chief minister) led army against combined armies of **Bijapur, Ahmadnagar, and Golconda**
  - Forces **routed**; victorious armies **sacked** the city
  - City **totally abandoned** within a few years
- **Note**: Relations between Sultans and rayas were **NOT always hostile** — Krishnadeva Raya supported claimants in Sultanates; Sultan of Bijapur intervened in Vijayanagara succession disputes. It was **Rama Raya's adventurous policy** of playing one Sultan against another that led to their alliance

#### Amara-Nayaka System

- Major **political innovation** of Vijayanagara Empire
- Features likely derived from **iqta system** of Delhi Sultanate
- **Amara-nayakas** = military commanders given territories to govern by the raya
- Collected **taxes and dues** from peasants, craftspersons, traders
- Retained part for personal use + maintaining stipulated contingent of **horses and elephants**
- Some revenue used for maintenance of **temples and irrigation works**
- Sent **tribute** to king annually; appeared in court with gifts
- Kings occasionally transferred them to assert control
- During 17th century: many nayakas established **independent kingdoms** → hastened collapse of imperial structure

#### The Capital City — Physical Layout

**Water Resources:**
- Natural basin formed by river **Tungabhadra** (flows north-easterly)
- Stunning **granite hills** forming girdle around city
- Embankments built along streams → **reservoirs** of varying sizes
- **Kamalapuram tank** (early 15th century): irrigated fields + conducted water to royal centre
- **Hiriya canal**: drew water from dam across Tungabhadra; irrigated valley between sacred centre and urban core; built by **Sangama dynasty** kings

**Fortifications:**
- **Abdur Razzaq** noted **seven lines of forts**
- Encircled city + agricultural hinterland + forests
- Outermost wall linked hills surrounding city
- **No mortar or cementing agent** used — stone blocks **wedge-shaped** (held in place); inner portion = earth packed with rubble
- **Square/rectangular bastions** projected outwards
- **Significance**: Enclosed **agricultural tracts** — to protect food supply during sieges (which could last months/years)
- Abdur Razzaq: "between the first, second and third walls there are cultivated fields, gardens and houses"
- Paes: rice fields, gardens, much water from two lakes between walls
- Second line around inner urban complex; third around royal centre

**Gateways and Roads:**
- Well-guarded gates linked city to major roads
- **Arch and dome** on gateway — features introduced by Turkish Sultans
- Style termed **Indo-Islamic** — grew through interaction with local building practices
- Roads wound around valleys, avoiding rocky terrain
- Important roads extended from temple gateways, lined by bazaars

#### The Royal Centre (South-Western part)

- Included over **60 temples** (patronage of temples = legitimising authority)
- About **30 building complexes** identified as palaces (relatively large, not associated with ritual functions)
- Difference: temples = entirely masonry; secular buildings' superstructure = perishable materials

**Key structures:**
- **"King's palace"** (largest enclosure — no definitive evidence of being royal residence)
- **"Audience hall"**: high platform with slots for wooden pillars; staircase to second floor
- **"Mahanavami dibba"**:
  - Massive platform: base ~**11,000 sq. ft**, height **40 ft**
  - Located on one of highest points in city
  - Evidence of wooden structure on top
  - Base covered with **relief carvings**
  - Rituals coincided with **Mahanavami** (great ninth day) — 10-day Hindu festival (September/October)
  - Known as **Dusehra** (north), **Durga Puja** (Bengal), **Navaratri/Mahanavami** (peninsular India)
  - Ceremonies: worship of image, worship of **state horse**, sacrifice of buffaloes/animals; dances, wrestling matches, processions of horses/elephants/chariots/soldiers; ritual presentations by nayakas
  - Last day: king inspected army in open field; nayakas brought gifts and tribute
  - Also called **"House of Victory"** by Paes
- **"Lotus Mahal"**: so named by 19th-century British travellers; may have been **council chamber** (king met advisers); features arches inspired by Indo-Islamic techniques; nine towers
- **"Elephant stables"**: near Lotus Mahal
- **Hazara Rama temple**: probably meant for king and family only; images in central shrine missing; walls have sculpted panels with **Ramayana scenes**

#### The Sacred Centre (Rocky northern end)

- Associated with several sacred traditions:
  - **Ramayana** tradition (sheltered monkey kingdom of Vali and Sugriva)
  - **Pampadevi** (local mother goddess) did penance to marry **Virupaksha** (form of Shiva — guardian deity of kingdom)
  - Marriage celebrated annually in Virupaksha temple
  - **Jaina temples** of pre-Vijayanagara period also present
- Temple building had long history: **Pallavas, Chalukyas, Hoysalas, Cholas**
- Rulers encouraged temple building to:
  - Associate with the divine (deity identified with king)
  - Function as centres of learning
  - Win support and recognition for power, wealth, and piety
- **Vijayanagara kings claimed to rule on behalf of god Virupaksha**
- All royal orders signed **"Shri Virupaksha"** in Kannada script
- Kings used title **"Hindu Suratrana"** (Sanskritisation of Arabic "Sultan" = Hindu Sultan)
- **Royal portrait sculpture** displayed in temples; king's visits to temples treated as important state occasions

**Architectural Features:**
- **Raya gopurams** (royal gateways): immense scale; dwarfed towers on central shrines; visible from great distance; signalled presence of temple; reminded of king's power
- **Mandapas** (pavilions) and **long, pillared corridors** running around shrines

**Virupaksha Temple:**
- Built over centuries; earliest shrine dated to **9th–10th centuries**
- Substantially enlarged with establishment of Vijayanagara Empire
- **Hall in front of main shrine** built by **Krishnadeva Raya** to mark his accession — decorated with **delicately carved pillars**
- Also credited with construction of **eastern gopuram**
- Halls used for: music/dance/drama; celebrating marriages of deities; deities swinging
- Central shrine now occupies relatively small part of complex

**Vitthala Temple:**
- Principal deity: **Vitthala** (form of Vishnu, generally worshipped in Maharashtra)
- Introduction of this deity in Karnataka = example of rulers drawing on different traditions to create **imperial culture**
- Has several halls and a **unique shrine designed as a chariot**
- **Chariot streets**: extended from temple gopuram in straight line; paved with stone slabs; lined with **pillared pavilions** where merchants set up shops

#### Bazaars and Trade

- **Domingo Paes** described: "broad and beautiful street" with merchants selling rubies, diamonds, emeralds, pearls, seed-pearls, cloths; evening fair with horses, fruits, vegetables, wood
- City "best-provided city in the world" — rice, wheat, pulses abundant and cheap
- **Fernao Nuniz**: markets "overflowing with abundance of fruits" — grapes, oranges, limes, pomegranates, jackfruit, mangoes; meat sold in abundance
- **Horse trade**: critical for warfare — import from Arabia and Central Asia; controlled by Arab traders; **kudirai chettis** (horse merchants) participated
- **Portuguese** arrived from **1498** — established trading/military stations on west coast; used **muskets**
- Markets dealt in **spices, textiles, precious stones**
- Revenue from trade contributed significantly to state prosperity

### 5. PREDICTED LONG ANSWERS

#### Q1 (8 marks): "Discuss the architectural traditions that inspired the architects of Vijayanagara. How did they transform these traditions?"

**Answer Structure:**

**Introduction:**
Vijayanagara's architecture was a synthesis of earlier South Indian traditions and Indo-Islamic innovations, creating a distinctive imperial style that projected the authority and piety of the ruling dynasty.

**Body — Earlier Traditions Drawn Upon:**
- **Pallavas, Chalukyas, Hoysalas, Cholas**: temple building had a long history in the region
  - Cholas: Brihadishvara temple at Thanjavur
  - Hoysalas: Chennakeshava temple at Belur
- Rulers of Vijayanagara "built on these traditions and carried them literally to new heights"

**Body — Transformations and Innovations:**
1. **Raya gopurams**: Royal gateways of immense scale that **dwarfed towers on central shrines** — signalled presence of temple from great distance; reminders of king's power to command resources and skills
2. **Mandapas and pillared corridors**: Long corridors running around shrines; halls for music, dance, drama, deity marriages
3. **Virupaksha temple**: Hall with **delicately carved pillars** built by Krishnadeva Raya; eastern gopuram added
4. **Vitthala temple**: Unique shrine as **stone chariot**; introduction of Maharashtra deity (Vitthala/Vishnu) showing **imperial culture** drawing on diverse traditions
5. **Chariot streets**: Straight streets from temple gopuram, paved with stone slabs, lined with pillared pavilion shops — merging commercial and sacred space
6. **Indo-Islamic features**: Arches and domes on gateways (from Turkish Sultans); Lotus Mahal with arches; "elephant stables" with domes
7. **Royal portrait sculpture**: New practice — displayed in temples
8. **Mahanavami dibba**: Massive platform (11,000 sq ft base, 40 ft high) with relief carvings — combining ritual and political function

**Body — Building Techniques:**
- **No mortar** — wedge-shaped stone blocks held in place; inner portion of rubble and earth
- Temples entirely of **masonry**; secular buildings used **perishable materials** for superstructure (now lost)
- **Elaborate water management**: Kamalapuram tank, Hiriya canal, embankments creating reservoirs, aqueducts

**Conclusion:**
Vijayanagara architects transformed regional temple traditions into an imperial architectural language. The fusion of Dravidian gopurams with Indo-Islamic arches, the monumental scale of royal gateways, and the integration of commercial and sacred spaces made Vijayanagara's architecture uniquely representative of its multicultural empire.

#### Q2 (6 marks): "What was the significance of the mahanavami dibba? Describe the rituals associated with this structure."

**Answer Structure:**

**Introduction:**
The mahanavami dibba was one of the most impressive structures in Vijayanagara's royal centre, located on one of the highest points in the city. It served as the focal point for the empire's most important political and religious festival.

**Body — Physical Description:**
- Massive platform: base approximately **11,000 sq. ft**, height **40 ft**
- Evidence of wooden structure on top
- Base covered with **relief carvings** depicting elephants, horses, dancers, processions

**Body — Rituals (Mahanavami/Navaratri):**
- 10-day Hindu festival during autumn (September/October)
- Called **Dusehra** (north India), **Durga Puja** (Bengal), **Mahanavami/Navaratri** (peninsular India)
- Ceremonies performed:
  1. **Worship of the image** (of the deity)
  2. **Worship of the state horse**
  3. **Sacrifice of buffaloes and other animals**
  4. **Dances, wrestling matches**
  5. **Processions** of caparisoned horses, elephants, chariots, and soldiers
  6. **Ritual presentations** before king by chief nayakas and subordinate kings
  7. **Last day**: King inspected his army + armies of nayakas in open field
  8. Nayakas brought **rich gifts** and stipulated tribute

**Body — Political Significance:**
- Displayed **prestige, power, and suzerainty** of the king
- Reinforced hierarchy: king at centre, nayakas expressing loyalty through gifts/tribute
- Military display served as both celebration and deterrence

**Conclusion:**
The mahanavami dibba was not merely a religious structure but a political instrument — the annual ritual reaffirmed the king's authority over his military chiefs while simultaneously demonstrating the empire's military strength to all observers.

# THEME 8: PEASANTS, ZAMINDARS AND THE STATE
## Agrarian Society and the Mughal Empire (c. Sixteenth–Seventeenth Centuries) | ~14 Marks — 🔴 HIGHEST PRIORITY

---

### 1. CBSE VALUE POINTS (Examiner Keywords)

#### Section A: Peasants and Agricultural Production

- **85%** of India's population lived in villages (16th–17th centuries)
- Both **peasants and landed elites** involved in agricultural production, claiming share of produce
- **Relationships**: cooperation, competition, and conflict
- **Mughal state** derived bulk of income from agricultural production
- State agents: revenue assessors, collectors, record keepers

**Sources for Agrarian History:**
- Peasants did not write about themselves
- Major source: chronicles and documents from Mughal court
- **Ain-i Akbari** by **Abu'l Fazl** — most important chronicle
  - Meticulously recorded: state arrangements for cultivation, revenue collection, relationship between state and zamindars
  - Presented vision of Akbar's empire where **social harmony** provided by strong ruling class
  - "View from the top" — supplemented by regional records
- Supplementary sources: detailed revenue records from **Gujarat, Maharashtra, Rajasthan** (17th–18th centuries); East India Company records for eastern India

**Peasant Types:**
- Terms: **raiyat** (plural riaya) or **muzarian**; also **kisan** or **asami**
- **Khud-kashta**: residents of village where they held land
- **Pahi-kashta**: non-resident cultivators from other village, cultivated on contractual basis
  - Choice (better revenue terms elsewhere) or compulsion (famine)
- Average peasant: pair of bullocks + two ploughs (most had even less)
- Gujarat: **6 acres** = affluent; Bengal: **5 acres** = average farm, **10 acres** = rich asami
- **Individual ownership** of land — bought and sold

**Irrigation and Technology:**
- Three factors for agricultural expansion: **abundance of land, available labour, mobility of peasants**
- Basic staples: **rice, wheat, millets** (most frequently cultivated)
- 40+ inches rainfall → rice zones; descending → wheat → millets
- **Monsoons** backbone; artificial irrigation for additional water
- Irrigation devices (from Babur Nama):
  - **Lahore, Dipalpur**: **wheel** ( Persian wheel) — buckets on rope circles turned by bullocks via gears
  - **Agra, Chandwar, Bayana**: **bucket** — rope over fork of wood with roller, tied to bullock
- State support: digging new canals (**nahr, nala**); repairing old ones — **shahnahr** in Punjab (Shah Jahan's reign)
- Technologies: **wooden plough** (light, iron tip/coulter — did not make deep furrows, preserved moisture); **drill** (pulled by giant oxen for planting); **broadcasting** most prevalent; narrow iron blade for hoeing/weeding

**Crop Diversity:**
- Two seasonal cycles: **kharif** (autumn) and **rabi** (spring)
- Minimum **two crops/year** (**do-fasla**); some regions **three crops**
- Agra: **39 varieties** of crops; Delhi: **43**; Bengal: **50 varieties of rice** alone
- **Jins-i kamil** (perfect crops) — encouraged by state for more revenue: **cotton, sugarcane** (also oilseeds, lentils)
- New crops from New World (17th century): **maize** (via Africa/Spain), **tomatoes, potatoes, chillies, pineapple, papaya**
- **Tobacco**: arrived first in Deccan; Ain does NOT mention it in northern India lists; Akbar/nobles first encountered it in **1604**; Jahangir **banned** it (ineffective); by end of 17th century: major article of consumption, cultivation, and trade

#### Section B: Village Community — Panchayats and Headmen

- Three constituents: **cultivators, panchayat, village headman (muqaddam or mandal)**

**Caste and Rural Milieu:**
- Deep inequities based on caste
- **Majur** (menials/agricultural labourers) — large section with least resources
- Muslim communities: **halalkhoran** (scavengers) housed outside village; **mallahzadas** (sons of boatmen) in Bihar comparable to slaves
- **Direct correlation** between caste, poverty, and social status at lower strata
- Mobility within caste hierarchy: **Gauravas** (around Vrindavan) sought Rajput status; **Ahirs, Gujars, Malis** rose due to cattle rearing/horticulture profitability; **Sadgops, Kaivartas** (eastern regions) acquired peasant status

**Panchayats:**
- **Assembly of elders** — important people with hereditary property rights
- In mixed-caste villages: **heterogeneous body**; oligarchy; village menials unlikely to be represented
- Decisions were **binding**
- Headed by **muqaddam/mandal** — chosen through consensus of elders, ratified by zamindar; dismissible if lost confidence
- Chief function: supervise preparation of **village accounts** (assisted by **patwari/accountant**)
- Derived funds from **individual contributions** to common pool
- Used for: entertaining revenue officials; community welfare (natural calamities); construction of bunds/canals
- Ensured **caste boundaries** were upheld; in eastern India, all marriages held in presence of mandal
- Authority to levy **fines** and **expulsion from community** (most drastic — person became outcaste, lost profession rights)
- Each **jati** (caste) had its own **jati panchayat** — wielded considerable power; arbitrated civil disputes (in Rajasthan); mediated land claims; decided marriage norms
- State respected jati panchayat decisions (except criminal matters)
- **Petitions** to panchayat: from lowest rungs; about extortionate taxation or **begar** (unpaid labour); panchayat = court of appeal
- In cases of excessive revenue demands: panchayat suggested **compromise**; if reconciliation failed → peasants **deserted village** (effective weapon — easy availability of uncultivated land)

**Village Artisans:**
- Up to **25%** of total village households
- Potters, blacksmiths, carpenters, barbers, goldsmiths — provided specialised services
- Compensated by: **share of harvest**, **allotment of land** (decided by panchayat)
- In Maharashtra: such lands became **miras** or **watan** (hereditary holding)
- Another variant: zamindars in Bengal paid "small daily allowance and diet money" — later called **jajmani system**
- Cash remuneration not entirely unknown
- Distinction between artisans and peasants was **fluid** — cultivators participated in craft production during agricultural lulls
- **Cash nexus** had developed: revenue assessed/collected in cash; weavers received advances in cash; producers of cotton, silk, indigo paid in cash
- NOT a "little republic" of fraternal partners — there was individual ownership, deep inequities based on caste and gender

#### Section C: Women in Agrarian Society

- **Men and women worked shoulder to shoulder** in fields
- Men: tilled and ploughed; Women: sowed, weeded, threshed, winnowed
- **Basis of production** = labour and resources of entire household
- **No gendered segregation** between home and field possible
- **Biological biases continued**: menstruating women not allowed to touch plough/potter's wheel (western India) or enter betel-leaf groves (Bengal)
- **Artisanal tasks dependent on female labour**: spinning yarn, sifting, kneading clay, embroidery
- More commercialised product = greater demand on women's labour
- Women worked in fields AND went to employers' houses and markets
- **Resource as child bearers** (labour-dependent society)
- High mortality rates (malnutrition, frequent pregnancies, death in childbirth) → shortage of wives
- **Customs distinct from elite groups**: **bride-price** (not dowry) to bride's family; **remarriage** legitimate for divorced and widowed women
- **Male control over women**: household headed by male; draconian punishments for suspected infidelity
- Women petitioned panchayat for redress (infidelity of husbands, neglect); but named as "mother/sister/wife of" male head
- **Property rights**: Among landed gentry, women had right to inherit; widows participated in rural land market; Hindu and Muslim women inherited **zamindaris** — free to sell or mortgage; women zamindars known in 18th-century Bengal (e.g., **Rajshahi** zamindari had woman at helm)

#### Section D: Forests and Tribes

- Forests covered approximately **40%** of India (informed estimate)
- Forest dwellers termed **jangli** — NOT meaning absence of "civilisation" but livelihood from gathering, hunting, shifting agriculture
- Activities **season-specific** (Bhils example): spring = forest produce; summer = fishing; monsoon = cultivation; autumn/winter = hunting
- **Mobility** was distinctive feature
- Forest = **subversive place (mawas)** for state — refuge for troublemakers
- **Babur**: jungles provided defence "behind which the people of the pargana become stubbornly rebellious and pay no taxes"

**Inroads into Forests:**
- State required **elephants** for army → **peshkash** (tribute) from forest people included elephant supply
- **Hunt** symbolised state's concern to relate to all subjects (Mughal ideology)
- Clearance for agricultural settlements (e.g., **Chandimangala** poem — Kaluketu cleared forest, distributed tools, established markets)
- **Trade**: forest products (honey, beeswax, **gum lac** — major overseas export in 17th century); elephants captured and sold; **barter** exchanges
- **Loha nis** (Punjab): overland trade between India and Afghanistan
- **Social transformation**: tribal chiefs became **zamindars**, some became **kings**; required armies
- **Ahom kings** (Assam): had **paiks** (people obliged to render military service for land); monopoly on wild elephant capture
- **Koch kings**: fought and subjugated neighbouring tribes (16th–17th centuries)
- **Cultural influences**: sufi saints (**pirs**) played role in acceptance of Islam among agricultural communities in newly colonised areas

#### Section E: The Zamindars

- **Landed proprietors** who did NOT participate directly in agricultural production
- Derived power from: **social status** (caste) + performing **khidmat** (services) for state
- Held extensive personal lands: **milkiyat** (property) — cultivated for private use with hired/servile labour; could **sell, bequeath, mortgage** at will
- Collected revenue on behalf of state (financially compensated)
- **Military resources**: fortresses (**qilachas**), armed contingent (cavalry, artillery, infantry)
- Abu'l Fazl: combined military strength = **384,558 cavalry, 4,277,057 infantry, 1,863 elephants, 4,260 cannons, 4,500 boats**
- Apex of rural society pyramid; "upper-caste" (Brahmana-Rajput) combined had firm control; also intermediate castes and Muslim zamindars

**Zamindari Consolidation:**
- Through: **colonisation of new lands**, **transfer of rights**, **state orders**, **purchase**
- **Buying and selling** of zamindaris accelerated **monetisation** in countryside
- Established **markets (haats)** for peasants to sell produce
- **Clan/lineage-based**: Rajputs and Jats consolidated over vast territories in north India; Sadgops in Bengal

**Relationship with Peasantry:**
- Exploitative class BUT with elements of **reciprocity, paternalism, and patronage**
- **Bhakti saints** condemned caste oppression but did NOT portray zamindars as exploiters (usually targeted revenue officials)
- In 17th-century agrarian uprisings, zamindars often received **peasant support** against the state

#### Section F: Land Revenue System

- Revenue from land = **economic mainstay** of Mughal Empire
- **Diwan**: office (daftar) responsible for supervising fiscal system
- Revenue officials and record keepers shaped agrarian relations

**Two Stages:**
1. **Assessment** → **jama** (amount assessed)
2. **Collection** → **hasil** (amount collected)

- Akbar's decree: amil-guzar should strive to make cultivators pay in **cash**; option of payment in **kind** kept open
- Both cultivated and cultivable lands measured in each province
- **Ain** compiled aggregates during Akbar's rule; Aurangzeb (1665) ordered annual records of cultivators in each village

**Land Classification (from Ain):**
| Category | Description |
|----------|-------------|
| **Polaj** | Annually cultivated; never allowed to lie fallow |
| **Parauti** | Left uncultivated for a time to recover strength |
| **Chachar** | Lay fallow for 3–4 years |
| **Banjar** | Uncultivated for 5+ years |

- First two kinds each had three classes: **good, middling, bad**
- Added together produce of each sort → **one-third** of medium produce = Royal dues

**Methods of Revenue Collection (from Ain):**
1. **Kankut**: kan (grain) + kut (estimates) — crops cut and estimated in three lots (good, middling, inferior)
2. **Batai/bhaoli**: crops reaped and stacked, divided by agreement (requires intelligent inspectors)
3. **Khet-batai**: divide fields after sowing
4. **Lang-batai**: after cutting, form heaps and divide

#### Section G: The Flow of Silver

- Mughal Empire among large territorial empires (with **Ming/China, Safavid/Iran, Ottoman/Turkey**)
- Political stability → vibrant **overland trade** from China to Mediterranean
- **New World** discovery → massive expansion of Asia's (especially India's) trade with Europe
- **Huge amounts of silver bullion** entered Asia to pay for Indian goods
- India had **no natural silver resources** — but huge inflow → **remarkable stability** of metal currency (especially silver **rupya**)
- Facilitated: unprecedented expansion of **minting**, **circulation of money**, ability of state to **extract taxes in cash**
- **Giovanni Careri** (Italian traveller, c. 1690): described how gold and silver from Americas circulated through Europe → Turkey → Persia → Arabia → Mocha, Basra → India; Dutch from Japan; all European purchases paid in ready money which remained in India

#### Section H: The Ain-i Akbari

- Culmination of large historical/administrative project by **Abu'l Fazl** at Akbar's order
- Completed in **1598** (42nd regnal year); gone through **five revisions**
- Part of larger **Akbar Nama** (three books):
  - Books 1 & 2: historical narrative
  - Book 3: **Ain-i Akbari** = compendium of imperial regulations + gazetteer of empire

**Five Books (Daftars):**
1. **Manzil-abadi**: imperial household and maintenance
2. **Sipah-abadi**: military and civil administration; biographical sketches of officials (mansabdars), learned men, poets, artists
3. **Mulk-abadi**: fiscal side; **revenue rates**, **"Account of the Twelve Provinces"** with detailed statistical information (geographic, topographic, economic profiles of subas; administrative/fiscal divisions: sarkars, parganas, mahals; measured area; assessed revenue/jama)
  - Eight columns for sarkars: pargana/mahal; qila (forts); measured area; naqdi (cash revenue); suyurghal (charity grants); zamindars; horsemen (sawar); foot-soldiers (piyada); elephants (fil)
4. **Fourth book**: religious, literary, cultural traditions
5. **Fifth book**: collection of Akbar's "auspicious sayings"

**Strengths:**
- Extraordinary document; far more than reproduction of official papers
- **Five revisions** show high degree of caution and search for authenticity
- Oral testimonies **cross-checked and verified**
- Numeric data reproduced **in words** to minimise transcriptional errors
- Unprecedented recording of information about empire AND people

**Limitations:**
- **Errors in totalling** (slips of arithmetic/transcription by assistants)
- **Skewed data**: not collected uniformly from all provinces (Bengal/Orissa data less complete)
- **Prices and wages** data mainly from areas around Agra — limited relevance for rest of country
- **View from the apex** — a view of society from the top

### 5. PREDICTED LONG ANSWERS

#### Q1 (8 marks): "Examine the role played by zamindars in Mughal India. Were they merely intermediaries between the state and the peasantry?"

**Answer Structure:**

**Introduction:**
Zamindars were landed proprietors who occupied the apex of rural society in Mughal India. Far from being simple intermediaries, they were a complex class with significant economic, military, and social power.

**Body — Basis of Power:**
1. **Social status**: Caste-based (Brahmana-Rajput combine dominated; also intermediate castes and Muslim zamindars)
2. **Khidmat**: Performed services for the state (revenue collection)
3. **Milkiyat lands**: Extensive personal property — cultivated with hired/servile labour; could sell, bequeath, mortgage at will
4. **Military resources**: Fortresses (qilachas); armed contingents — combined strength: 384,558 cavalry, 4,277,057 infantry, 1,863 elephants, 4,260 cannons

**Body — Functions Beyond Revenue Collection:**
- **Colonisation of agricultural land**: spearheaded settlement of new lands; provided cultivators with means of cultivation (cash loans)
- **Established markets (haats)**: facilitated trade
- **Monetisation**: Buying/selling of zamindaris accelerated monetisation in countryside
- **Patronage and reciprocity**: Relationship with peasantry had elements of reciprocity and paternalism — bhakti saints did NOT portray them as exploiters
- **Peasant support**: In 17th-century agrarian uprisings, zamindars often received peasant support against the state

**Body — Zamindari Consolidation:**
- Through: conquest, colonisation of new lands, transfer of rights, state orders, purchase
- **Buying and selling** was brisk — allowed even "lower" castes to enter zamindar ranks
- Clan/lineage consolidation: Rajputs and Jats in north India; Sadgops in Bengal

**Conclusion:**
Zamindars were far more than intermediaries. They were powerful landed proprietors with military resources, personal estates, and significant economic roles including colonisation, market establishment, and monetisation. Their relationship with the peasantry was complex — combining exploitation with paternalism — and their loyalty to the state was conditional. In the 17th century, rebellious zamindars hastened the decline of Mughal central authority.

#### Q2 (8 marks): "Describe the role of women in Mughal agrarian society. How did their position differ from elite women?"

**Answer Structure:**

**Introduction:**
In Mughal agrarian society, women were integral to agricultural and artisanal production. Their position and experiences differed significantly from elite women, shaped by the demands of peasant household economy.

**Body — Women's Economic Role:**
- **Shoulder to shoulder with men**: Women worked alongside men in fields
  - Men: tilled and ploughed
  - Women: sowed, weeded, threshed, winnowed
- Production based on **entire household's labour** — no gendered segregation between home and field
- **Artisanal tasks**: Spinning yarn, sifting, kneading clay, embroidery — all dependent on female labour
- Greater commercialisation of product → greater demand on women's labour
- Women worked in fields AND went to employers' houses and markets

**Body — Women as Reproductive Resource:**
- Valued as **child bearers** in labour-dependent society
- High mortality (malnutrition, frequent pregnancies, childbirth death) → shortage of wives
- Led to distinctive customs:
  - **Bride-price** (not dowry) to bride's family
  - **Remarriage** legitimate for divorced and widowed women
- Male control: household headed by male; draconian punishments for suspected infidelity

**Body — Body and Biological Restrictions:**
- Menstruating women: not allowed to touch plough or potter's wheel (western India); not to enter betel-leaf groves (Bengal)

**Body — Women and Panchayat:**
- Petitioned panchayat for redress: husband's infidelity, neglect of wife and children
- Named as "mother/sister/wife of" male head — **not as individuals**
- State and "superior" castes intervened to ensure family adequately provided for

**Body — Women as Property Owners:**
- Among landed gentry: right to **inherit property**
- Hindu and Muslim women inherited **zamindaris** — free to sell or mortgage
- Widows actively participated in rural land market
- **Women zamindars** known in 18th-century Bengal — **Rajshahi** zamindari (biggest and most famous) had woman at helm

**Conclusion:**
Women in agrarian society were neither confined to domestic spaces nor powerless. They were crucial economic agents, property owners in certain contexts, and active participants in village governance through petitions. Their customs (bride-price, remarriage) differed from elite practice. Yet they remained subject to patriarchal control — their identities recorded only through male relatives.

#### Q3 (6 marks): "Analyse the Ain-i Akbari as a source for reconstructing agrarian history. What are its strengths and limitations?"

**Answer Structure:**

**Introduction:**
The Ain-i Akbari, authored by Abu'l Fazl and completed in 1598, is the most important textual source for understanding agrarian relations in Mughal India. However, like all historical sources, it must be used critically.

**Body — Strengths:**
- **Comprehensive scope**: Three of five books deal with administration and fiscal matters
- **Quantitative data**: Revenue rates, measured areas, geographic/topographic/economic profiles of all subas (provinces), sarkars, parganas
- **Eight-column format**: Detailed information on forts, measured area, cash revenue, charity grants, zamindars, troops
- **Five revisions**: Shows caution and search for authenticity
- **Oral testimonies cross-checked** before incorporation
- **Numbers written in words** to minimise transcriptional errors
- Departed from tradition of only recording political events — recorded information about empire AND people

**Body — Limitations:**
- **View from the top**: Presents Akbar's empire as harmonious; any revolt "predestined to fail"
- **Arithmetic errors**: Detected in totalling by Abu'l Fazl's assistants
- **Skewed data**: Not collected uniformly — Bengal and Orissa have less complete data
- **Prices and wages**: Mainly from areas around Agra — limited relevance for rest of country
- **Does not capture peasant perspective**: Peasants did not write about themselves; we see them through state records
- **Peasant agency invisible**: Conflicts, resistance, petitions to panchayats need to be read against or supplementary to Ain's narrative

**Conclusion:**
Despite its limitations, the Ain-i Akbari remains an extraordinary document — a benchmark for studying India at the turn of the 17th century. Its quantitative evidence on agrarian relations is uncontested in value. However, historians must supplement it with regional records (Gujarat, Maharashtra, Rajasthan revenue records; East India Company records) and panchayat petitions to reconstruct the full picture of agrarian society — one characterised by peasant initiative, social differentiation, and frequent conflict, not just top-down imperial harmony.

#### Q4 (6 marks): "To what extent can agricultural production in the sixteenth-seventeenth centuries be characterised as subsistence agriculture?"

**Answer Structure:**

**Introduction:**
While basic staples formed the foundation of agricultural production, the Mughal countryside cannot be characterised as purely subsistence-oriented. Agriculture was a complex mix of subsistence and commercial production.

**Body — Evidence of Commercial Agriculture:**
- **Jins-i kamil** (perfect crops): Cotton, sugarcane, oilseeds — encouraged by state for higher revenue; grown for market
- **Cotton**: grown over great swathe of central India and Deccan plateau
- **Bengal**: famous for sugar production
- **New World crops**: Maize (via Africa/Spain), tomatoes, potatoes, chillies — integrated into farming
- **Tobacco**: spread from Deccan to become major article of consumption and trade
- **Cash nexus**: Revenue collected in cash; artisans paid advances in cash; producers of cotton/silk/indigo received cash wages
- **Trade links**: Villages connected to towns; silver flowing into India from across globe (Americas, Japan)
- **Zamindars established markets (haats)** for sale of produce
- **Precious metals** flowed in because Indian manufactures exported for gold/silver

**Body — Evidence of Subsistence Elements:**
- Basic staples: rice, wheat, millets most frequently cultivated
- Average peasant: pair of bullocks + two ploughs — limited capacity
- In Gujarat: 6 acres = affluent; Bengal: 5 acres = average — very small holdings
- Monsoon-dependent; vulnerable to droughts and famines

**Conclusion:**
Agricultural production was NOT purely subsistence. Subsistence and commercial production were "closely intertwined in an average peasant's holding." The jins-i kamil system, monetisation of revenue, integration with overseas trade networks, and the inflow of silver all point to a commercially vibrant countryside. However, vulnerability to natural disasters and small holdings ensured that subsistence remained a concern for most peasants.
