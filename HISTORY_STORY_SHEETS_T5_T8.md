# 🟤 THEME 5: THROUGH THE EYES OF TRAVELLERS — Perceptions of Society (c. 10th–17th century)

## 🎬 The story of the chapter
Three foreigners — a captured scholar from Central Asia, a Moroccan wanderer who couldn't stop walking, and a French doctor looking for a debate — wrote about India from three different moods: Al-Biruni came to understand, Ibn Battuta came to marvel, Bernier came to compare and condemn. Their writings show us their own societies as much as ours — and teach the historian's first lesson: every traveller sees through a lens.

## 1. Al-Biruni and the Kitab-ul-Hind

**From Khwarizm to the Punjab.** **Al-Biruni was born in 973, in Khwarizm, in present-day Uzbekistan.** Khwarizm was an **important centre of learning**, and Al-Biruni received the **best education available at the time**; he was well versed in **several languages — Syriac, Arabic, Persian, Hebrew and Sanskrit**. He did **not know Greek**, but was familiar with the works of **Plato and other Greek philosophers through Arabic translations**. In **1017**, when **Sultan Mahmud invaded Khwarizm**, he took several scholars and poets back to his capital **Ghazni** — Al-Biruni was one of them. He arrived **as a hostage**, but gradually developed a liking for the city, where he **spent the rest of his life until his death at the age of 70**. It was in Ghazni that he developed an interest in India — not unusual, because **Sanskrit works on astronomy, mathematics and medicine had been translated into Arabic from the eighth century onwards**, and when the **Punjab became part of the Ghaznavid empire**, contacts with the local population created "an environment of mutual trust and understanding." Al-Biruni **spent years in the company of Brahmana priests and scholars, learning Sanskrit** and studying religious and philosophical texts; his itinerary is not clear, but he likely **travelled widely in the Punjab and parts of northern India**. **Travel literature was already an accepted part of Arabic literature** — dealing with lands from the **Sahara desert in the west to the River Volga in the north** — so while **few people in India would have read Al-Biruni before 1500, many outside India did**.

**His translations (a favourite MCQ):** Al-Biruni's expertise in several languages let him translate both ways — he **translated several Sanskrit works, including Patanjali's work on grammar, into Arabic**, and for his Brahmana friends he **translated the works of Euclid (the Greek mathematician) into Sanskrit**.

**His stated objectives (Source box):** Al-Biruni described his work as "**a help to those who want to discuss religious questions with them (the Hindus), and as a repertory of information to those who want to associate with them.**"

**The Kitab-ul-Hind.** Written **in Arabic**, it is **simple and lucid**, a **voluminous text divided into 80 chapters** covering **religion and philosophy, festivals, astronomy, alchemy, manners and customs, social life, weights and measures, iconography, laws and metrology** (metrology = the science of measurement). Generally — though not always — each chapter followed a **distinctive structure: beginning with a question, following it with a description based on Sanskritic traditions, and concluding with a comparison with other cultures**. Scholars have argued this "**almost geometric structure, remarkable for its precision and predictability**," owed much to his **mathematical orientation**. He probably intended the work for **peoples living along the frontiers of the subcontinent**; he knew the existing Arabic translations of Sanskrit, Pali and Prakrit texts — fables to astronomy and medicine — but was **critical of how they were written and clearly wanted to improve on them**.

**Where "Hindu" comes from (glossed box):** the term "**Hindu**" derives from an **Old Persian word, used c. sixth–fifth centuries BCE**, for the region **east of the river Sindhu (Indus)**. The Arabs kept the Persian usage — the region was "**al-Hind**" and its people "**Hindi**." Later the **Turks** called the people east of the Indus "**Hindu**," their land "**Hindustan**," their language "**Hindavi**." **None of these expressions indicated religious identity** — religious connotations came much later.

## 2. Ibn Battuta's Rihla

**An early globe-trotter.** **Ibn Battuta's book of travels, the Rihla, written in Arabic, provides extremely rich details about the social and cultural life of the subcontinent in the fourteenth century.** This **Moroccan traveller was born in Tangier** into one of the most **respectable and educated families, known for expertise in Islamic religious law (shari'a)**; he received literary and scholastic education young. **Unlike most of his class, he considered experience gained through travel a more important source of knowledge than books** — he just loved travelling. Before setting off for India in **1332–33**, he had **made pilgrimage trips to Mecca** and travelled extensively in **Syria, Iraq, Persia, Yemen, Oman and trading ports on the coast of East Africa**. Travelling **overland through Central Asia**, he **reached Sind in 1333**; lured by the reputation of **Muhammad bin Tughlaq** as "a generous patron of arts and letters," he set off for Delhi **passing through Multan and Uch**. **The Sultan was impressed by his scholarship and appointed him the qazi (judge) of Delhi**, a post he held for several years until he **fell out of favour** (and was, the sources say, thrown into prison). He then travelled onward — reaching **Zaytun (now Quanzhou)** in China, journeying **as far as Beijing**, before deciding to return home in **1347**. His account is often **compared with that of Marco Polo**, who visited China (and India) from **Venice** in the late thirteenth century.

**Source box — "The bird leaves its nest":** from the Rihla — "**My departure from Tangier, my birthplace... I set out alone, having neither fellow-traveller... nor caravan whose party I might join, but swayed by an overmastering impulse within me and a desire long-cherished in my bosom to visit these illustrious sanctuaries. So I braced my resolution to quit all my dear ones, female and male, and forsook my home as birds forsake their nests... My age at that time was twenty-two years.**" He returned home in **1354, about 30 years after he had set out**.

**How hard travel was (quote these numbers):** travelling in the fourteenth century was **arduous and hazardous**: per Ibn Battuta, it took **forty days from Multan to Delhi**, **about fifty days from Sind to Delhi**, **forty days from Daulatabad to Delhi**, and **ten days from Gwalior to Delhi**. **Robbers were a constant danger**: he preferred travelling **in a caravan with companions**, but highway robbers attacked anyway — travelling from **Multan to Delhi, his caravan was attacked; many fellow travellers lost their lives; the survivors, including Ibn Battuta, were severely wounded**. Illness and loneliness too: the Rihla records that **attacked by fever, "I actually tied myself on the saddle with a turban-cloth in case I should fall off by reason of my weakness"** — and when he reached **Tunis**, the townsfolk greeted the shaikh and the qazi's son "but not a soul said a word of greeting to me... I felt so sad at heart on account of my loneliness that I could not restrain the tears... and wept bitterly," until **one pilgrim, realising the cause of his distress, came up with a greeting**.

**The "enjoyment of curiosities."** An **inveterate traveller**, Ibn Battuta spent years across **north Africa, West Asia, parts of Central Asia (he may even have visited Russia), the subcontinent and China**. When he returned, **the local ruler issued instructions that his stories be recorded**: the task fell to **Ibn Juzayy**, who says in his introduction (Source box) that the ruler directed that Ibn Battuta "should dictate an account of the cities which he had seen... of the interesting events which had clung to his memory, and of those whom he had met of the rulers of countries, of their distinguished men of learning, and their pious saints" — a narrative giving "**entertainment to the mind and delight to the ears and eyes.**" Note the difference from Al-Biruni: Ibn Battuta recorded **everything that was unfamiliar and exciting** — the "enjoyment of curiosities" — while Al-Biruni analysed.

**In the footsteps box (name all three):** between **1400 and 1800**, visitors wrote **travelogues in Persian**, while Indian visitors to Central Asia, Iran and the Ottoman empire also wrote — following Al-Biruni and Ibn Battuta. The best known: **Abdur Razzaq Samarqandi (visited south India in the 1440s), Mahmud Wali Balkhi (travelled widely in the 1620s — he even became a sort of sanyasi for a time), and Shaikh Ali Hazin (came to north India in the 1740s — disappointed and even disgusted, expecting red-carpet treatment)**. Most saw India as **a land of wonders**.

## 3. François Bernier — A Doctor with a Difference

**The earlier Europeans first.** After the **Portuguese arrived c. 1500**, several wrote detailed accounts of Indian social customs and religious practices; the **Jesuit Roberto Nobili** even **translated Indian texts into European languages**, and the best-known Portuguese writer, **Duarte Barbosa**, wrote a detailed account of **trade and society in south India**. After **1600**, growing numbers of **Dutch, English and French** travellers came. The most famous, the **French jeweller Jean-Baptiste Tavernier, visited India at least six times**, fascinated by **trading conditions**, comparing India with **Iran and the Ottoman empire**. Some, like the **Italian doctor Manucci, never returned to Europe and settled down in India**.

**Who Bernier was.** **François Bernier, a Frenchman, was a doctor, political philosopher and historian** who came to the Mughal Empire **in search of opportunities**. He was in India for **twelve years, from 1656 to 1668**, closely associated with the Mughal court — first as **physician to Prince Dara Shukoh, the eldest son of Emperor Shah Jahan**, and later as **intellectual and scientist with Danishmand Khan, an Armenian noble at the Mughal court**.

**Comparing "East" and "West."** Bernier travelled widely and **frequently compared what he saw in India with the situation in Europe**. He **dedicated his major writing to Louis XIV, king of France**, and wrote other works as **letters to influential officials and ministers**. In virtually every instance he **described India as a bleak situation in comparison to developments in Europe** — an assessment that was **not always accurate** — because his aim was to **influence policy-makers and the intelligentsia to make the "right" decisions**. His works were **published in France in 1670–71** and translated into **English, Dutch, German and Italian within the next five years**; between **1670 and 1725 his account was reprinted eight times in French, and by 1684 three times in English** — in marked contrast to **Arabic and Persian accounts, which circulated as manuscripts and were generally not published before 1800**. The printing press gave European writings the power to **create an image of India for Europeans**; later, after **1750**, Indians like **Shaikh Itisamuddin and Mirza Abu Talib** visited Europe, **confronted this image, and tried to influence it by producing their own versions** of matters.

**Source box — travelling with the Mughal army (Kashmir march):** Bernier lists his kit: **two good Turkoman horses, a powerful Persian camel and driver, a groom, a cook, and a servant to go before his horse with a flask of water** (per the custom of the country); **a tent of moderate size, a carpet, a portable bed of four strong light canes, a pillow, a mattress, round leather table-cloths, dyed napkins, three small bags of culinary utensils** in a large bag inside a **capacious double sack of leather thongs** holding provisions, linen and apparel; **excellent rice for five or six days, sweet biscuits flavoured with anise, limes and sugar** — "nor have I forgotten a **linen bag with its small iron hook for the purpose of suspending and draining dahi or curds; nothing being considered so refreshing in this country as lemonade and dahi.**"

## 4. Making Sense of an Alien World — Al-Biruni and the Sanskritic Tradition

**The three "barriers" (a guaranteed question).** Al-Biruni discussed the barriers that obstruct understanding. **First: language** — Sanskrit was so different from Arabic and Persian that "ideas and concepts could not be easily translated from one language into another." He described Sanskrit as a language "of an **enormous range, both in words and inflections**... calling one and the same thing by various names... and using one and the same word for a variety of subjects." **Second: differences in religious beliefs and practices.** **Third: the self-absorption and consequent insularity of the local population.** Yet — the irony the NCERT highlights — despite knowing these problems, **Al-Biruni depended almost exclusively on the works of Brahmanas**, citing passages from **the Vedas, the Puranas, the Bhagavad Gita, the works of Patanjali, the Manusmriti**, etc., to understand Indian society.

**Scepticism too (Source box "God knows best"):** travellers did not always believe what they were told. Faced with the story of a **wooden idol that supposedly lasted 216,432 years**, Al-Biruni asks: "How, then, could wood..." — a polite doubt, ending with "God knows best!"

**Al-Biruni on caste.** He tried to explain caste **by looking for parallels in other societies**: in **ancient Persia**, four social categories were recognised — **knights and princes; monks, fire-priests and lawyers; physicians, astronomers and other scientists; and peasants and artisans** — so social divisions were **not unique to India**. At the same time he pointed out that **within Islam all men were considered equal, differing only in observance of piety**. He followed the Brahmanical account — **Brahman from the mouth of Brahma, Kshatriya from the shoulders and hands ("their degree is not much below that of the Brahmana"), Vaishya from the thigh, Shudra from the feet** — but noted: "**Much as these classes differ from each other, they live together in the same towns and villages, mixed together in the same houses and lodgings.**" And he **disapproved of the notion of pollution**: everything which falls into impurity "strives and succeeds in regaining its original condition of purity" — "**the sun cleanses the air, and the salt in the sea prevents the water from becoming polluted.**" The NCERT's critical point: his description was **deeply influenced by normative Sanskrit texts** (rules as the Brahmanas laid them down), whereas **in real life the system was not quite so rigid** — for instance, the categories defined as **antyaja** (literally, born outside the system) **often provided inexpensive labour to both peasants and zamindars**: subjected to **social oppression** yet **included within economic networks**.

## 5. Ibn Battuta and the Excitement of the Unfamiliar

**The connected world.** By the time Ibn Battuta reached Delhi in the fourteenth century, the subcontinent was part of **a global network of communication stretching from China in the east to north-west Africa and Europe in the west** — urban centres where people speaking **Arabic, Persian, Turkish and other languages** shared ideas, information and anecdotes: stories of **pious men, kings who could be both cruel and generous, and the lives of ordinary people**. Anything unfamiliar was highlighted "to ensure that the listener or the reader was suitably impressed by accounts of distant yet accessible worlds."

**The coconut and the paan (his famous strategy).** Faced with things unknown to his audience, Ibn Battuta described them **through comparison with the familiar**. The **paan**: "The betel is a tree which is cultivated in the same manner as the **grape-vine**... The betel has no fruit and is grown only for the sake of its leaves... one takes **areca nut**; this is like a **nutmeg** but is broken up until it is reduced to small pellets... Then he takes the leaves of betel, puts a little **chalk** on them, and masticates them along with the betel." The **coconut**: "These trees... look exactly like **date-palms**, except that the one produces nuts... The nut of a coconut tree **resembles a man's head**, for in it are what look like **two eyes and a mouth**, and the inside of it when it is green looks like the **brain**, and attached to it is a fibre..." (the fibre = the hair of the head).

**Indian cities.** Ibn Battuta found cities **full of exciting opportunities for those with drive, resources and skills** — **densely populated and prosperous**, except for occasional disruptions from wars and invasions. Cities had **crowded streets and bright, colourful markets stacked with a wide variety of goods**. **Delhi** he described as "a vast city, with a great population, **the largest in India**"; **Daulatabad (in Maharashtra) was no less and easily rivalled Delhi in size**. (Source box on Dehli:) "The rampart round the city is without parallel. The **breadth of its wall is eleven cubits**; inside it are **houses for the night sentry and gate-keepers**... **store-houses for storing edibles, magazines, ammunition, ballistas and siege machines**. The grains stored... can last for a long time without rotting... In the interior of the rampart, **horsemen as well as infantrymen move from one end of the city to another**. The rampart is pierced by **windows** through which light enters. The **lower part is built of stone; the upper part of bricks**... There are **twenty-eight gates (darwaza)**, of which the **Budaun darwaza is the greatest**; inside the **Mandwi darwaza there is a grain market**; adjacent to the **Gul darwaza an orchard**... It has a fine cemetery in which graves have **domes**, and those that do not have a dome have an arch... in the cemetery they sow **flowers such as tuberose, jasmine, wild rose**... and flowers blossom there in all seasons."

**Bazaars and prosperity.** Bazaars were not only places of economic transactions but **hubs of social and cultural activities** — most had **a mosque and a temple**, and some had spaces for **public performances by dancers, musicians and singers**. While Ibn Battuta did not explain the towns' prosperity, historians have used his account to suggest that **towns derived a significant portion of their wealth through the appropriation of surplus from villages**. He found **Indian agriculture very productive because of the fertility of the soil, which allowed farmers to cultivate two crops a year**; the subcontinent was **well integrated with inter-Asian trade networks** — Indian manufactures in great demand in **West Asia and Southeast Asia**: **Indian textiles, particularly cotton cloth, fine muslins, silks, brocade and satin** — and **certain varieties of fine muslin were so expensive they could be worn only by the nobles and the very rich**.

**Source box — Music in the market (Daulatabad):** in Daulatabad there was a market for **male and female singers called Tarababad** — "one of the greatest and most beautiful bazaars," with numerous shops, each with a door into the owner's house, **decorated with carpets**, a **swing** at the centre on which sat the **female singer** "decked with all kinds of finery," her attendants swinging her; in the middle stood a **large cupola** where the **chief of the musicians took his place every Thursday after dawn prayers**, while female singers "sing before him and dance until dusk"; there were **mosques** in the bazaar, and a **Hindu ruler alighted at the cupola every time he passed — even some Muslim rulers did the same.**

**A unique system of communication.** The state **encouraged merchants**: almost all trade routes were **well supplied with inns and guest houses**. Ibn Battuta was **amazed by the efficiency of the postal system**, which let merchants **send information, remit credit across long distances, and dispatch goods required at short notice** — so efficient that while it took **fifty days to reach Delhi from Sind, the news reports of spies reached the Sultan in just five days**. (Source box, "On horse and on foot":) the postal system was of **two kinds**: the **horse-post, called uluq, run by royal horses stationed at a distance of every four miles**; the **foot-post with three stations per mile, called dawa** (one-third of a mile). At every third of a mile there was a **well-populated village, outside which were three pavilions** where sat **men with girded loins ready to start**, each carrying **a rod, two cubits in length, with copper bells at the top**. The courier ran holding the letter in one hand and the rod with bells in the other; hearing the ringing, the men of the next pavilion got ready, and one took the letter and "runs at top speed shaking the rod all the while until he reaches the next dawa." **The foot-post was quicker than the horse-post** — it was often used to transport **the fruits of Khurasan**, much desired in India.

**Source box — "A strange nation" (Abdur Razzaq, 1440s):** a mixture of emotions — he did not appreciate the **port of Calicut (Kozhikode, Kerala)**, "a people the likes of whom I had never imagined," "a strange nation"; but arriving in **Mangalore** and crossing the **Western Ghats**, he saw a temple that "filled him with admiration": "Within three leagues (about nine miles) of Mangalore, I saw an **idol-house the likes of which is not to be found in all the world**... square, approximately **ten yards a side, five yards in height, all covered with cast bronze, with four porticos**. In the entrance portico was a statue in the likeness of a human being, full stature, **made of gold. It had two red rubies for eyes, so cunningly made that you would say it could see.**"

## 6. Bernier and the "Degenerate" East

**His method — binary opposition.** If Ibn Battuta described what excited him by its novelty, **Bernier belonged to a different intellectual tradition: comparing and contrasting India with Europe, generally emphasising the superiority of the latter**, focusing on what he considered depressing. His **Travels in the Mughal Empire** is marked by **detailed observations, critical insights and reflection**, trying to place Mughal history in a **universal framework**; his representation works on the **model of binary opposition — India presented as the inverse of Europe**.

**The question of landownership (his core argument).** According to Bernier, **one fundamental difference between Mughal India and Europe was the lack of private property in land**. A firm believer in the virtues of private property, he saw **crown ownership of land as harmful for both the state and its people**: the emperor **owned all the land and distributed it among his nobles**, with disastrous consequences — **landholders could not pass their land to their children, so they were averse to any long-term investment** in the sustenance and expansion of production; the absence of private property **prevented the emergence of the class of "improving" landlords (as in Western Europe)**; it led to **the uniform ruination of agriculture, excessive oppression of the peasantry, and a continuous decline in the living standards of all sections of society except the ruling aristocracy**. (This perception was not unique to Bernier — it is found in **most travellers' accounts of the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries**.)

**Source box — the poor peasant:** "Of the vast tracts of country constituting the empire of Hindustan, many are little more than **sand, or barren mountains, badly cultivated, and thinly populated**. Even a considerable portion of the good land remains **untilled for want of labourers**; many of whom **perish in consequence of the bad treatment they experience from Governors**. The poor people, when they become incapable of discharging the demands of their **rapacious lords**, are not only deprived of the means of subsistence but also lose their children, **carried away as slaves**. Thus the peasantry, driven to despair by so excessive a tyranny, **abandon the country**." The NCERT adds: Bernier was **participating in contemporary European debates about the nature of state and society**, intending his description of Mughal India **as a warning to those who did not recognise the "merits" of private property**.

**"No middle state."** Bernier described Indian society as **undifferentiated masses of impoverished people subjugated by a small minority of a very rich and powerful ruling class** — between the poorest and the richest, **no social group or class worth the name**: "**There is no middle state in India.**" Hence his picture: the Mughal king was **the king of "beggars and barbarians"**; the cities **ruined and contaminated with "ill air"**; the fields "**overspread with bushes**" and full of "**pestilential marishes**" (marshes) — all because of **one reason: crown ownership of land**.

**Source box — Pelsaert's poverty:** the **Dutch traveller Pelsaert** (early decades of the seventeenth century) was shocked by "**poverty so great and miserable that the life of the people can be depicted or accurately described only as the home of stark want and the dwelling place of bitter woe**." Holding the state responsible: "**So much is wrung from the peasants that even dry bread is scarcely left to fill their stomachs.**"

**The historian's correction (the NCERT's critique — quote this in long answers):** **none of the Mughal official documents suggests that the state was the sole owner of land**. **Abu'l Fazl**, the sixteenth-century official chronicler of Akbar's reign, described the **land revenue as "remunerations of sovereignty" — a claim made by the ruler on his subjects for the protection he provided, rather than as rent on land he owned**. European travellers may have regarded such claims as rent **because land revenue demands were often very high** — but it was **not a rent, or even a land tax, but a tax on the crop** (see Theme 8).

**His influence — oriental despotism.** Bernier's descriptions influenced Western theorists from the **eighteenth century** onwards: the French philosopher **Montesquieu** used this account to develop the idea of **oriental despotism** — rulers in Asia (the Orient) enjoying **absolute authority over their subjects, who were kept in conditions of subjugation and poverty**, with **all land belonging to the king** and **private property non-existent**.

**Travellers on women and slaves.** Travellers were generally **men intrigued by the condition of women**; some took social inequities for granted as "natural." **Slaves were openly sold in markets, like any other commodity, and regularly exchanged as gifts**: Ibn Battuta, reaching **Sind**, purchased "**horses, camels and slaves**" as gifts for **Sultan Muhammad bin Tughlaq**; at **Multan** he presented the governor with "**a slave and horse together with raisins and almonds**." The Sultan, happy with the sermon of a preacher named **Nasiruddin**, gave him "**a hundred thousand tankas (coins) and two hundred slaves**." There was **considerable differentiation among slaves**: some **female slaves in the Sultan's service were experts in music and dance** (Ibn Battuta enjoyed their performance at the **wedding of the Sultan's sister**); female slaves were also employed **to keep a watch on his nobles**. Slaves were generally used for **domestic labour**, and their services were **indispensable for carrying women and men on palanquins or dola**. **The price of slaves, particularly female slaves for domestic labour, was very low, and most families who could afford it kept at least one or two.** European travellers highlighted the treatment of women as a **marker of difference between Western and Eastern societies** — Bernier chose the practice of **sati** for detailed description, noting that while **some women seemed to embrace death cheerfully, others were forced to die** — and his most poignant description (Source box) is of a **child sati at Lahore**.

# 🟤 THEME 8: PEASANTS, ZAMINDARS AND THE STATE — Agrarian Society and the Mughal Empire (c. 16th–17th centuries)

## 🎬 The story of the chapter
The Mughal Empire stood on the plough. This chapter follows the grain from the field to the treasury: the peasant with his pair of bullocks, the village panchayat with its common fund, the women whose labour no record names, the forest tribes with their seasonal calendars, the zamindars in their qilachas, the diwan's clerks measuring every field, and the silver of the world pouring into India to pay for its cotton. Over all of it sits the Ain-i Akbari — the empire's own account of itself, which we must learn to read against the grain.

## 1. Peasants and Agricultural Production

**The village and its people.** The **basic unit of agricultural society was the village**, inhabited by peasants who performed the **manifold seasonal tasks** of production — **tilling the soil, sowing seeds, harvesting the crop** — and contributed labour to **agro-based goods such as sugar and oil**. But rural India was **not settled peasant production alone**: large tracts of **dry land or hilly regions** were not cultivable in the same way, and **forest areas made up a substantial proportion of the territory** — keep this varied topography in mind.

**Looking for sources (a favourite question).** Peasants **did not write about themselves**, so our major source for the agrarian history of the sixteenth and early seventeenth centuries is **chronicles and documents from the Mughal court** — above all the **Ain-i Akbari** (the Ain), authored by **Akbar's court historian Abu'l Fazl**, which meticulously recorded **the arrangements made by the state to ensure cultivation, enable revenue collection, and regulate relations between the state and rural magnates, the zamindars**. Its **central purpose** was to present **a vision of Akbar's empire where social harmony was provided by a strong ruling class**; any revolt or assertion of autonomous power was, in its eyes, **predestined to fail** — so **whatever we learn from the Ain about peasants remains a view from the top**. Fortunately it can be supplemented: **detailed revenue records from Gujarat, Maharashtra and Rajasthan (17th–18th centuries)**, and the **extensive records of the East India Company** for eastern India. These record **instances of conflicts between peasants, zamindars and the state**, giving insight into **peasants' perception of, and their expectations of fairness from, the state**.

**Peasants and their lands.** The term Indo-Persian sources most frequently used for a peasant was **raiyat (plural, riaya) or muzarian**; we also meet **kisan or asami**. Seventeenth-century sources distinguish **khud-kashta** — **residents of the village in which they held their lands** — from **pahi-kashta** — **non-resident cultivators who belonged to some other village but cultivated lands elsewhere on a contractual basis**. People became pahi-kashta **either out of choice** (for example, when terms of revenue in a distant village were more favourable) **or out of compulsion** (for example, **economic distress after a famine**). The **average peasant of north India seldom possessed more than a pair of bullocks and two ploughs; most possessed even less**. In **Gujarat**, peasants with about **six acres** were considered affluent; in **Bengal**, **five acres was the upper limit of an average peasant farm — ten acres made one a rich asami**. Cultivation was based on the **principle of individual ownership** — peasant lands were **bought and sold like other property**.

**Source box — Babur on the quick village:** from the Babur Nama — if peasants **flee a place, "they do it in such a way that not a sign or trace of them remains in a day and a half"**; but if they fix on a place to settle, "they need not dig water courses because their **crops are all rain-grown**, and as the population of Hindustan is unlimited it swarms in. They make a tank or a well; they need not build houses or set up walls... **khas-grass abounds, wood is unlimited, huts are made, and straightaway there is a village or a town!**"

**Irrigation and technology.** (Source box, Babur Nama:) "The greater part of Hindustan country is situated on **level land**... it nowhere has **running waters**... **Autumn crops grow by the downpour of the rains themselves; and strange it is that spring crops grow even when no rains fall.**" **Young trees** were watered "**by means of buckets or wheels**": in **Lahore, Dipalpur** and those parts, people watered **by a wheel** — two circles of rope long enough to suit the well's depth, strips of wood fixed between them, **pitchers fastened on**, the rope-and-pitcher wheel set over the wheel-well; at one end of the wheel-axle a second wheel, and close to it another on an upright axle; **the bullock turns the last wheel, its teeth catch in the teeth of the second**, turning the wheel with pitchers; a trough collects the water "and from this the water is conveyed everywhere." In **Agra, Chandwar, Bayana** and those parts, people watered **with a bucket**: a fork of wood at the well-edge with a roller between the forks, a rope tied to a large bucket over the roller and to a bullock — "**one person must drive the bullock, another empty the bucket.**" **Irrigation projects received state support**: the state dug new canals (**nahr, nala**) and repaired old ones — like the **shahnahr in the Punjab during Shah Jahan's reign**. Agricultural technology harnessed **cattle energy**: the **wooden plough, light and easily assembled with an iron tip or coulter**, did **not make deep furrows, which preserved moisture** during the intensely hot months; a **drill pulled by a pair of giant oxen planted seeds**, but **broadcasting of seed was the most prevalent method**; **hoeing and weeding were done simultaneously with a narrow iron blade with a small wooden handle**.

**An abundance of crops.** Agriculture was organised around **two major seasonal cycles — kharif (autumn) and rabi (spring)** — so most regions produced **a minimum of two crops a year (do-fasla)**, and some, with assured water, even **three**. The Ain records: **Agra produced 39 varieties of crops, Delhi 43, over the two seasons; Bengal produced 50 varieties of rice alone.** Staples did not mean subsistence alone: sources speak of **jins-i kamil (literally, "perfect crops")** — crops the **Mughal state encouraged because they brought in more revenue**: **cotton and sugarcane were jins-i kamil par excellence** — cotton across **central India and the Deccan plateau**, **Bengal famous for its sugar** — plus **oilseeds (mustard) and lentils**. Subsistence and commercial production were **closely intertwined in an average peasant's holding**. **New crops** arrived in the seventeenth century: **maize (makka), introduced via Africa and Spain**, listed as a major crop of **western India**; and from the **New World** came **tomatoes, potatoes and chillies**, and fruits like the **pineapple and the papaya**. (Source box — the spread of tobacco:) tobacco **arrived first in the Deccan**, spreading to northern India in the early seventeenth century; **the Ain does not mention tobacco** in its northern lists; **Akbar and his nobles came across tobacco for the first time in 1604**; smoking in **hookahs or chillums** "caught on in a big way"; **Jahangir was so concerned about its addiction that he banned it — totally ineffectively**: by the end of the seventeenth century tobacco was a **major article of consumption, cultivation and trade all over India**. (Box — agricultural prosperity and population growth:) despite famines and epidemics, India's population increased, by economic historians' calculations, **by about 50 million people between 1600 and 1800 — an increase of about 33 per cent over 200 years**.

## 2. The Village Community

**Three constituents.** Peasants held lands in **individual ownership**, yet belonged to a **collective village community** with **three constituents — the cultivators, the panchayat, and the village headman (muqaddam or mandal)**.

**Caste and the rural milieu.** Caste inequities meant the **cultivators were a highly heterogeneous group**; among those who tilled the land, a **sizeable number worked as menials or agricultural labourers (majur)**. Despite the abundance of cultivable land, **certain caste groups were assigned menial tasks and thus relegated to poverty** — a **large section of the village population** with the **least resources**, constrained by their position in the caste hierarchy, "**much like the Dalits of modern...**"

**Panchayats and headmen.** The **village panchayat was an assembly of elders, usually important people with hereditary rights over their property**; in **mixed-caste villages** it was usually a **heterogeneous body**. It was an **oligarchy**, representing various castes and communities — though **the village menial-cum-agricultural worker was unlikely to be represented** — and its **decisions were binding** on members. It was headed by the **headman, the muqaddam or mandal**: chosen through the **consensus of the village elders**, with the choice **ratified by the zamindar**; headmen held office **as long as they enjoyed the confidence of the village elders, failing which they could be dismissed by them**. The headman's **chief function** was to **supervise the preparation of village accounts, assisted by the accountant or patwari**. The panchayat **derived its funds from contributions by individuals to a common financial pool**, used for **defraying the costs of entertaining revenue officials**, for **community welfare activities such as tiding over natural calamities (like floods)**, and often for **constructing a bund or digging a canal** which peasants could not afford individually. (Box — corrupt mandals:) mandals often misused their positions — principally accused of **defrauding village accounts in connivance with the patwari, and underassessing the revenue owed from their own lands, passing the additional burden to the smaller cultivator**.

**The panchayat's powers.** One important function was to **ensure that caste boundaries among the village's communities were upheld**: in **eastern India all marriages were held in the presence of the mandal** — the headman oversaw conduct "**chiefly to prevent any offence against their caste**." Panchayats could **levy fines and inflict more serious punishments like expulsion from the community** — a **drastic step**, usually **for a limited period**: the expelled person **became an outcaste and lost the right to practise his profession**; it was intended as a **deterrent to violation of caste norms**.

**Jati panchayats.** In addition, **each caste or jati had its own jati panchayat**, and these wielded **considerable power**: in **Rajasthan** they **arbitrated civil disputes between members of different castes, mediated contested claims on land, decided whether marriages were performed according to the norms of the caste group, determined who had ritual precedence in village functions** — and in most cases, **except in matters of criminal justice, the state respected the decisions of jati panchayats**.

**Petitions against the powerful.** Archival records from **western India (Rajasthan, Maharashtra)** contain **petitions to the panchayat complaining about extortionate taxation or the demand for unpaid labour (begar) imposed by the "superior" castes or officials of the state**, usually by **villagers from the lowest rungs**, often **collectively** — protesting "morally illegitimate demands." Especially in **times of drought**, excessive tax demands **endangered subsistence**; petitioners held that **the right to the basic minimum for survival was sanctioned by custom**, and regarded the **village panchayat as the court of appeal**.

**Village artisans.** **Marathi documents and early British village surveys** reveal **substantial numbers of artisans — sometimes as high as 25 per cent of total village households**; but the artisan/peasant distinction was **fluid** — many groups did both. **Cultivators and their families** participated in craft production — **dyeing, textile printing, baking and firing pottery, making and repairing agricultural implements** — especially in the **lulls between sowing and weeding or weeding and harvesting**. **Village artisans — potters, blacksmiths, carpenters, barbers, even goldsmiths — provided specialised services**, compensated in a **variety of ways**: most commonly **a share of the harvest, or an allotment of land** (perhaps cultivable wastes, decided by the panchayat); in **Maharashtra** such lands became the artisans' **miras or watan — their hereditary holding**. Another variant: **mutually negotiated remuneration, mostly goods for services** — **eighteenth-century Bengal zamindars paid blacksmiths, carpenters, even goldsmiths "a small daily allowance and diet money"** — a system **later described as the jajmani system, though the term was not in vogue in the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries**. **Cash remuneration was not entirely unknown either.**

**A "little republic"?** Some **British officials in the nineteenth century** saw the village as a **"little republic"** of fraternal partners sharing resources and labour — but the NCERT insists this was **not rural egalitarianism**: there was **individual ownership of assets and deep inequities based on caste and gender**; **a group of powerful individuals decided village affairs, exploited weaker sections, and had the authority to dispense justice**. More importantly, **a cash nexus had already developed through trade between villages and towns**: in the Mughal heartland **revenue was assessed and collected in cash**; **artisans producing for the export market (e.g. weavers) received advances or wages in cash**, as did producers of **cotton, silk or indigo**. (Box:) the traveller **Jean-Baptiste Tavernier** found it remarkable that in India "a village must be very small indeed if it has not a **money-changer called a shroff**" — bankers making remittances who "**enhance the rupee as they please for paisa and the paisa for these (cowrie) shells**."

## 3. Women in Agrarian Society

**Fields and biases.** Men **tilled and ploughed**; women **sowed, weeded, threshed and winnowed the harvest**. With **nucleated villages and individuated peasant farming**, the basis of production was the **labour and resources of the entire household** — so a **gendered segregation between home (women) and world (men) was not possible**. Yet **biases related to women's biological functions continued**: **menstruating women were not allowed to touch the plough or the potter's wheel in western India, or enter the groves where betel-leaves (paan) were grown in Bengal**. **Artisanal tasks — spinning yarn, sifting and kneading clay for pottery, embroidery — depended on female labour**; and **the more commercialised the product, the greater the demand on women's labour**. Peasant and artisan women worked **not only in the fields but went to the houses of their employers or to the markets if necessary** (migrant women from neighbouring villages worked at construction sites — the painting of **Fatehpur Sikri's construction shows women crushing stones**).

**Different norms among the lower strata.** Among the lower strata, marriage norms differed from elite ones: many rural communities required **bride-price rather than dowry**, and **remarriage was considered legitimate both among divorced and widowed women**. But the importance of women as a **reproductive force** meant a great **fear of losing control over them**: the **household was headed by a male**, women were **kept under strict control by male family and community members**, who could inflict **draconian punishments if they suspected infidelity**.

**Women's petitions and property.** Documents from **western India — Rajasthan, Gujarat and Maharashtra — record petitions sent by women to the village panchayat, seeking redress and justice**: wives protested against **the infidelity of their husbands or the neglect of the wife and children by the male head of the household, the grihasthi**. While **male infidelity was not always punished, the state and "superior" caste groups did intervene to ensure the family was adequately provided for**. Note this telling detail: **in most cases when women petitioned, their names were excluded from the record — the petitioner was referred to as the mother, sister or wife of the male head of the household.** Among the **landed gentry, women had the right to inherit property**: instances from the **Punjab** show **women, including widows, actively participating in the rural land market as sellers of property inherited by them**; **Hindu and Muslim women inherited zamindaris which they were free to sell or mortgage**. **Women zamindars were known in eighteenth-century Bengal** — and one of the **biggest and most famous zamindaris, Rajshahi, had a woman at the helm**.

## 4. Forests and Tribes

**Beyond settled villages.** Apart from the intensively cultivated provinces of northern and north-western India, **huge swathes of forests — dense forest (jangal) or scrubland (kharbandi) — existed all over eastern India, central India, northern India (including the Terai on the Indo-Nepal border), Jharkhand, and in peninsular India down the Western Ghats and the Deccan plateau**. Informed conjectures suggest an **average forest cover of about 40 per cent**. Forest dwellers were termed **jangli** — but **being jangli did not mean an absence of "civilisation"**: it described those whose **livelihood came from the gathering of forest produce, hunting and shifting agriculture**, activities that were **largely season-specific**. Among the **Bhils**, for example: **spring reserved for collecting forest produce, summer for fishing, monsoon months for cultivation, autumn and winter for hunting**.

**Inroads into forests.** External forces entered the forest in different ways. **The state required elephants for the army**, so the **peshkash (tribute) levied from forest people often included a supply of elephants**. In **Mughal political ideology, the hunt symbolised the overwhelming concern of the state to relate to all its subjects, rich and poor** — court historians say regular hunting expeditions enabled the emperor to **travel across his extensive territories and personally attend to the grievances of its inhabitants**; court painters frequently painted the hunt, resorting to the device of **inserting a small scene somewhere in the picture that functioned as a symbol of a harmonious reign**.

**Source box — clearance of forests (Chandimangala):** from a **sixteenth-century Bengali poem, Chandimangala, composed by Mukundaram Chakrabarti**; the hero **Kalaketu set up a kingdom by clearing forests**: "Hearing the news, outsiders came from various lands. Kalaketu then bought and distributed among them **heavy knives, axes, battle-axes and pikes**. From the north came the **Das (people), one hundred** of them... From the south came the **harvesters, five hundred of them under one organiser**. From the west came **Zafar Mian, together with twenty-two thousand men**. **Sulaimani beads in their hands, they chanted the names of their pir and paighambar (Prophet).** Having cleared the forest they **established markets**. Hundreds and hundreds of foreigners ate and entered the forest. **Hearing the sound of the axe, tigers became apprehensive and ran away, roaring.**" (Pargana = an administrative subdivision of a Mughal province.)

**Source box — trade between hill tribes and plains, c. 1595:** Abu'l Fazl on the **suba of Awadh**: "From the northern mountains quantities of goods are carried **on the backs of men, of stout ponies and of goats**, such as **gold, copper, lead, musk, tails of the kutas cow (the yak), honey, chuk (an acid composed of orange juice and lemon boiled together), pomegranate seed, ginger, long pepper, majith (a plant producing a red dye) root, borax, zedoary (a root resembling turmeric), wax, woollen stuffs, wooden ware, hawks, falcons, black falcons, merlins**... In exchange they carry back **white and coloured cloths, amber, salt, asafoetida, ornaments, glass and earthenware.**"

**Commercial and social change.** The **spread of commercial agriculture** impinged on forest life: **forest products — honey, beeswax and gum lac — were in great demand; gum lac became a major item of overseas export from India in the seventeenth century**; **elephants were captured and sold**. Trade involved **barter** as well; some tribes, like the **Lohanis in the Punjab, were engaged in overland trade between India and Afghanistan, and in the town-country trade in the Punjab itself**. Socially, **tribes had their chieftains** — like the "big men" of the village; **many tribal chiefs became zamindars, some even kings**, requiring armies recruited from **lineage groups** or through **fraternity military service**. **Tribes in the Sind region had armies comprising 6,000 cavalry and 7,000 infantry**; in **Assam, the Ahom kings had their paiks — people obliged to render military service in exchange for land** — and the **capture of wild elephants was declared a royal monopoly by the Ahom kings**.

## 5. The Zamindars

**Who they were.** Zamindars were a class that **lived off agriculture but did not participate directly in the processes of agricultural production** — **landed proprietors who enjoyed certain social and economic privileges by virtue of their superior status in rural society**. **Caste was one factor** accounting for their elevated status; another was that they **performed certain services (khidmat) for the state**. They held **extensive personal lands termed milkiyat (property)**, cultivated for their **private use, often with the help of hired or servile labour**, and which they **could sell, bequeath or mortgage at will**. They also **often collected revenue on behalf of the state, a service for which they were compensated financially**. And **control over military resources was another source of power**: most zamindars had **fortresses (qilachas)** and an **armed contingent comprising units of cavalry, artillery and infantry**. Visualise rural social relations as a pyramid: **zamindars constituted its very narrow apex**. Abu'l Fazl's account shows an **"upper-caste" Brahmana-Rajput combine** with firm control over rural society, a **fairly large representation from intermediate castes**, and a **liberal sprinkling of Muslim zamindaris**.

**How zamindaris grew.** Documents suggest **conquest** could be the source of some zamindaris — **the dispossession of weaker people by a powerful military chieftain** — though the state would unlikely allow such aggression **unless the zamindar had been confirmed by an imperial order (sanad)**. More important were the **slow processes of consolidation**: **colonisation of new lands, transfer of rights, orders of the state, and purchase** — processes which **permitted people of relatively "lower" castes to enter zamindar ranks**, as **zamindaris were bought and sold quite briskly**. A combination of factors allowed **clan- or lineage-based consolidation**: the **Rajputs and Jats** consolidated control over **vast swathes of northern India**, while **peasant-pastoralists like the Sadgops carved out powerful zamindaris in central and south-western Bengal**.

**Their role — and the two-sided relationship.** Zamindars **spearheaded the colonisation of agricultural land** and **helped settle cultivators by providing the means of cultivation, including cash loans**; the **buying and selling of zamindaris accelerated monetisation** in the countryside; they **sold produce from their milkiyat lands** and often **established markets (haats)** to which peasants came to sell their produce. Though **there can be little doubt that zamindars were an exploitative class**, their relationship with the peasantry **had an element of reciprocity, paternalism and patronage** — two aspects reinforce this: **first, the bhakti saints, who eloquently condemned caste-based and other oppression, did not portray the zamindars (or, interestingly, the moneylender) as exploiters — usually it was the revenue official of the state who was the object of their ire; second, in a large number of agrarian uprisings which erupted in north India in the seventeenth century, zamindars often received the support of the peasantry in their struggle against the state.** (Box — a parallel army:) per the Ain, the **combined military strength of the zamindars of Mughal India was 384,558 cavalry, 4,277,057 infantry, 1,863 elephants, 4,260 cannons, and 4,500 boats.**

## 6. Land Revenue System

**The apparatus.** **Revenue from the land was the economic mainstay of the Mughal Empire.** The state therefore built an administrative apparatus to ensure **control over agricultural production** and to **fix and collect revenue** across the empire — including the **office (daftar) of the diwan**, responsible for **supervising the fiscal system of the empire**. **Revenue officials and record keepers penetrated the agricultural domain and became a decisive agent in shaping agrarian relations.**

**Assessment and collection.** The state first sought **specific information about the extent of agricultural lands and their produce** before fixing taxes. The **land revenue arrangements consisted of two stages — first assessment, then actual collection**: the **jama** was the **amount assessed**, as opposed to **hasil**, the **amount collected**. In his list of duties of the **amil-guzar (revenue collector)**, **Akbar decreed that while he should strive to make cultivators pay in cash, the option of payment in kind was also to be kept open**. The state tried to **maximise its claims**, but actual realisation was sometimes **thwarted by local conditions**. Both **cultivated and cultivable lands were measured** in each province; the **Ain compiled the aggregates under Akbar**, and efforts continued later — in **1665 Aurangzeb expressly instructed revenue officials to prepare annual records of the number of cultivators in each village**. Yet **not all areas were measured successfully — forests covered huge areas and remained unmeasured**.

**Source box — classification of lands under Akbar (the Ain):** "**Polaj** is land which is **annually cultivated for each crop in succession and is never allowed to lie fallow**. **Parauti** is land **left out of cultivation for a time that it may recover its strength**. **Chachar** is land that has **lain fallow for three or four years**. **Banjar** is land **uncultivated for five years and more**. Of the first two kinds there are **three classes — good, middling, and bad**. They **add together the produce of each sort, and the third of this represents the medium produce, one-third part of which is exacted as the Royal dues.**"

**Two linked boxes:** (**The mansabdari system**) the Mughal administrative system had at its apex a **military-cum-bureaucratic apparatus (mansabdari)** responsible for civil and military affairs; **some mansabdars were paid in cash (naqdi), while the majority were paid through assignments of revenue (jagirs)** in different regions, and **were transferred periodically**. (**Amin**) an official **responsible for ensuring that imperial regulations were carried out in the provinces**.

## 7. The Flow of Silver

**Why silver came.** The Mughal Empire was among the **large territorial empires of Asia** — with the **Ming (China), Safavid (Iran) and Ottoman (Turkey)** — that consolidated power and resources in the sixteenth–seventeenth centuries; their **political stability created vibrant networks of overland trade from China to the Mediterranean Sea**. The **voyages of discovery and the opening of the New World** massively expanded **Asia's (particularly India's) trade with Europe**, diversifying India's overseas trade and its commodity composition. **Expanding trade brought huge amounts of silver bullion into Asia to pay for goods procured from India, and a large part of that bullion gravitated towards India** — which was good, **as India did not have natural resources of silver**. Result: the **sixteenth–eighteenth centuries saw remarkable stability in the availability of metal currency, particularly the silver rupya**, facilitating **unprecedented expansion of minting and the circulation of money**, and **the Mughal state's ability to extract taxes and revenue in cash**.

**Source box — Giovanni Careri (c. 1690), how silver came to India (based on Bernier):** "All the **gold and silver which circulates throughout the world at last centres here** [in the Mughal Empire]... as much of it comes out of **America**, after running through several Kingdoms of **Europe**, goes partly into **Turkey** for several sorts of commodities, and part into **Persia by the way of Smirna for silk**. Now the Turks not being able to abstain from **coffee, which comes from Hyeman (Oman) and Arabia**... nor Persia, Arabia and the Turks themselves able to go without the **commodities of India**, send vast quantities of money to **Moka (Mocha) on the Red Sea... to Bassora (Basra) at the bottom of the Persian Gulf**... which is afterwards sent over in ships to **Indostan (Hindustan)**. Besides the Indian, Dutch, English and Portuguese ships that every year carry the commodities of Indostan to **Pegu, Tanasserri (parts of Myanmar), Siam (Thailand), Ceylon (Sri Lanka)**... the Maldive Islands, Mozambique and other places... **All that the Dutch fetch from the mines in Japan, sooner or later, goes to Indostan**; and the goods carried hence into Europe, whether to **France, England or Portugal**, are all purchased for **ready money, which remains there.**"

## 8. The Ain-i Akbari of Abu'l Fazl Allami

**What it is.** The **Ain-i Akbari** was the **culmination of a large historical, administrative project of classification** undertaken by **Abu'l Fazl at the order of Emperor Akbar**; it was **completed in 1598 — the forty-second regnal year — after five revisions**. It was part of the larger **Akbar Nama** project of history writing, which **comprised three books**: the **first two provided the historical narrative** (Theme 9); the **Ain-i Akbari, the third book, was organised as a compendium of imperial regulations and a gazetteer of the empire**. It gives detailed accounts of the **organisation of the court, administration and army, the sources of revenue, the physical layout of the provinces (subas)**, and the **literary, cultural and religious traditions of the people**, with **intricate quantitative information**. Its purpose was partly practical — it **informed the emperor about the varied customs and practices across his territories** — but remember: **this is a view of the regions from the centre, a view of society from its apex.**

**The five books (daftars).** The Ain is made up of **five books, of which the first three describe the administration**. **Book one, manzil-abadi, concerns the imperial household and its maintenance.** **Book two, sipah-abadi, covers the military and civil administration and the establishment of servants** — including **notices and short biographical sketches of imperial officials (mansabdars), learned men, poets and artists**. **Book three, mulk-abadi, deals with the fiscal side of the empire** — rich quantitative information on revenue rates, followed by the "**Account of the Twelve Provinces**": the **geographic, topographic and economic profile of all subas**, their administrative and fiscal divisions (**sarkars, parganas and mahals**), **total measured area, and assessed revenue (jama)**. For each **sarkar** the Ain gives **tables with eight columns**: (1) **pargana/mahal**; (2) **qila (forts)**; (3) **arazi and zamin-i paimuda (measured area)**; (4) **naqdi, revenue assessed in cash**; (5) **suyurghal, grants of revenue in charity**; (6) **zamindars**; (7) and (8) **details of the castes of these zamindars and their troops — horsemen (sawar), foot-soldiers (piyada) and elephants (fil)**. The **fourth and fifth books** deal with the **religious, literary and cultural traditions** of the people and contain **a collection of Akbar's "auspicious sayings."**

**Source box — "Moistening the rose garden of fortune" (Abu'l Fazl's method):** Akbar's mandate: "Write with the pen of sincerity the account of the glorious events..." Abu'l Fazl explains his research: "**I spent much labour and research in collecting the records and narratives of His Majesty's actions and I was a long time interrogating the servants of the State and the old members of the illustrious family. I examined both prudent, truth-speaking old men and active-minded, right-actioned young ones and reduced their statements to writing.** Royal commands were issued to the provinces, that those who from old service remembered the events of the past should copy out the notes and memoranda and transmit them to the court... materials... recited in the royal hearing... I obtained the chronicle of events beginning at the **Nineteenth Year of the Divine Era, when the Record Office was established**... I also took much trouble to incorporate many of the reports which ministers and high officials had submitted... I constructed **a reservoir for irrigating and moistening the rose garden of fortune (the Akbar Nama)**."

**Problems with the Ain as a source (a certain exam question).** The manuscript was **revised five times**, suggesting caution and a search for authenticity: **oral testimonies were cross-checked and verified** before being incorporated as "facts," and in the quantitative sections **all numeric data were reproduced in words to minimise transcriptional errors**. Yet historians point out its problems: **numerous errors in totalling have been detected**, ascribed to **simple slips of arithmetic or transcription by Abu'l Fazl's assistants** — generally minor; and the **quantitative data are somewhat skewed**: data were **not collected uniformly from all provinces** — **for many subas the caste composition of zamindars was compiled, but such information is not available for Bengal and Orissa**; and the **detailed list of prices and wages is mainly derived from data in or around the imperial capital of Agra, of limited relevance for the rest of the country**. These limitations notwithstanding, the Ain remains **an extraordinary document** — a **major breakthrough in the tradition of medieval chroniclers, who wrote mostly about wars, conquests, political machinations and dynastic turmoil** — by giving quantitative information about the empire's products and people.
